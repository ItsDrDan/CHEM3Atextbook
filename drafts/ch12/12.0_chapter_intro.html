Patient Case: Mateo's Blood Gas

Months after his overnight stay (Chapters 10 and 11), Mateo Herrera is back in the emergency department. He was checking drip lines in the almond orchard on a dusty afternoon when an asthma attack started, and this one is severe: his airways are so narrow that he cannot breathe out fully. Carbon dioxide, which his lungs normally exhale, is building up in his blood.

A respiratory therapist draws an arterial blood gas (ABG), a small sample of arterial blood tested for oxygen, carbon dioxide, and acidity. The result: pH 7.30. The normal range for arterial blood is 7.35 to 7.45. A difference of 0.10 sounds tiny, but pH is a logarithmic scale: at pH 7.30, Mateo's blood holds roughly one-quarter more hydronium ions than it does at 7.40. His blood is too acidic, a condition called acidosis.

In this chapter you will learn what makes a substance an acid or a base, how to tell strong acids from weak ones, how water itself takes part, how to calculate pH, and how buffers in the blood work to keep pH in its narrow range. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Describe the properties of acids and bases, use indicators, and write neutralization reactions (Section 12.1).
  • Use the Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry definitions and identify conjugate acid–base pairs (Section 12.2).
  • Identify acids, bases, and salts from their formulas and classify acids and bases as strong or weak (Section 12.3).
  • Use Kw to relate [H3O+] and [OH−] and classify solutions as acidic, basic, or neutral (Section 12.4).
  • Calculate pH, pOH, [H3O+], and [OH−] for strong acid and strong base solutions (Section 12.5).
  • Explain what a buffer is and how the blood's bicarbonate buffer resists changes in pH (Section 12.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Acids and bases are everywhere in health care and in the Valley. Blood pH is one of the most closely watched numbers in an intensive care unit; stomach acid, antacids, and acid reflux are daily nursing topics; acidic drinks and bacterial acids decide whether tooth enamel dissolves; and farmers test and adjust soil and irrigation water pH. This chapter builds on neutralization and electrolytes (Chapter 7), molarity (Chapter 11), and scientific notation and significant figures (Chapter 1), and it leads into equilibrium (Chapter 13), where the double arrows you meet here are explained.
