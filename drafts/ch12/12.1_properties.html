Learning Objectives

  • List the general properties of acids and of bases.
  • Use litmus, phenolphthalein, and red cabbage indicator to tell whether a solution is acidic or basic.
  • Write equations for an acid reacting with a metal, with a carbonate, and with a base (neutralization).
  • Explain why solutions of acids and bases conduct electricity.

Lemon juice, vinegar, and stomach acid taste or feel nothing like soap, baking soda, or drain cleaner, yet chemists sorted all of them into two families, acids and bases, long before anyone knew about ions. They did it by observation: how each tastes and feels, what it does to certain plant dyes, and how it reacts with metals, carbonates, and each other. Those observations are still how you will spot acids and bases in the lab.

Properties of Acids

An acid is a substance that, in water, makes the solution sour and reactive in a recognizable set of ways:

  • Sour taste. Citric acid in lemons and limes, acetic acid in vinegar, and phosphoric and citric acids in sodas give them their tang. (Taste is how acids were first recognized; never taste anything in a lab.)
  • Turn blue litmus paper red and keep phenolphthalein colorless.
  • React with active metals such as magnesium, zinc, and iron to make hydrogen gas. This is why acids corrode metal, and why a car battery (which holds sulfuric acid) carries a "corrosive" warning.
  • React with carbonates and bicarbonates to make carbon dioxide gas, which fizzes out of the solution.
  • Neutralize bases.
  • Conduct electricity in water, because they form ions (they are electrolytes, Section 7.4).

Properties of Bases

A base (an alkaline substance) has its own set of properties:

  • Bitter taste and a slippery, soapy feel. Soap, milk of magnesia, and many antacids are basic, and antacids often taste chalky.
  • Turn red litmus paper blue and turn phenolphthalein pink.
  • Neutralize acids.
  • Conduct electricity in water.
  • Strong bases such as sodium hydroxide (lye, in drain cleaner) are caustic: they damage skin and eyes, and the slippery feel on your fingers is the base reacting with the oils and proteins of your skin.

Professor Tip

A quick memory aid for litmus: "BRA", Blue turns Red in Acid. For phenolphthalein, think "pink means base." And never use taste or touch to identify a chemical. Those properties are how acids and bases were first described, not lab tests; you have indicators for that.

Indicators

An indicator is a dye whose color depends on how acidic or basic a solution is, that is, on the balance between H+ and OH− ions. Three indicators come up in CHEM 3A:

  • Litmus is a dye from lichens, sold soaked into paper strips. Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue. Litmus tells you only "acid or base," not how strong.
  • Phenolphthalein (fee-nol-THAY-leen) is colorless in acidic and neutral solutions and turns pink in basic solutions (above about pH 8). It is the indicator in most acid–base titrations (Section 11.5): the first lasting pink tells you that all the acid has been used up.
  • Red cabbage indicator is a purple extract made by soaking chopped red cabbage in hot water. Its pigments (anthocyanins, the same family that colors blueberries and red onions) change through a whole rainbow, so one indicator can sort solutions across the pH scale, which you will meet in Section 12.5.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Reading indicators

Problem. Three unknown solutions are tested. (a) Solution A turns red litmus blue. (b) Solution B stays colorless with phenolphthalein and turns red cabbage indicator pink. (c) A few drops of phenolphthalein turn solution C pink. One is KOH(aq), one is Ca(OH)2(aq), and one is HNO3(aq). Which solution must be the nitric acid?

Solution. A is basic (red litmus to blue). C is basic (pink phenolphthalein). B is acidic: phenolphthalein stays colorless and cabbage turns pink, an acid color. So B is HNO3; A and C are the two hydroxides.

Check. Formulas agree: HNO3 starts with H (an acid), and KOH and Ca(OH)2 contain hydroxide (bases).

Reactions of Acids

Three reaction types from Chapter 7 are really acid reactions. Each equation below is balanced; check the atoms yourself.

Acid + active metal → salt + hydrogen gas (a single-displacement reaction, Section 7.8):

Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)

You can see both halves of this in the lab. Drop a strip of magnesium into hydrochloric acid under an upside-down tube filled with water, and the bubbles of hydrogen collect at the top, pushing the water out. Zinc in hydrochloric acid makes hydrogen too: hold a burning splint at the mouth of a test tube of the collected gas, and it goes off with a small "pop" as the hydrogen burns. That pop is the standard test for H2.

Acid + carbonate (or bicarbonate) → salt + water + carbon dioxide (a gas-evolution reaction, Section 7.6). The H2CO3 that forms first breaks down into H2O and CO2:

CaCO3(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

NaHCO3(s) + HC2H3O2(aq) → NaC2H3O2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

The second equation is the baking soda and vinegar "volcano." The first is why limestone and marble statues slowly dissolve in acid rain, and why a calcium carbonate antacid fizzes in stomach acid.

Acid + base → salt + water (neutralization, Section 7.6):

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)

Neutralization is a double-replacement reaction, so the "inside–outside" pattern from Section 7.3 predicts the products when the acid is written first: in HCl + NaOH, the two inside ions (Cl and Na) pair up as the salt, and the two outside ions (H and OH) pair up as water, HOH, which is H2O.

A salt is the ionic compound made from the base's cation and the acid's anion. When a strong acid neutralizes a strong base, the net ionic equation is always the same:

H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Writing acid reactions

Problem. Write a balanced equation for each reaction: (a) zinc metal dropped into hydrochloric acid; (b) sodium carbonate added to nitric acid; (c) sulfuric acid neutralized by potassium hydroxide.

Solution.

(a) Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)

(b) Na2CO3(s) + 2 HNO3(aq) → 2 NaNO3(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

(c) H2SO4(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → K2SO4(aq) + 2 H2O(l)

Check. Count atoms on each side. In (b): 2 Na, 2 N, 1 C, 2 H, and 9 O on both sides (3 + 6 on the left; 6 + 1 + 2 on the right). In (c), two H+ from H2SO4 need two OH−, so two KOH and two waters.

Acids and Bases Are Electrolytes

Pure water barely conducts electricity. Dissolve an acid or a base in it and the solution conducts, because the acid or base forms ions that carry charge through the solution (Section 7.4). Hydrochloric acid gives H+ and Cl−; sodium hydroxide gives Na+ and OH−. Some acids and bases form many ions and conduct strongly; others form only a few and conduct weakly. That difference, strong versus weak, is the subject of Section 12.3.

Biology Connection: The Skin's Acid Mantle

The surface of healthy skin is mildly acidic, usually around pH 4 to 6 and often a little below 5. Lactic acid and other acids in sweat, and fatty acids from skin oils, keep it that way. This thin acidic film, sometimes called the acid mantle, helps the outer skin layer hold together as a barrier and favors the harmless bacteria that normally live on skin over some that cause infections. Ordinary bar soap is basic (often around pH 9 to 10), which is one reason frequent hand washing can leave skin dry and irritated, and why many cleansers for sensitive skin and for patients' skin care are formulated closer to skin's own pH.

Valley Connection: Acid for Alkaline Soils

Many San Joaquin Valley soils are not acidic at all; they are alkaline, and some contain natural lime (calcium carbonate, CaCO3). Trouble comes when a soil holds too much sodium. Sodium makes the tiny clay particles disperse, they clog the pores in the soil, and irrigation water ponds on the surface instead of soaking in. University of California soil scientists describe these sodic soils as having a high exchangeable-sodium content and often a very high pH, above 8.5, and they note that many soils on the Valley's west side are both sodic and salty.

The fix is calcium, which pushes sodium off the clay so it can be washed below the roots. Growers can add gypsum (calcium sulfate) directly. But if the soil already contains lime, they can add an acid instead and let chemistry free the calcium that is already there. Sulfuric acid, mixed into the soil or injected into irrigation water, reacts with the lime, an acid + carbonate reaction:

H2SO4(aq) + CaCO3(s) → CaSO4(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

The product, calcium sulfate, is gypsum itself. Growers can also spread elemental sulfur; soil bacteria slowly oxidize it to sulfuric acid, which then dissolves the lime. The acid works fast but is dangerous to handle; sulfur is safer but can take until the next summer to act. Gypsum is also spread on east-side orchards and vineyards irrigated with very pure snowmelt canal water, where the low salt content of the water itself slows infiltration. A University of California Cooperative Extension publication from Tulare County reports that 1 to 2 tons of gypsum per acre can increase infiltration as much as threefold.

Sources: A. O'Geen, Reclaiming Saline, Sodic, and Saline-Sodic Soils, UC Agriculture and Natural Resources Publication 8519 (2015), escholarship.org/uc/item/3791z02f; B. Peacock, Can Gypsum Improve Water Penetration?, UC Cooperative Extension Tulare County Publication IG8-97 (ucanr.edu).

Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up the litmus colors: acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue.
  • Expecting phenolphthalein to change color in acid: it is colorless in acids and in neutral water; only bases turn it pink.
  • Forgetting the CO2 and H2O in an acid + carbonate reaction (writing H2CO3 as a product).
  • Leaving H2 out of an acid + metal reaction, or forgetting to balance the H atoms.

Summary

  • Acids: sour, blue litmus to red, phenolphthalein colorless, react with active metals (H2) and carbonates (CO2), neutralize bases.
  • Bases: bitter and slippery, red litmus to blue, phenolphthalein pink, neutralize acids.
  • Red cabbage indicator runs from red (strong acid) through purple (near neutral) to green and yellow (strong base).
  • Acid + base → salt + water; acids and bases are electrolytes.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A cleaning solution turns red cabbage indicator green. Is it acidic or basic? What color would it turn red litmus paper and phenolphthalein?

Hint

Look up green in the red cabbage color chart in 12.1: is it an acid color or a base color? Then use BRA for litmus and "pink means base" for phenolphthalein.

Answer

Basic (green is a base color for cabbage). It turns red litmus blue and phenolphthalein pink.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write a balanced equation for the reaction of magnesium carbonate with hydrochloric acid. What would you see?

Hint

Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. Build the salt from the ion charges, then balance Cl and H. The gas tells you what you would see.

Answer

MgCO3(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). The solid dissolves with fizzing as CO2 gas bubbles out.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Write the balanced equation for the neutralization of nitric acid by calcium hydroxide, and name the salt.

Hint

Acid + base → salt + water. Ca2+ needs two NO3−, so count the HNO3 and waters that takes; the salt is the base's cation with the acid's anion.

Answer

2 HNO3(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → Ca(NO3)2(aq) + 2 H2O(l). The salt is calcium nitrate, made of the base's cation (Ca2+) and the acid's anion (NO3−).
