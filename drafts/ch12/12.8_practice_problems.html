12.1 Properties of Acids and Bases

Problem 12.1

Which observation shows that a solution is basic?

A. It turns blue litmus paper red.
B. It stays colorless when phenolphthalein is added.
C. It turns red cabbage indicator green.
D. It fizzes when a piece of chalk (calcium carbonate) is dropped in.

Hint

For each choice, name the indicator and recall its color in acid and in base (BRA for litmus). Which kind of solution makes chalk fizz?

Answer

C. Red cabbage indicator turns blue, green, or yellow in basic solutions; green means roughly pH 9 to 11.

  • A is the acid test ("BRA": blue turns red in acid). A base turns red litmus blue; this choice swaps the colors.
  • B describes an acidic or neutral solution. Phenolphthalein turns pink only in base.
  • D is a property of acids: acid + carbonate makes CO2 gas. A base does not fizz with a carbonate.

Problem 12.2

In a lab modeled on Experiment 12a (pH of Household Items), a student adds red cabbage indicator to seven samples and records the colors below.

(d) Name an acidic sample and a basic sample that would fizz if mixed, and identify the gas that forms.

Problem 12.3

A strip of aluminum foil is dropped into hydrochloric acid. (a) Write the balanced equation. (b) Describe what you would see. (c) Which property of acids does this reaction show?

Hint

(a) Acid + active metal → salt + hydrogen gas; aluminum forms Al3+. (b) Think about the gas and the metal. (c) Compare with the list of acid properties in 12.1.

Answer

(a) 2 Al(s) + 6 HCl(aq) → 2 AlCl3(aq) + 3 H2(g)

Check: 2 Al, 6 H, and 6 Cl on each side. Aluminum forms Al3+, so each Al needs three Cl−, and the six H atoms leave as three H2 molecules.

(b) Bubbles of a colorless gas (hydrogen) form on the metal, and the foil slowly dissolves. The solution may get warm.

(c) Acids react with active metals to make hydrogen gas, a single-displacement reaction (Section 7.8), which is why acids corrode metals.

Problem 12.4

Some effervescent antacid tablets contain potassium bicarbonate, KHCO3. Which set of products forms when KHCO3 reacts with hydrochloric acid?

A. KCl(aq) + H2CO3(aq), and nothing else
B. KCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
C. KCl(aq) + H2(g) + CO2(g)
D. KCl(aq) + H2O(l), with no gas

Hint

Swap partners first: what forms from H+ and HCO3−? Then use the unstable-products table to see what that product becomes.

Answer

B. Acid + bicarbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide:

KHCO3(s) + HCl(aq) → KCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

Check: 1 K, 2 H, 1 C, 3 O, and 1 Cl on each side. The CO2 is the fizz you see in the glass.

  • A stops halfway. H2CO3 forms first but breaks down into H2O and CO2, so it is not written as a product.
  • C mixes in the acid + metal reaction. H2 forms when an acid reacts with an active metal, not with a bicarbonate.
  • D leaves out the CO2. Any carbonate or bicarbonate releases CO2 gas with an acid; that is why the tablet fizzes.

Problem 12.5

Write a balanced molecular equation for each neutralization, and name the salt: (a) hydroiodic acid with lithium hydroxide; (b) hydrochloric acid with barium hydroxide. (c) Write the net ionic equation that the two reactions share.

Hint

The salt is the base's cation with the acid's anion; Ba(OH)2 has two OH−, so it needs two acid molecules. For (c), cross out the spectator ions.

Answer

(a) HI(aq) + LiOH(aq) → LiI(aq) + H2O(l)    salt: lithium iodide

(b) 2 HCl(aq) + Ba(OH)2(aq) → BaCl2(aq) + 2 H2O(l)    salt: barium chloride

In (b), Ba(OH)2 supplies two OH−, so it needs two HCl and makes two waters. Each salt is the base's cation with the acid's anion.

(c) H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)

All four reactants are strong electrolytes, so Li+, I−, Ba2+, and Cl− are spectator ions. In (b) the equation H+ + OH− → H2O happens twice for every Ba(OH)2.

Problem 12.6

The surface of healthy skin is usually around pH 5, while many ordinary bar soaps are around pH 9 to 10. Which statement is correct?

A. Skin's surface is basic, so it would turn red litmus blue.
B. The soap is acidic, because it removes oils from the skin.
C. A drop of phenolphthalein on wet soap lather would stay colorless.
D. The soap is basic, so it would turn red litmus paper blue.

Hint

Classify each pH first: below 7 acidic, above 7 basic. Then check each indicator statement: BRA for litmus, pink above about pH 8 for phenolphthalein.

Answer

D. A pH above 7 is basic, and bases turn red litmus blue. (The slippery feel of soap is another base property.) This is the reason soaps for sensitive skin are often formulated closer to skin's own pH.

  • A reverses the classification. A pH near 5 is mildly acidic (the "acid mantle"), so skin would turn blue litmus red, not red litmus blue.
  • B confuses what soap does with its pH. Removing oils is not an acid property; a pH of 9 to 10 is basic.
  • C has the indicator backward. Phenolphthalein turns pink above about pH 8, so it would turn pink in the lather.

Problem 12.7

A soil scientist checks a west-side Valley field for natural lime (calcium carbonate) by putting a few drops of dilute hydrochloric acid on a soil sample. (a) If the soil contains lime, what will she see, and what gas forms? Write the balanced equation. (b) A sample of gypsum (calcium sulfate, CaSO4) gives no fizz. Why not? (c) Growers who cannot handle sulfuric acid safely can spread elemental sulfur instead; soil bacteria turn it into sulfuric acid. Balance this overall equation: __ S(s) + __ O2(g) + __ H2O(l) → __ H2SO4(aq).

Hint

(a) Acid + carbonate → salt + water + CO2. (b) Does CaSO4 contain a carbonate or bicarbonate? (c) Balance S first, then H with water, and O last.

Answer

(a) The soil fizzes, because acid + carbonate makes carbon dioxide gas:

CaCO3(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

(b) Calcium sulfate is a salt with no carbonate or bicarbonate ion, so there is nothing to make CO2. It is not a base, and it does not neutralize the acid.

12.2 Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry Definitions; Conjugate Pairs

Problem 12.8

Which statement is the Brønsted–Lowry definition of a base?

A. A substance that donates a proton (H+)
B. A substance that produces OH− ions when it dissolves in water
C. A substance that accepts a proton (H+)
D. Any substance whose formula contains hydrogen

Hint

Brønsted–Lowry definitions are about a proton (H+) moving between substances. Recall what an acid does with the proton; a base does the opposite. Which choice is the older Arrhenius definition?

Answer

C. Brønsted–Lowry bases are proton acceptors ("BAD": bases accept, acids donate).

  • A is the Brønsted–Lowry definition of an acid.
  • B is the Arrhenius definition of a base. It is narrower: it misses bases such as NH3 that contain no hydroxide.
  • D is a common trap. NH3 and CH4 both contain H, and neither donates it in water; having H atoms does not decide anything.

Problem 12.9

Identify the Brønsted–Lowry acid, base, conjugate acid, and conjugate base in each reaction.

(a) HCN(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + CN−(aq)

(b) HCO3−(aq) + OH−(aq) → CO32−(aq) + H2O(l)

(c) C5H5N(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ C5H5NH+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Hint

For each reaction, follow the H+: the reactant that loses an H is the acid, and the one that gains it is the base. Each conjugate shares a common piece with its partner.

Answer

(a) HCN loses H+: acid; CN− is its conjugate base. H2O gains H+: base; H3O+ is its conjugate acid.

(b) HCO3− loses H+: acid; CO32− is its conjugate base. OH− gains H+: base; H2O is its conjugate acid.

(c) C5H5N (pyridine, an amine-like base) gains H+: base; C5H5NH+ is its conjugate acid. H2O loses H+: acid; OH− is its conjugate base.

Check: every pair differs by exactly one H and one unit of charge. Notice that water is the base in (a) and the acid in (c), and that bicarbonate, which is often a base, acts as an acid toward the stronger base OH− in (b).

Problem 12.10

What is the conjugate base of the ammonium ion, NH4+?

A. NH52+
B. NH4
C. NH2−
D. NH3

Hint

A conjugate base has exactly one fewer H and a charge one lower than its acid.

Answer

D. To make a conjugate base, remove one H+: one fewer H, and the charge goes down by 1, from +1 to 0. NH4+ − H+ = NH3.

  • A adds an H+. That is the direction for a conjugate acid, and NH4+ does not actually accept another proton.
  • B removes the charge without removing the H. A proton carries the +1 charge with it.
  • C removes two protons. Conjugates differ by exactly one H+; NH2− is the conjugate base of NH3, not of NH4+.

Problem 12.11

Write the conjugate base of (a) HClO2; (b) H2S; (c) HC2O4− (hydrogen oxalate). Write the conjugate acid of (d) PO43−; (e) H2O; (f) C2H3O2− (acetate).

Hint

Conjugate base: remove one H+ (one fewer H, charge down 1). Conjugate acid: add one H+ (one more H, charge up 1). Watch the charges on the ions.

Answer

Conjugate base: remove one H and lower the charge by 1. Conjugate acid: add one H and raise the charge by 1.

(a) ClO2−    (b) HS−    (c) C2O42−    (d) HPO42−    (e) H3O+    (f) HC2H3O2

In (c), the charge goes from −1 to −2, not to 0: removing a positive proton makes the ion more negative. In (f), the H that acetate gains is the acidic H of acetic acid, written first in HC2H3O2.

Problem 12.12

In the reaction below, which two species form a conjugate acid–base pair?

NH4+(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ NH3(aq) + H3O+(aq)

A. NH4+ and H2O
B. NH4+ and NH3
C. H2O and NH3
D. NH4+ and H3O+

Hint

A conjugate pair has one member on each side of the arrow, and the two differ by exactly one H+.

Answer

B. NH4+ donates a proton and becomes NH3; the two differ by exactly one H+. (The other pair is H2O and H3O+.)

  • A pairs the two reactants. They are the acid and the base of the forward reaction, not partners; a conjugate pair always has one member on each side.
  • C pairs species that differ by more than one H+ (different elements, too). Water's partner here is H3O+.
  • D pairs the two acids, one from each side. A pair is an acid with its own conjugate base.

Problem 12.13

The dihydrogen phosphate ion, H2PO4−, is amphoteric. (a) Write a balanced equation for H2PO4− acting as an acid toward OH−. (b) Write a balanced equation for H2PO4− acting as a base toward H3O+. (c) What does "amphoteric" mean, and what common substance is the most important example?

Hint

(a) As an acid, H2PO4− gives one H+ to OH−. (b) As a base, it takes one from H3O+. Check the charges. (c) See water's double role in 12.2.

Answer

(a) H2PO4−(aq) + OH−(aq) → HPO42−(aq) + H2O(l)

(b) H2PO4−(aq) + H3O+(aq) → H3PO4(aq) + H2O(l)

Check the charges: (a) −1 + (−1) = −2 on the left and −2 + 0 on the right; (b) −1 + 1 = 0 on both sides. Atoms balance too.

(c) An amphoteric substance can act as either an acid or a base, depending on what it reacts with. Water is the most important example. (Because H2PO4− can neutralize both added base and added acid, phosphate is a buffer inside cells, Section 12.6.)

Problem 12.14

The stomach bacterium Helicobacter pylori makes the enzyme urease, which breaks down urea:

CO(NH2)2(aq) + H2O(l) → CO2(aq) + 2 NH3(aq)

(a) Show that the equation is balanced. (b) The ammonia then reacts with stomach acid: NH3 + H3O+ → NH4+ + H2O. Identify the Brønsted–Lowry acid and base. (c) How many moles of H3O+ can the ammonia from 1 mol of urea neutralize? (d) Why does this help the bacterium survive?

Hint

(a) Count C, H, N, and O on each side. (b) BAD: who donates the proton, and who accepts it? (c) Use the urea-to-NH3 mole ratio; each NH3 accepts one proton.

Answer

(a) Left: 1 C, 2 O (one in urea, one in water), 2 N, and 6 H (4 + 2). Right: 1 C and 2 O in CO2, and 2 N and 6 H in 2 NH3. Balanced.

(b) H3O+ donates a proton: acid. NH3 accepts it: base. (Their conjugates are H2O and NH4+.)

(c) 1 mol urea gives 2 mol NH3, and each NH3 accepts one proton, so 2 mol H3O+.

(d) Removing H3O+ makes the fluid right around the bacterium less acidic, so it can survive long enough to burrow into the protective mucus layer of the stomach lining.

12.3 Identifying Acids, Bases, and Salts; Strong and Weak

Problem 12.15

Classify each substance as a strong acid, weak acid, strong base, weak base, salt, or none of these. (Some fit two labels.) (a) HBr; (b) HF; (c) LiOH; (d) CH3CH2CH2NH2 (propylamine); (e) MgCl2; (f) glycerol, HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH; (g) H2CO3; (h) NaHCO3.

Hint

Check the green-sheet strong lists first. Then: H first (acid), metal hydroxide or amine (base), metal with another anion (salt). Carbonates and bicarbonates can be salts and bases.

Answer
  • (a) Strong acid: on the strong-acid list.
  • (b) Weak acid: not on the list. HF is the classic trap among the halogen acids.
  • (c) Strong base: a Group 1 hydroxide.
  • (d) Weak base: an amine; the lone pair on N accepts a proton.
  • (e) Salt: Mg2+ (from a base) with Cl− (from an acid).
  • (f) None of these: a molecular alcohol. Its –OH groups are covalently bonded, not hydroxide ions.
  • (g) Weak acid: carbonic acid is not on the strong list.
  • (h) Salt and weak base: bicarbonate accepts protons, as in baking soda and antacids.

Problem 12.16

Which of these is a strong acid?

A. HF, hydrofluoric acid
B. H3PO4, phosphoric acid
C. HC2H3O2, acetic acid
D. HClO4, perchloric acid

Hint

Only six common acids are strong; recall the green-sheet list. Having more H atoms, or a halogen, does not make an acid strong.

Answer

D. HClO4 is one of the six strong acids on the green sheet (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4), so it ionizes completely in water.

  • A is weak. HCl, HBr, and HI are strong, but HF is not, even though fluorine is in the same group.
  • B is weak. Having three H atoms does not make an acid strong; strength is about how completely it ionizes, and H3PO4 is not on the list.
  • C is weak: the acid in vinegar is mostly intact molecules in water.

Problem 12.17

Which statement best describes a 0.10 M solution of lactic acid, HC3H5O3?

A. [H3O+] = 0.10 M, because each lactic acid molecule gives one H+.
B. Most of the lactic acid stays as intact HC3H5O3 molecules, so [H3O+] is much less than 0.10 M.
C. It contains no H3O+ at all, because lactic acid is weak.
D. It has the same [H3O+] as 0.10 M HCl, because the two concentrations are equal.

Hint

Is lactic acid on the green-sheet strong-acid list? How much of a weak acid ionizes, compared with a strong acid?

Answer

B. Lactic acid is not on the strong-acid list, so it is weak: only a small fraction of its molecules donate a proton at any moment.

HC3H5O3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + C3H5O3−(aq)

  • A treats a weak acid as if it ionized completely. That shortcut, [H3O+] = acid molarity, works only for strong acids.
  • C goes too far the other way. A weak acid ionizes slightly, not zero; the solution is still acidic.
  • D confuses concentration with strength. Equal molarities do not mean equal ionization: the HCl is all ions, the lactic acid mostly molecules.

Problem 12.18

Write the ionization or dissociation equation in water for each, with the correct arrow: (a) HI; (b) formic acid, HCHO2 (the acid in ant stings); (c) LiOH; (d) Ca(OH)2; (e) dimethylamine, (CH3)2NH.

Hint

Strong acids and bases get a single arrow; weak ones get a double arrow. Acids and amines react with water; hydroxides just dissociate (count the OH−).

Answer

(a) HI(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + I−(aq)    (strong acid: single arrow)

(b) HCHO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + CHO2−(aq)    (weak acid: double arrow)

(c) LiOH(aq) → Li+(aq) + OH−(aq)    (strong base)

(d) Ca(OH)2(aq) → Ca2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq)    (strong base; two OH− per formula unit)

(e) (CH3)2NH(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ (CH3)2NH2+(aq) + OH−(aq)    (weak base)

Only the H written first in HCHO2 is acidic, so formate is CHO2−. The amine in (e) takes a proton from water onto its nitrogen, like NH3.

Problem 12.19

B. HNO3, nitric acid

Problem 12.20

Predict the salt and write a balanced molecular equation for each neutralization: (a) nitric acid with potassium hydroxide; (b) acetic acid with calcium hydroxide; (c) sulfuric acid with barium hydroxide (the salt is insoluble). (d) In each, how many waters form per formula unit of salt?

Hint

Salt = base's cation + acid's anion; balance so every H+ meets an OH−. Check solubility on the green sheet. The number of waters equals the H+ neutralized.

Answer

(a) HNO3(aq) + KOH(aq) → KNO3(aq) + H2O(l)

(b) 2 HC2H3O2(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → Ca(C2H3O2)2(aq) + 2 H2O(l)

Problem 12.21

Which equation is written correctly?

A. HF(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + F−(aq)
B. HNO3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + NO3−(aq)
C. Sr(OH)2(aq) → Sr2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq)
D. NH3(aq) + H2O(l) → NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Hint

Classify each substance as strong or weak first: strong gets a single arrow, weak a double arrow. Then check that each equation balances.

Answer

C. Sr(OH)2 is a strong base, so it gets a single arrow, and each formula unit releases two OH−. Mass and charge balance (+2 and 2 × −1 = 0).

  • A uses a single arrow for a weak acid. HF ionizes only slightly, so it needs a double arrow.
  • B uses a double arrow for a strong acid. HNO3 ionizes completely, so it gets a single arrow.
  • D uses a single arrow for a weak base. Ammonia accepts protons from water only to a small extent.

Problem 12.22

Three 0.10 M solutions are tested with a light-bulb conductivity tester: hydrochloric acid (HCl), acetic acid (HC2H3O2), and sucrose (table sugar, C12H22O11). Predict whether the bulb glows brightly, dimly, or not at all for each, and explain using the particles present.

Hint

Bulb brightness tracks how many ions are present. Which is a strong acid (all ions), which is weak (mostly molecules), and which forms no ions?

Answer

HCl: bright. A strong acid is completely ionized, so the solution is full of H3O+ and Cl− ions that carry current: a strong electrolyte.

Acetic acid: dim. A weak acid is mostly intact molecules, with only a few H3O+ and C2H3O2− ions: a weak electrolyte.

Sucrose: not at all (or no more than water). Sucrose dissolves as whole molecules and forms no ions: a nonelectrolyte.

The two acids have the same concentration, so the difference in brightness comes from strength (how completely they ionize), not from how much acid was dissolved.

12.4 Water: Autoionization and Kw

Problem 12.24

Which statement about pure water at 25 °C is true?

A. It contains no ions at all, because it is neutral.
B. [H3O+] = [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−7 M.
C. [H3O+] = [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 M.
D. [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−7 M, but [OH−] = 0, because only acids make ions.

Hint

Write water's autoionization: each H3O+ forms with one OH−. The two equal concentrations multiply to give Kw; what must each one be?

Answer

B. Water autoionizes, H2O + H2O ⇄ H3O+ + OH−, making one of each ion. In pure water the two are equal, and their product is Kw: (1.0 × 10−7)(1.0 × 10−7) = 1.0 × 10−14.

  • A mixes up "neutral" with "no ions." Neutral means the two ion concentrations are equal; pure water has a tiny but real amount of each.
  • C uses Kw itself as each concentration. 1.0 × 10−14 is the product of the two.
  • D forgets that autoionization makes the ions in pairs: every H3O+ formed leaves an OH− behind.

Problem 12.32

12.5 pH and pOH of Strong Acid and Base Solutions

Problem 12.33

What is the pH of 0.0042 M HNO3?

A. −2.38
B. 2.4
C. 2.38
D. 11.62

Hint

HNO3 is strong, so [H3O+] equals its molarity. Take −log, and count significant figures in the concentration for the decimal places.

Answer

C. HNO3 is a strong acid, so [H3O+] = 0.0042 M = 4.2 × 10−3 M.

\( \text{pH} = -\log(4.2 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.3767\ldots = 2.38 \)

Two significant figures in 4.2 give two decimal places. Check: the concentration is between 10−3 and 10−2 M, so the pH is between 2 and 3.

  • A uses pH = log[H3O+] and drops the minus sign in the definition. Ordinary pH values are positive.
  • B has the wrong number of decimal places. A two-significant-figure concentration needs two decimal places in the pH.
  • D is 14.00 − 2.38, the pOH. An acid must have a pH below 7.

Problem 12.34

Hint

KOH is strong with one OH−. Take −log[OH−] for the pOH, then subtract from 14.00; two significant figures give two decimal places.

Answer

KOH is a strong base with one OH−, so [OH−] = 4.5 × 10−3 M.

\( \text{pOH} = -\log(4.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.3468\ldots = 2.35 \qquad \text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.35 = 11.65 \)

pOH 2.35, pH 11.65. Two significant figures give two decimal places, and a pH above 7 fits a base. Check the other way: [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14 ÷ 4.5 × 10−3 = 2.22 × 10−12 M, and −log of that is also 11.65.

12.6 Buffers

Problem 12.44

Which mixture is a buffer?

A. HCl and NaCl
B. NH3 and NaOH
C. HNO2 and NaNO3
D. HCN and KCN

Hint

A buffer needs a weak acid with its own conjugate base (the same formula, one H+ less). Cross out strong acids first, then check each partner.

Answer

D. HCN (hydrocyanic acid) is a weak acid, and KCN supplies CN−, its conjugate base. The two differ by exactly one H+.

  • A is a strong acid with its salt. Cl− is too weak a base to take back protons, so nothing in the mixture can neutralize added acid.
  • B is a weak base with a strong base, not with its conjugate acid. NH3's partner would be NH4+ (from NH4Cl), and nothing here neutralizes added base.
  • C is a near miss: HNO2 is weak, but NO3− (nitrate) is not its conjugate base. The partner of HNO2 is NO2− (nitrite), from NaNO2.

Problem 12.45

A formate buffer contains formic acid, HCHO2, and sodium formate, NaCHO2. (a) Identify the weak acid and the conjugate base. (b) Write the net ionic equation for the reaction that occurs when a little HCl is added. (c) Write the net ionic equation for the reaction when a little NaOH is added.

Hint

(a) Which species has the acidic H, and which is the same formula minus one H+? (b) The base partner takes H3O+. (c) The acid partner gives a proton to OH−. Leave Na+ out.

Answer

(a) Weak acid: HCHO2. Conjugate base: CHO2− (formate), supplied by the salt; Na+ is a spectator.

(b) CHO2−(aq) + H3O+(aq) → HCHO2(aq) + H2O(l)

(c) HCHO2(aq) + OH−(aq) → CHO2−(aq) + H2O(l)

The conjugate base handles added acid, and the weak acid handles added base. In each case a strong acid or base is replaced by a member of the weak buffer pair, so the pH changes only a little.

Problem 12.46

In the blood's buffer of carbonic acid (H2CO3) and bicarbonate (HCO3−), which species neutralizes a small amount of added OH−?

B. HCO3−

H2CO3(aq) + OH−(aq) → HCO3−(aq) + H2O(l)

Problem 12.47

Problem 12.48

A 1.00 L phosphate buffer contains 0.050 mol H2PO4− and 0.050 mol HPO42−. (a) Which partner reacts when HCl is added? Write the reaction. (Ignore the small change in volume, and the H+ from the weak acid.)

(a) Added acid reacts with the base partner, 1 : 1. (b) Subtract from one partner and add to the other. (c) What happens to the extra acid once the base partner runs out?

(a) HPO42−(aq) + H3O+(aq) → H2PO4−(aq) + H2O(l)

C. A buffer needs a weak acid and its conjugate base. HCl is completely ionized, and its "conjugate base" Cl− does not accept protons, so added acid simply adds to [H3O+] and the pH falls.

  • A is false: NaCl is the classic salt, a base's cation (Na+) with an acid's anion (Cl−).
  • B has HCl's strength backward. HCl is a strong acid (on the list), and a strong acid does neutralize added base; the missing piece is a partner to neutralize added acid.
  • D is false: a buffer contains an acid partner and a base partner, one for each kind of addition.

Problem 12.50

During a hard sprint, muscle cells release lactic acid, HC3H5O3, into the blood. (a) Write the equation for lactic acid reacting with bicarbonate ion, HCO3−. (b) The H2CO3 formed breaks down. Write that equation. (c) Use (a) and (b) to explain why a runner breathes hard for a while after the sprint ends.

Hint

(a) Lactic acid donates its H+ to HCO3−. (b) H2CO3 breaks down as in acid + carbonate reactions. (c) Where does the extra CO2 go?

Answer

(a) HC3H5O3(aq) + HCO3−(aq) → C3H5O3−(aq) + H2CO3(aq)

(b) H2CO3(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(aq)

In (a), lactic acid donates a proton (acid) and bicarbonate accepts it (base): the base partner of the blood buffer is neutralizing the added acid. Check: 4 C, 7 H, 6 O, and a −1 charge on each side.

(c) Buffering the lactic acid makes extra CO2. Breathing hard clears that CO2 through the lungs, and with it the acid. If the CO2 stayed, it would form H2CO3 again and lower the pH.

Problem 12.51

A technician dilutes 5.00 mL of 0.600 M HCl with water to a total volume of 250.0 mL. (a) What is the molarity of the diluted acid? (b) What is its pH? (c) What color would red cabbage indicator turn in the diluted acid? (d) A classmate forgets the dilution and finds the pH of the 0.600 M stock. What pH does she get?

Hint

(a) Use M1V1 = M2V2 (Section 11.4). (b) HCl is strong; take −log and count significant figures. (c) Use the red cabbage chart. (d) Take −log of the stock molarity.

Answer

(a) \( M_2 = \dfrac{M_1 V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{(0.600\ M)(5.00\ \text{mL})}{250.0\ \text{mL}} = 0.0120\ M \)

(b) \( \text{pH} = -\log(0.0120) = 1.9208\ldots = 1.921 \)

(a) 0.0120 M (Section 11.4); (b) HCl is strong, so [H3O+] = 0.0120 M, and the pH is 1.921: three significant figures (0.0120) give three decimal places.

(c) A pH of about 2 is in the 1 to 2 range: red.

(d) −log(0.600) = 0.222. Diluting by a factor of 50 raises the pH by log 50 = 1.70 units, so skipping the dilution makes the acid look 50 times more concentrated than it is.

Problem 12.52

A student mixes 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HNO3 with 25.0 mL of 0.120 M NaOH.

HNO3(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaNO3(aq) + H2O(l)

(a) How many moles of H3O+ and of OH− are mixed? Which is in excess, and by how many moles? (b) What is the concentration of the excess ion in the 50.0 mL of mixture? (c) Find the pH, and classify the final solution as acidic, basic, or neutral. (d) Would a drop of phenolphthalein turn the mixture pink?

Hint

(a) Moles = L × M for each; they react 1 : 1, so subtract. (b) Divide the leftover moles by the total volume. (c) Leftover OH− means pOH first. (d) Phenolphthalein is pink above about pH 8.

Answer

(a) \( 0.0250\ \text{L} \times 0.100\ \tfrac{\text{mol}}{\text{L}} = 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol H}_3\text{O}^+ \qquad 0.0250\ \text{L} \times 0.120\ \tfrac{\text{mol}}{\text{L}} = 3.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol OH}^- \)

(a) The acid and base react 1 : 1, so OH− is in excess by 3.00 × 10−3 − 2.50 × 10−3 = 5.0 × 10−4 mol (two significant figures: subtraction keeps the decimal places, 0.00050 mol).

(b) \( [\text{OH}^-] = \dfrac{5.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol}}{0.0500\ \text{L}} = 0.010\ M \)

(c) \( \text{pOH} = -\log(0.010) = 2.00 \qquad \text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.00 = 12.00 \)

(b) 0.010 M, using the total volume, 25.0 + 25.0 = 50.0 mL. (c) pH 12.00, basic. (d) Yes: phenolphthalein turns pink above about pH 8. The NaNO3 formed is a salt of a strong acid and a strong base and does not change the pH; only the leftover NaOH does.

Problem 12.53

A nurse compares two antacids: one dose of milk of magnesia contains 0.400 g of magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)2 (58.32 g/mol), and one chewable tablet contains 0.500 g of calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (100.09 g/mol). Treat stomach acid as 0.0800 M HCl.

Mg(OH)2(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + 2 H2O(l)

CaCO3(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

(a) What volume of the stomach acid can each dose neutralize, in mL? (b) Which neutralizes more acid? (c) Which one can cause belching, and why? (d) Is Mg(OH)2 a strong base by the green-sheet list?

Hint

(a) The goal is the mole: g → mol antacid → mol HCl (from each equation) → volume of 0.0800 M acid. (c) Which product is a gas? (d) Check the green-sheet strong-base list.

Answer

(a) \( 0.400\ \text{g Mg(OH)}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{58.32\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol HCl}}{1\ \text{mol Mg(OH)}_2} = 0.013717\ \text{mol HCl} \qquad V = \dfrac{0.013717\ \text{mol}}{0.0800\ \text{mol/L}} = 0.1715\ \text{L} = 171\ \text{mL} \)

\( 0.500\ \text{g CaCO}_3 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{100.09\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol HCl}}{1\ \text{mol CaCO}_3} = 9.991 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol HCl} \qquad V = \dfrac{9.991 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}}{0.0800\ \text{mol/L}} = 0.1249\ \text{L} = 125\ \text{mL} \)

(a) Milk of magnesia: 171 mL; calcium carbonate tablet: 125 mL. (b) The milk of magnesia dose, even though it has less mass, because Mg(OH)2 has a smaller molar mass and each formula unit still neutralizes two HCl.

(c) Calcium carbonate: acid + carbonate makes CO2 gas. (d) No. Only Group 1 hydroxides and Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, and Ba(OH)2 are strong; Mg(OH)2 is poorly soluble and treated as a weak base, but the solid still reacts completely with acid. (Context only; antacids are taken as directed on the label or by the provider.)

Problem 12.54

A stockroom shelf holds these chemicals: HBr, KBr, benzoic acid (HC7H5O2, a food preservative), sodium benzoate (NaC7H5O2), pyridine (C5H5N, a weak base), pyridinium chloride (C5H5NHCl), and NaCl. (a) Write the conjugate base of benzoic acid and the conjugate acid of pyridine. (b) Which pairs of chemicals from the shelf would make a buffer? (c) HBr and KBr also contain a conjugate pair, HBr and Br−. Why don't they make a buffer? (d) For one of your buffers, write the reaction that neutralizes added H3O+ and the one that neutralizes added OH−.

Hint

(a) Remove one H+ from benzoic acid; add one H+ to pyridine. (b) Look for a weak acid or weak base with its conjugate partner; ignore Na+ and Cl−. (c) Is HBr strong?

Answer

(a) Conjugate base of HC7H5O2: C7H5O2− (benzoate). Conjugate acid of C5H5N: C5H5NH+ (pyridinium).

(b) Benzoic acid + sodium benzoate (a weak acid and its conjugate base; Na+ is a spectator) and pyridine + pyridinium chloride (a weak base and its conjugate acid; Cl− is a spectator).

(c) HBr is a strong acid: it is completely ionized, and Br− is too weak a base to take a proton back, so the mixture cannot neutralize added acid.

(d) For the benzoate buffer:

C7H5O2−(aq) + H3O+(aq) → HC7H5O2(aq) + H2O(l)

HC7H5O2(aq) + OH−(aq) → C7H5O2−(aq) + H2O(l)

For the pyridine buffer: C5H5N + H3O+ → C5H5NH+ + H2O, and C5H5NH+ + OH− → C5H5N + H2O.

Problem 12.55

A student dissolves 0.56 g of KOH (56.11 g/mol) in water and fills a volumetric flask to the 500.0 mL line. (a) What is the molarity of KOH? (b) Find [OH−], pOH, pH, and [H3O+]. (c) What color would red cabbage indicator turn in this solution? (d) Which would raise [OH−] more: dissolving 0.010 mol of KOH or 0.010 mol of Ca(OH)2 in the same volume? Explain.

Hint

(a) Grams → moles, then divide by liters. (b) KOH is strong with one OH−: pOH, then pH, then Kw. (c) Use the cabbage chart. (d) Count the OH− per formula unit.

Answer

(a) \( 0.56\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{56.11\ \text{g}} = 9.98 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \qquad M = \dfrac{9.98 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}}{0.5000\ \text{L}} = 0.01996\ M = 0.020\ M \)

(b) \( \text{pOH} = -\log(0.01996) = 1.6998\ldots = 1.70 \qquad \text{pH} = 14.00 - 1.70 = 12.30 \qquad [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{0.01996} = 5.0 \times 10^{-13}\ M \)

(a) 0.020 M (two significant figures, set by 0.56 g). (b) KOH is a strong base with one OH−: [OH−] = 0.020 M, pOH 1.70, pH 12.30, [H3O+] = 5.0 × 10−13 M.

(c) pH 12.30 is in the 12 to 14 range: yellow.

(d) Ca(OH)2: each formula unit releases two OH−, so 0.010 mol gives 0.020 mol OH−, twice as much as 0.010 mol of KOH. (Both are strong bases.)

Problem 12.56

What is the conjugate base of the dihydrogen phosphate ion, H2PO4−?

A. HPO42−
B. PO43−
C. H3PO4
D. HPO4−

Hint

A conjugate base is one rung down the proton ladder: one fewer H, and the charge goes down by 1.

Answer

A. A conjugate base is one rung down the proton ladder: remove one H+ (one H, and the charge goes down by 1). H3PO4 ↔ H2PO4− ↔ HPO42− ↔ PO43−.

  • B removes two protons, two rungs down. A conjugate is always one H+ away.
  • C adds a proton: H3PO4 is the conjugate acid of H2PO4−.
  • D removes the H but forgets to change the charge. Removing H+ takes a positive charge away, so −1 becomes −2.

Problem 12.57

For the reaction NH4+(aq) + CO32−(aq) ⇄ NH3(aq) + HCO3−(aq): (a) label the acid, base, conjugate acid, and conjugate base; (b) name the common piece in each conjugate pair; (c) check your labels by reading the reverse reaction.

Hint

(a) Follow the H+ from left to right (BAD). (b) Find the piece each pair shares. (c) Read right to left: does the conjugate acid hand its H+ back to the conjugate base?

Answer

(a) NH4+ loses H+: acid; NH3 is its conjugate base. CO32− gains H+: base; HCO3− is its conjugate acid.

(b) NH4+/NH3 share the "NH3" piece; CO32−/HCO3− share the "CO3" piece. Each pair differs by one H and one unit of charge.

(c) In reverse, HCO3− (the conjugate acid) gives its H+ to NH3 (the conjugate base), re-forming CO32− and NH4+. The reverse reaction agrees with the labels.
