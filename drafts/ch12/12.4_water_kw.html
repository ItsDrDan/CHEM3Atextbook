Learning Objectives

  • Write the equation for the autoionization of water.
  • Use Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C to find one ion concentration from the other.
  • Classify a solution as acidic, basic, or neutral from [H3O+] or [OH−].
  • Find [H3O+] and [OH−] in solutions of strong acids and strong bases.

Even the purest water contains a few ions. That fact links every acidic and basic solution together: once you know how much H3O+ a solution has, you also know how much OH− it has. The concentrations in this section use square brackets: [H3O+] means "the molarity of H3O+," in mol/L (Section 11.3).

Water Reacts with Itself

Water is amphoteric (Section 12.2), so one water molecule can donate a proton to another. This is the autoionization (self-ionization) of water:

H2O(l) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + OH−(aq)

One molecule acts as an acid and becomes OH−; the other acts as a base and becomes H3O+. The double arrow matters: the ions find each other and turn back into water almost as fast as they form, so at any instant only a tiny fraction of water molecules are ionized. In pure water at 25 °C,

[H3O+] = [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−7 M

That is about two ionized molecules for every billion water molecules. Because the two concentrations are equal, pure water is neutral.

The Ion-Product Constant, Kw

Multiply the two concentrations in pure water and you get 1.0 × 10−14. The key fact is that this product stays the same in every water solution at 25 °C, whether it is pure water, lemon juice, blood, or drain cleaner:

\( K_w = [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \quad (\text{at } 25\ {}^\circ\text{C}) \)

Kw is called the ion-product constant of water. It makes [H3O+] and [OH−] an inverse pair, like the two ends of a seesaw: when an acid raises [H3O+], [OH−] must fall, and when a base raises [OH−], [H3O+] must fall. Rearranged:

\( [\text{OH}^-] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]} \qquad [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{[\text{OH}^-]} \)

(Kw changes with temperature; this book always uses the 25 °C value.)

Acidic, Basic, and Neutral Solutions

Compare the ion concentrations with 1.0 × 10−7 M. The table below summarizes the three cases.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Classifying solutions at 25 °C
Solution[H3O+][OH−]Comparison
Acidicgreater than 1.0 × 10−7 Mless than 1.0 × 10−7 M[H3O+] > [OH−]
Neutral1.0 × 10−7 M1.0 × 10−7 M[H3O+] = [OH−]
Basicless than 1.0 × 10−7 Mgreater than 1.0 × 10−7 M[H3O+] < [OH−]

Watch the negative exponents. 1 × 10−4 (0.0001) is larger than 1 × 10−7 (0.0000001), so [H3O+] = 1 × 10−4 M is acidic. The smaller the negative exponent's size, the bigger the number.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): [OH−] from [H3O+]

Problem. A sports drink has [H3O+] = 2.5 × 10−3 M. Find [OH−] and classify the drink.

Solution.

\( [\text{OH}^-] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{2.5 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-12}\ M \)

[H3O+] is greater than 1.0 × 10−7 M, so the drink is acidic.

Check. Multiply back: (2.5 × 10−3)(4.0 × 10−12) = 1.0 × 10−14. Two significant figures, matching 2.5.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): [H3O+] from [OH−]

Problem. A diluted household cleaner has [OH−] = 3.2 × 10−5 M. Find [H3O+] and classify it.

Solution.

\( [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{3.2 \times 10^{-5}} = 3.125 \times 10^{-10} = 3.1 \times 10^{-10}\ M \)

[H3O+] is less than 1.0 × 10−7 M (and [OH−] is greater), so the cleaner is basic.

Check. One concentration went up from 10−7 and the other went down; the exponents −5 and −10 add to −15, and 3.2 × 3.1 ≈ 10, so the product is about 10 × 10−15 = 10−14.

Strong Acids and Bases Set the Concentrations

Because a strong acid ionizes completely (Section 12.3), each mole of a strong monoprotic acid such as HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, or HClO4 gives one mole of H3O+. The few ions from water's own autoionization are negligible next to that. So for these acids,

[H3O+] = molarity of the acid

For a strong base, [OH−] = molarity × the number of OH− in the formula: 1 for NaOH or KOH, 2 for Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, or Ba(OH)2.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A strong acid and a strong base

Problem. Find [H3O+] and [OH−] in (a) 0.020 M HCl and (b) 0.0050 M Sr(OH)2.

Solution. (a) HCl is strong, so [H3O+] = 0.020 M = 2.0 × 10−2 M, and

\( [\text{OH}^-] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{2.0 \times 10^{-2}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-13}\ M \)

(b) Sr(OH)2 → Sr2+ + 2 OH−, so [OH−] = 2 × 0.0050 M = 0.010 M = 1.0 × 10−2 M, and

\( [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.0 \times 10^{-2}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-12}\ M \)

Check. The acid solution has far more H3O+ than OH−; the base solution has the reverse. Forgetting the 2 in (b) would give 2.0 × 10−12 M, a common error.

Professor Tip

On the TI-36X Pro, enter 1.0 × 10−14 with the ×10n key: 1.0, ×10n, (−) 14, then ÷ and the other concentration entered the same way. Use the (−) negative key for the exponent, not the subtraction key. Then do a sanity check: if one concentration is above 10−7, the other must be below it.

Biology Connection: A Lining That Survives Its Own Acid

Gastric fluid in the stomach can reach [H3O+] of about 10−2 M, while the cells of the stomach wall work at nearly neutral conditions, about 10−7 M: a difference of roughly 100,000 times. The cells survive because they are covered by a thick layer of mucus, and the surface cells secrete bicarbonate (HCO3−) into it. Bicarbonate is a base, so it neutralizes H3O+ that diffuses into the mucus before it reaches the cells. When this barrier is damaged, for example by some pain relievers or by H. pylori infection (Section 12.2), acid can reach the lining and cause an ulcer, an open sore.

Common Mistakes

  • Misreading negative exponents: 10−3 is larger than 10−9.
  • Subtracting instead of dividing: [OH−] = Kw ÷ [H3O+], not 10−14 − [H3O+].
  • Thinking a basic solution has no H3O+: every water solution has both ions; only the balance changes.
  • Forgetting the 2 for Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, and Ba(OH)2.

Summary

  • Autoionization: H2O + H2O ⇄ H3O+ + OH−; in pure water each ion is 1.0 × 10−7 M at 25 °C.
  • Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 in every water solution at 25 °C; the two concentrations are inversely related.
  • Acidic: [H3O+] > 10−7 M. Neutral: equal. Basic: [OH−] > 10−7 M.
  • Strong acids: [H3O+] = acid molarity. Strong bases: [OH−] = base molarity × number of OH−.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A sample of urine has [OH−] = 6.7 × 10−9 M. Find [H3O+] and classify it.

Answer

[H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14 ÷ 6.7 × 10−9 = 1.5 × 10−6 M. This is greater than 10−7 M, so the sample is acidic (mildly).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Find [H3O+] and [OH−] in 0.0050 M NaOH.

Answer

NaOH is a strong base with one OH−: [OH−] = 5.0 × 10−3 M. [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14 ÷ 5.0 × 10−3 = 2.0 × 10−12 M.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which solution is more acidic: A, with [H3O+] = 4.0 × 10−4 M, or B, with [OH−] = 4.0 × 10−4 M? Find [OH−] in A.

Answer

A is acidic ([H3O+] above 10−7 M); B is basic. A is more acidic. In A, [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 ÷ 4.0 × 10−4 = 2.5 × 10−11 M.
