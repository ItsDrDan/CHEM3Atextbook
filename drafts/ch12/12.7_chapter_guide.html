Learning Objectives

  • Recognize acids and bases by their properties, definitions, and formulas, and classify them as strong or weak.
  • Identify conjugate acid–base pairs and write ionization and neutralization equations.
  • Use Kw, pH, and pOH to describe strong acid and strong base solutions.
  • Explain how buffers, including the blood bicarbonate buffer, resist changes in pH.

Key Ideas

1. Properties and indicators

Acids: sour, blue litmus to red, phenolphthalein colorless, react with active metals (H2) and carbonates (CO2). Bases: bitter, slippery, red litmus to blue, phenolphthalein pink. Red cabbage runs red (acid) → purple → green and yellow (base). Acid + base → salt + water. (Section 12.1)

2. Definitions and conjugate pairs

Arrhenius: acids make H+, bases make OH−. Brønsted–Lowry: acids donate H+, bases accept H+ ("BAD"). H+ in water is H3O+. Conjugate pairs differ by one H+; water is amphoteric. (Section 12.2)

3. Acids, bases, salts; strong and weak

Acids: H first or –COOH. Bases: hydroxides, carbonates, ammonia and amines. Salts: a base's cation with an acid's anion. Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4; strong bases: Group 1 hydroxides and Ca, Sr, Ba hydroxides. Strong: single arrow; weak: double arrow. Strength is not concentration. (Section 12.3)

4. Water and Kw

H2O + H2O ⇄ H3O+ + OH−. Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C. Acidic: [H3O+] > 10−7 M; neutral: equal; basic: [OH−] > 10−7 M. (Section 12.4)

5. pH and pOH

pH = −log[H3O+]; [H3O+] = 10−pH; pH + pOH = 14.00. Decimal places in pH = significant figures in the concentration. For strong bases, find [OH−] (times 2 for M(OH)2), then pOH, then pH. Blood: 7.35–7.45. (Section 12.5)

6. Buffers

A weak acid plus its conjugate base (or weak base plus conjugate acid) resists pH change: the base partner neutralizes added acid, the acid partner neutralizes added base. Blood: CO2 + H2O ⇄ H2CO3 ⇄ H+ + HCO3−; more CO2 means lower pH. (Section 12.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Pairs and arrows

Problem. For the reaction of hypochlorous acid, HClO (a weak acid), with water, write the equation with the correct arrow and label both conjugate acid–base pairs.

Solution.

HClO(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + ClO−(aq)

Pair 1: HClO (acid) / ClO− (conjugate base). Pair 2: H2O (base) / H3O+ (conjugate acid). The double arrow is used because HClO is not on the strong-acid list.

Check. Each pair differs by one H+ and one unit of charge.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): The full set from one concentration

Problem. Find [H3O+], [OH−], pH, and pOH for 0.0030 M Ca(OH)2.

Solution. Ca(OH)2 → Ca2+ + 2 OH−, so [OH−] = 2 × 0.0030 = 0.0060 M = 6.0 × 10−3 M.

\( \text{pOH} = -\log(6.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.22 \qquad \text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.22 = 11.78 \)

\( [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{6.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 1.7 \times 10^{-12}\ M \)

Check. Basic, so pH > 7. Two significant figures give two decimal places; −log(1.67 × 10−12) = 11.78 agrees.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Comparing two blood samples

Problem. Arterial sample A has pH 7.45 and sample B has pH 7.35, the two ends of the normal range. Which has the higher [H3O+], and by what factor?

Solution. A: 10−7.45 = 3.5 × 10−8 M. B: 10−7.35 = 4.5 × 10−8 M. Sample B (lower pH) has more H3O+, by a factor of 100.10 = 1.26, about 1.3.

Check. Lower pH means more acidic. Even the whole normal range is only about a 1.3-fold change in [H3O+], which shows how tightly the body controls blood pH.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling HF a strong acid, or calling alcohols (C2H5OH) bases.
  • Pairing the two reactants instead of each reactant with its product partner.
  • Pressing ln instead of log, or dropping the minus sign in pH = −log[H3O+].
  • Wrong decimal places in pH; forgetting the factor of 2 for Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, Ba(OH)2.
  • Reporting pOH as pH for a base; thinking a pH difference of 1 is a factor of 2 instead of 10.
  • Making a "buffer" from a strong acid and its salt.
