Learning Objectives

  • Define a buffer and explain why it resists changes in pH.
  • Decide whether a mixture is a buffer: a weak acid with its conjugate base, or a weak base with its conjugate acid, and pick the best buffer from a list by process of elimination.
  • Write the reactions by which each part of a buffer neutralizes added acid or added base.
  • Describe the blood's bicarbonate buffer and how breathing changes blood pH.

Your blood pH stays between 7.35 and 7.45 even though your cells make acid all day, you drink acidic sodas, and you take antacids. A drop of strong acid in pure water changes its pH dramatically; the same drop in blood barely moves it. The difference is a buffer.

What a Buffer Does

A buffer is a solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Compare pure water. Adding just 1.0 mL of 1.0 M HCl (0.0010 mol of a strong acid) to 1.0 L of pure water raises [H3O+] from 1.0 × 10−7 M to about 1.0 × 10−3 M, so the pH falls from 7.00 to 3.00, a ten-thousandfold change in [H3O+]. Added instead to a liter of an acetate buffer that is 0.10 M in each partner, the same acid changes the pH by only about 0.01 unit.

As a thought experiment (the CHEM 3A lab uses red cabbage indicator, not a pH meter), picture a pH probe dipped in each of two beakers that both start at pH 7. Add the same small amount of acid to each: the probe in plain water drops to about 4, while the probe in the buffer reads about 6.9. Add the same small amount of base instead: water jumps to about 10, and the buffer reads about 7.1. The buffer does not stop the change; it shrinks it to almost nothing.

What a Buffer Is Made Of

A buffer contains two partners in roughly similar amounts:

  • a weak acid and its conjugate base (Section 12.2), or
  • a weak base and its conjugate acid.

The conjugate partner is usually added as a salt. For example, an acetate buffer is made from acetic acid, HC2H3O2, and sodium acetate, NaC2H3O2, which dissolves to give the acetate ion, C2H3O2− (Na+ is just a spectator). An ammonia buffer is made from NH3 and NH4Cl.

Why must the acid be weak? A strong acid such as HCl is completely ionized, and its "conjugate base" Cl− is too weak a base to take a proton back. So HCl with NaCl cannot neutralize added acid, and it is not a buffer.

How a Buffer Works

Each partner handles one kind of threat. Using HA for the weak acid and A− for its conjugate base:

  • Added acid is neutralized by the conjugate base: A− + H3O+ → HA + H2O. A strong acid is replaced by a weak one.
  • Added base is neutralized by the weak acid: HA + OH− → A− + H2O. A strong base is replaced by a weak one.

Either way, the H3O+ or OH− that would have changed the pH is used up, and what forms in its place is a weak acid or a weak base that ionizes only slightly. The pH shifts a little, not a lot. You can also picture the buffer pair as a reaction that can run either way: add base and it shifts to make more A−; add acid and it shifts back to make more HA. Chapter 13 gives this kind of response a name, Le Chatelier's principle.

Each buffer pair holds the pH near its own value. An acetate buffer works best around pH 4.7, and the H2PO4−/HPO42− pair inside cells works near pH 7. In blood, the bicarbonate system holds the pH at about 7.4 because the lungs and kidneys keep adjusting both partners (below). This matters because enzymes and other proteins work only within a narrow pH range; outside it, they change shape and stop working.

A buffer is not unlimited. The amount of acid or base it can absorb before the pH changes sharply is its buffer capacity. Add more acid than there is A− to react with it (or more base than there is HA), and the buffer is "used up"; after that, the pH changes as if the buffer were not there. More concentrated buffers have larger capacities.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Buffer or not?

Problem. Which of these mixtures are buffers? (a) HF and NaF; (b) HNO3 and NaNO3; (c) NH3 and NH4Cl; (d) HC2H3O2 and NaCl; (e) KH2PO4 and K2HPO4.

Solution.

  • (a) Buffer: HF is a weak acid, and NaF supplies F−, its conjugate base.
  • (b) Not a buffer: HNO3 is a strong acid.
  • (c) Buffer: NH3 is a weak base, and NH4Cl supplies NH4+, its conjugate acid.
  • (d) Not a buffer: acetic acid is weak, but Cl− is not its conjugate base (acetate is).
  • (e) Buffer: H2PO4− (a weak acid) and HPO42− (its conjugate base) differ by one H+. This phosphate pair buffers the fluid inside your cells.

Check. For each "yes," the two species differ by exactly one H+, and neither is a strong acid or base.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Writing the neutralizing reactions

Problem. An acetate buffer contains HC2H3O2 and C2H3O2−. Write the reaction that occurs when (a) a little HCl is added and (b) a little NaOH is added.

Solution. (a) The base partner takes the proton:

C2H3O2−(aq) + H3O+(aq) → HC2H3O2(aq) + H2O(l)

(b) The acid partner gives up a proton:

HC2H3O2(aq) + OH−(aq) → C2H3O2−(aq) + H2O(l)

Check. Each product is a member of the buffer pair plus water; nothing new and strong is left behind. Adding acid makes more of the weak acid; adding base makes more of the conjugate base.

Professor Tip

To spot a buffer, ask two questions. (1) Is there a weak acid (or weak base)? If either substance is on the strong list, stop: no buffer. (2) Is its conjugate partner there too, the same formula plus or minus one H+, often as a sodium or potassium salt? Ignore spectator ions such as Na+, K+, and Cl− when you compare formulas.

Picking the Best Buffer: Process of Elimination

Multiple-choice questions often list several pairs and ask which would make the best buffer if mixed in roughly equal amounts. Cross out the wrong ones until one is left:

  1. Cross out any pair with a strong acid or a strong base (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4; NaOH, KOH, and the other strong bases). Their conjugates are negligible, so they cannot neutralize anything.
  2. Cross out pairs that do not differ by exactly one H+, ignoring spectator ions. HCN and HI share nothing but H; H3PO4 and Na2HPO4 differ by two H+.
  3. Cross out pairs with no weak acid (or weak base): two salts of the same anion, such as NaF with KF, or a lone salt such as NaCl.
  4. What is left is a weak acid with a salt of its own conjugate base, such as H2CO3 with NaHCO3 or H3PO4 with KH2PO4 (or a weak base with a salt of its conjugate acid).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Which pair makes a buffer?

Problem. Which pair would make a buffer if mixed in roughly equal amounts? A. HI and NaI; B. HC2H3O2 and NaNO3; C. HNO2 and KNO2; D. KOH and KCl; E. NaF and KF.

Solution. Eliminate step by step:

  • A: HI is a strong acid. Crossed out (step 1).
  • D: KOH is a strong base. Crossed out (step 1).
  • B: acetic acid is weak, but NO3− is not acetate; HC2H3O2 and NO3− do not differ by one H+. Crossed out (step 2).
  • E: F− from both salts, but no HF, the weak acid. Crossed out (step 3).
  • C: HNO2 is a weak acid (it is not HNO3), and KNO2 supplies NO2−, its conjugate base. Buffer.

Check. HNO2 and NO2− differ by exactly one H+, and neither is on the strong list.

The Bicarbonate Buffer in Blood

The most important buffer in your blood is carbonic acid, H2CO3 (a weak acid), with its conjugate base, bicarbonate, HCO3−. Carbonic acid forms when carbon dioxide dissolves in the water of blood plasma, so the whole system links CO2 to H+:

CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq) ⇄ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)

The buffer works in both directions:

  • Acid enters the blood (for example, lactic acid from hard-working muscles): HCO3− + H+ → H2CO3, which breaks down to CO2 + H2O. The extra CO2 is exhaled.
  • Base enters the blood: H2CO3 + OH− → HCO3− + H2O.

What makes this buffer special is that the body can adjust both partners. The lungs control CO2, and so H2CO3, within minutes by changing how fast and deeply you breathe. The kidneys control HCO3− more slowly, over hours to days, by keeping or excreting bicarbonate and excreting acid in urine. Reading the equation from left to right shows how breathing changes pH:

  • Too little breathing (as in Mateo's severe asthma attack, Section 12.5): CO2 builds up, more H2CO3 forms, more H+ is released, and the pH falls. This is respiratory acidosis; his pH of 7.30 is the result.
  • Too much breathing (hyperventilation, for example during a panic attack): CO2 is blown off, the reactions run backward, H+ is used up, and the pH rises: respiratory alkalosis.

Why an equilibrium "runs backward" when something is removed is explained by Le Chatelier's principle in Chapter 13. At the CHEM 3A level, the key idea is the chain: more CO2, more acid; less CO2, less acid.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): The blood buffer at work

Problem. (a) Write the reaction by which the blood buffer neutralizes the H3O+ released by lactic acid. (b) Explain, using the CO2 equation, why someone whose breathing slows during an opioid overdose develops acidosis.

Solution. (a) The base partner, bicarbonate, takes the proton:

HCO3−(aq) + H3O+(aq) → H2CO3(aq) + H2O(l)

(b) Slow, shallow breathing does not remove CO2 as fast as cells make it. CO2 accumulates in the blood, more of it forms H2CO3, and the H2CO3 releases H+. [H3O+] rises, so the pH falls below 7.35.

Check. In (a) the two species that change, HCO3− and H2CO3, differ by one H+: a conjugate pair, as a buffer should be.

Biology Connection: Lactic Acid and Exercise

During a sprint or a heavy lift, muscle cells break down glucose faster than oxygen can arrive, and they make lactic acid (HC3H5O3, a weak acid). The H+ it releases is buffered, partly inside the muscle cells by phosphate and proteins, and partly in the blood by bicarbonate. The bicarbonate reaction makes extra CO2, which is one reason you breathe so hard after intense exercise: your lungs are clearing the CO2 and, with it, the acid. When acid is made faster than the buffers and lungs can handle it, the muscle becomes more acidic, which contributes to the burning feeling and fatigue during hard effort. (The soreness you feel a day or two later is thought to come mainly from small injuries to muscle fibers, not from leftover lactic acid.)

Biology Connection: Microbiology of Tooth Decay

Dental plaque is a biofilm, a sticky community of bacteria on the tooth surface. Some of these bacteria, especially Streptococcus mutans, ferment sugars from food and drink and release lactic acid. Within minutes of a sugary snack, the pH in plaque can fall below the enamel's critical pH of about 5.5 (Section 12.5), and minerals begin to dissolve from the tooth. Saliva is the mouth's buffer: it carries bicarbonate (and phosphate) that neutralize the acid, and over roughly half an hour to an hour the plaque pH climbs back and enamel can remineralize. Frequent snacking or sipping sugary drinks restarts the acid attack before saliva finishes buffering, which is why dental caries (cavities) depend more on how often sugar is eaten than on how much. People with xerostomia (dry mouth) have less saliva and less buffering, and are at higher risk.

Common Mistakes

  • Using a strong acid (HCl with NaCl, HNO3 with KNO3) as a buffer: the acid must be weak.
  • Pairing a weak acid with the wrong salt: the salt must contain that acid's own conjugate base.
  • Accepting a pair that differs by two H+ (H3PO4 with HPO42−) or a pair of two salts with no weak acid.
  • Thinking a buffer keeps pH perfectly constant: it limits the change, and only until its capacity is used up.
  • Mixing up which partner reacts: the conjugate base neutralizes added acid; the weak acid neutralizes added base.
  • Getting the CO2 direction backward: more CO2 in the blood means more acid and a lower pH.

Summary

  • A buffer resists pH change; it contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid). To pick one from a list, cross out strong acids and bases, pairs that do not differ by one H+, and pairs with no weak acid.
  • The conjugate base neutralizes added acid; the weak acid neutralizes added base. Buffer capacity is limited.
  • Blood: CO2 + H2O ⇄ H2CO3 ⇄ H+ + HCO3−. The lungs adjust CO2; the kidneys adjust HCO3−.
  • CO2 buildup (hypoventilation) causes respiratory acidosis; blowing off CO2 (hyperventilation) causes respiratory alkalosis.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which mixture is a buffer: (a) HBr and KBr; (b) HNO2 and NaNO2; (c) NaOH and NaCl? Explain.

Answer

Only (b): HNO2 is a weak acid, and NaNO2 supplies its conjugate base, NO2−. (a) HBr is a strong acid. (c) NaOH is a strong base, and Cl− is not its conjugate acid.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

An ammonia buffer contains NH3 and NH4+. Write the reaction that neutralizes added H3O+ and the reaction that neutralizes added OH−.

Answer

Added acid: NH3 + H3O+ → NH4+ + H2O (the weak base reacts). Added base: NH4+ + OH− → NH3 + H2O (the conjugate acid reacts).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A patient who is very anxious breathes rapidly for several minutes. Predict what happens to blood CO2, [H3O+], and pH, and name the condition.

Answer

Rapid breathing removes CO2, so less H2CO3 forms and less H+ is released: [H3O+] falls and the pH rises above 7.45. This is respiratory alkalosis.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Which pair would make the best buffer: (a) HBr and NaBr; (b) H3PO4 and NaH2PO4; (c) NaCl and KCl; (d) HC2H3O2 and HCl? Give the reason each other pair fails.

Answer

(b): H3PO4 is a weak acid, and NaH2PO4 supplies H2PO4−, one H+ less. (a) HBr is a strong acid. (c) Two salts and no weak acid. (d) HCl is a strong acid, and it is not acetic acid's conjugate base; there is no acetate.
