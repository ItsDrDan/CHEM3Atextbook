Learning Objectives

  • Identify acids, bases, and salts from their chemical formulas and from the functional groups in a structure (–COOH acid, amine base).
  • Classify acids and bases as strong or weak using the strong-acid and strong-base lists on the green sheet.
  • Write ionization equations, using a single arrow for strong acids and bases and a double arrow for weak ones.
  • Explain that acids give up protons one at a time (monoprotic and polyprotic acids), and relate the strength of an acid to the strength of its conjugate base.
  • Predict the salt formed in a neutralization reaction.

A chemical label in a hospital storeroom, a fertilizer bag, or an exam question usually gives you a formula, not a description. With a few patterns you can read a formula and say whether the substance is an acid, a base, a salt, or none of these, and whether it ionizes completely or only a little in water.

Identifying Acids, Bases, and Salts from Formulas

Acids. In the formulas of most acids the acidic hydrogen is written first. There are three common kinds (naming them was Section 4.5):

  • Binary acids: H with a nonmetal, such as HCl, HBr, HF.
  • Oxyacids: H with a polyatomic anion that contains oxygen, such as HNO3, H2SO4, H3PO4, HClO4.
  • Organic (carboxylic) acids: carbon compounds with a –COOH group, such as acetic acid, written HC2H3O2 or CH3COOH. Only the H of the –COOH group is acidic. Citric acid in fruit and lactic acid in muscle are also carboxylic acids.

Bases. Three common kinds:

  • Metal hydroxides: ionic compounds that contain OH−, such as NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)2, Mg(OH)2.
  • Carbonates and bicarbonates: ionic compounds that contain CO32− or HCO3−, such as Na2CO3, NaHCO3 (baking soda), CaCO3. These anions accept protons.
  • Ammonia and amines: NH3 and organic molecules with an –NH2 (or –NH–) group, an amine. The lone pair on nitrogen accepts a proton. Many medicines, such as pseudoephedrine, are amines.

Salts. A salt is an ionic compound whose cation comes from a base and whose anion comes from an acid: NaCl, KNO3, CaSO4, NH4Cl. Salts form in neutralization reactions. (Carbonate and bicarbonate salts are both salts and bases.)

Neither. Molecular compounds with no acidic H, no OH− ion, and no amine group, such as CH4, glucose (C6H12O6), or ethanol (C2H5OH), are neither acids nor bases. The –OH in ethanol is covalently bonded; it is not a hydroxide ion.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Acid, base, salt, or neither?

Problem. Classify each: (a) HBr; (b) LiOH; (c) KBr; (d) H3PO4; (e) CH3OH (methanol); (f) K2CO3; (g) CH3CH2NH2.

Solution. (a) acid (binary). (b) base (metal hydroxide). (c) salt (K+ from a base, Br− from an acid). (d) acid (oxyacid). (e) neither: a molecular alcohol; its OH is not a hydroxide ion. (f) salt and base: carbonate accepts protons. (g) base: an amine.

Check. Ionic compounds with OH− or CO32− are bases; a metal or NH4+ with any other anion is a salt; H written first means acid.

Medicines and flavorings are usually shown as structures, not neat formulas with the H written first. Then look for the functional group: a –COOH (carboxylic acid) group means an acid, and an amine nitrogen (–NH2, –NH–) means a base. An –OH bonded to carbon (an alcohol) is neither, and so is the –COO– of an ester, where the O that would have held the acidic H is bonded to carbon instead.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Acid or base from a structure

Problem. Would each molecule act as an acid, a base, or neither in water? (a) Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), whose structure has a benzene ring carrying one –COOH group and one ester group. (b) Isoamyl acetate, a compound that gives bananas their smell; its only oxygen-containing group is an ester, CH3–COO–CH2CH2CH(CH3)2. (c) Pseudoephedrine, a decongestant, whose structure has an –OH on a carbon and an –NH–CH3 (amine) group.

Solution. (a) Acid: the H of the –COOH group is acidic. The ester does not change that. (b) Neither: an ester has no acidic H (no –COOH) and no amine nitrogen; its H atoms are all bonded to carbon. (c) Base: the lone pair on the amine nitrogen accepts a proton. Its –OH is an alcohol group, not a hydroxide ion.

Check. Ask two questions of any structure: is there an O–H on a C=O carbon (–COOH)? Is there a nitrogen with a lone pair (an amine)? If neither, it is probably neither an acid nor a base.

Strong and Weak Acids

A strong acid ionizes completely in water: every molecule donates its proton, so the solution contains ions and essentially no intact acid molecules. A weak acid ionizes only slightly: most molecules stay intact, and only a small fraction donate a proton at any moment. Strong acids are strong electrolytes; weak acids are weak electrolytes (Section 7.4).

Only six common acids are strong. They are listed on the green sheet, and they are worth memorizing:

Any acid not on this list is weak, including HF, acetic acid, citric acid, lactic acid, H3PO4, and carbonic acid (H2CO3). For sulfuric acid, only the first proton comes off completely; that detail does not change any CHEM 3A problem, which use the other strong acids for pH calculations.

One Proton at a Time

An acid with one acidic hydrogen is monoprotic (mono = one, protic = proton): HCl, HBr, HNO3, and acetic acid (only the H of its –COOH group) each give up one H+. An acid with more than one acidic hydrogen, such as H2CO3 or H3PO4, is polyprotic, and it gives them up one at a time, each step a separate reaction with its own conjugate base:

H2CO3 + H2O ⇄ H3O+ + HCO3−
HCO3− + H2O ⇄ H3O+ + CO32−

Each step is weaker than the one before, because pulling a positive proton away from an ion that is already negative is harder. Sulfuric acid follows the same pattern: its first step is complete (H2SO4 + H2O → H3O+ + HSO4−), and the HSO4− left behind acts as a weak monoprotic acid:

HSO4− + H2O ⇄ SO42− + H3O+

Strong Acid, Weak Conjugate Base

Strengths come in matched pairs: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. A strong acid gives its proton away completely and never takes it back, so its conjugate base has essentially no pull on protons. Chemists call such bases negligible. A weak acid holds its proton more tightly, so its conjugate base is a weak base that can take a proton back.

This is why HCl and NaCl cannot make a buffer (Section 12.6): Cl− is too weak a base to take a proton back. A weak acid and its weak conjugate base can each react, which is exactly what a buffer needs.

Strong and Weak Bases

The strong bases are the soluble metal hydroxides: the Group 1 hydroxides and the hydroxides of calcium, strontium, and barium. They dissociate completely into metal ions and OH−. (Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, and Ba(OH)2 do not dissolve in large amounts, but the part that dissolves is fully dissociated.) Weak bases, such as ammonia, amines, and bicarbonate, accept protons from water only to a small extent, so they make relatively little OH−.

Writing Ionization Equations: One Arrow or Two

The arrow tells the reader how far the reaction goes:

  • A single arrow (→) means the reaction goes essentially to completion. Use it for strong acids and strong bases.
  • A double arrow (⇄) means the forward and reverse reactions both happen, and most of the substance stays unreacted. Use it for weak acids and weak bases. (Chapter 13 explains this balance, called equilibrium.)

HCl(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + Cl−(aq)     (strong acid)

HF(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + F−(aq)     (weak acid)

Ba(OH)2(aq) → Ba2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq)     (strong base)

NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq)     (weak base)

Notice that one formula unit of Ba(OH)2 gives two OH− ions. You will need that 1 : 2 ratio for pH calculations in Section 12.5.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Strong or weak, and the equation

Problem. Classify each as a strong or weak acid or base, and write its ionization (or dissociation) equation in water: (a) HNO3; (b) HC2H3O2; (c) KOH.

Solution.

(a) strong acid: HNO3(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + NO3−(aq)

(b) weak acid: HC2H3O2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + C2H3O2−(aq)

(c) strong base: KOH(aq) → K+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Check. HNO3 is on the strong-acid list; acetic acid is not, so it gets a double arrow; KOH is a Group 1 hydroxide. Mass and charge balance in each equation.

Strength Is Not Concentration

"Strong" and "concentrated" mean different things. Strength is about what fraction of the acid ionizes; it is a property of the substance. Concentration is how much acid is dissolved per liter (Chapter 11). A 0.0010 M HCl solution is dilute but still a strong acid: every HCl molecule is ionized. A 6 M acetic acid solution is concentrated but still a weak acid: only a small fraction of its molecules are ionized.

Professor Tip

Do not try to memorize weak acids; there are thousands. Memorize the six strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4) and the strong bases (Group 1 hydroxides plus Ca, Sr, and Ba hydroxides). If an acid or base is not on those lists, call it weak and give it a double arrow. HF is the classic trap: it is a halogen acid, but it is weak.

Salts from Neutralization

When an acid neutralizes a base, the base's cation and the acid's anion become the salt. Write the salt's formula with charges that balance (Section 4.2), then balance the equation so that every H+ meets an OH−. Neutralization is a double replacement, so with the acid written first, the "inside" ions (the base's cation and the acid's anion) form the salt and the "outside" ions (H and OH) form water. A balancing trick: write the water as HOH until the equation is balanced. Written that way, the OH group is easy to count against the hydroxides in the base; balance the polyatomic ions first, zigzag to their partners, then rewrite HOH as H2O.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Predicting the salt

Problem. Predict the salt and write a balanced equation: (a) HBr + Sr(OH)2; (b) H3PO4 + NaOH (all three protons react).

Solution. (a) Sr2+ and Br− give SrBr2; two OH− need two HBr.

2 HBr(aq) + Sr(OH)2(aq) → SrBr2(aq) + 2 H2O(l)

(b) Na+ and PO43− give Na3PO4; three H+ need three NaOH.

H3PO4(aq) + 3 NaOH(aq) → Na3PO4(aq) + 3 H2O(l)

Check. The number of waters equals the number of H+ neutralized: 2 in (a), 3 in (b).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Stomach acid is mostly hydrochloric acid, a strong acid secreted by parietal cells in the stomach lining. It is fully ionized, which is why gastric fluid is so acidic (typically about pH 1.5 to 3.5; Section 12.5). The acid helps digest proteins and kills many swallowed microbes. Over-the-counter antacids are bases that neutralize some of that acid: magnesium hydroxide and aluminum hydroxide are weak, poorly soluble metal hydroxides, and calcium carbonate and sodium bicarbonate are basic salts. Each forms a salt and water (and CO2 for the carbonates). Because they only neutralize acid that is already there, antacids act quickly but briefly; other medicines, such as proton pump inhibitors, instead reduce how much acid the parietal cells make. Nurses ask about antacid use because these bases can change how some other oral medicines are absorbed. (Context only; follow the provider's orders and facility policy.)

Major Connection: Chemistry

In a titration lab, the titrant in the buret is almost always a strong base (NaOH) or a strong acid (HCl), because it reacts completely and predictably. The unknown can be strong or weak. When you titrate the citric acid in soda (Experiment 12b), the weak acid still reacts completely with NaOH; it is the strong titrant that pulls every proton off. Analytical and quality-control chemists in food, water, and pharmaceutical labs run titrations like this every day.

Explore It: Particle Beaker

A beaker holds one substance dissolved in water. Test it with a light bulb and look at the particles: a strong acid or base is all ions and lights the bulb brightly, a weak one is mostly whole molecules and glows dimly, and a nonelectrolyte leaves the bulb off. Then decide which substance it is.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling HF strong because HCl, HBr, and HI are: HF is weak.
  • Calling any compound with "OH" a base: alcohols such as ethanol and methanol are molecular and do not release OH−.
  • Confusing strong with concentrated: a dilute strong acid is still fully ionized.
  • Using a single arrow for a weak acid, or writing a weak acid as if it were all ions.
  • Forgetting the 2 in front of OH− when Ca(OH)2, Sr(OH)2, or Ba(OH)2 dissociates.
  • Pulling all the protons off a polyprotic acid at once: they come off one step at a time, and each conjugate base is one H+ away from its acid.
  • Calling an ester an acid: only a –COOH group has an acidic H.

Summary

  • Acids: H written first (binary, oxyacids) or a –COOH group. Bases: metal hydroxides, carbonates and bicarbonates, ammonia and amines. Salts: a base's cation with an acid's anion.
  • Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4. Strong bases: Group 1 hydroxides and Ca, Sr, Ba hydroxides. All others are weak.
  • Strong: single arrow, all ions. Weak: double arrow, mostly molecules.
  • Monoprotic acids give one H+; polyprotic acids give theirs one at a time. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base (Cl− and NO3− are negligible bases).
  • Acid + base → salt + water; the number of waters equals the number of H+ neutralized.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify each as a strong acid, weak acid, strong base, weak base, salt, or none of these: (a) HI; (b) HNO2; (c) NH3; (d) Ba(OH)2; (e) NaNO3; (f) C6H12O6.

Hint

Check the green-sheet strong lists first. Then ask: H written first (acid)? Metal hydroxide or amine (base)? Metal with another anion (salt)? None of these (neither)?

Answer

(a) strong acid (on the list); (b) weak acid (not on the list; note HNO2 is not HNO3); (c) weak base; (d) strong base; (e) salt; (f) none (glucose, a molecular compound).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the ionization equation for lactic acid, HC3H5O3, in water. Which arrow do you use, and why?

Hint

The acid gives one H+ to water to make H3O+. Is lactic acid on the six-acid strong list? That decides single or double arrow.

Answer

HC3H5O3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + C3H5O3−(aq). A double arrow, because lactic acid is not on the strong-acid list, so it is weak and only slightly ionized.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Write a balanced neutralization equation for HClO4 with Ca(OH)2, and name the salt.

Hint

Ca2+ pairs with two ClO4−, so count the HClO4 and waters needed. Name the salt: cation first, then the polyatomic anion from the green sheet.

Answer

2 HClO4(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → Ca(ClO4)2(aq) + 2 H2O(l). The salt is calcium perchlorate.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Ibuprofen's structure has a –COOH group; ethyl acetate (a nail-polish remover solvent) is an ester, CH3–COO–CH2CH3; amphetamine has an –NH2 group. Classify each as an acid, a base, or neither.

Hint

Look for the functional group: –COOH means acid, an amine nitrogen (–NH2) means base, and an ester (–COO– between carbons) has neither.

Answer

Ibuprofen: acid (–COOH). Ethyl acetate: neither (an ester has no acidic H and no amine). Amphetamine: base (amine nitrogen accepts a proton).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Which is the stronger base, Br− or F−? Explain using the strengths of their conjugate acids.

Hint

Write each ion's conjugate acid (add H+) and check the strong-acid list. The weaker the conjugate acid, the stronger the base.

Answer

F−. Its conjugate acid, HF, is weak, so F− is a weak base. Br− comes from HBr, a strong acid, so Br− is a negligible base.
