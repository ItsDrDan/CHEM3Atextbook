Learning Objectives

  • Define acids and bases using the Arrhenius definition and the Brønsted–Lowry definition.
  • Explain why H+ is called a proton and how it becomes the hydronium ion, H3O+.
  • Identify the Brønsted–Lowry acid, base, conjugate acid, and conjugate base in a reaction, and check the labels by reading the reverse reaction.
  • Write the formula of the conjugate acid or conjugate base of a given species, and explain why water is amphoteric.

The properties in Section 12.1 tell you whether something is an acid or a base. The definitions in this section tell you why. Both come down to one tiny particle, the hydrogen ion, and where it goes.

The Arrhenius Definition

In the 1880s the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius noticed that acids and bases both make water conduct electricity, and he explained it with ions:

  • An Arrhenius acid produces hydrogen ions, H+, when it dissolves in water.
  • An Arrhenius base produces hydroxide ions, OH−, when it dissolves in water.

HCl(aq) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq)     NaOH(aq) → Na+(aq) + OH−(aq)

This definition explains neutralization neatly (H+ + OH− → H2O), but it has a gap. Ammonia, NH3, is clearly a base: it turns red litmus blue and neutralizes acids. Yet it contains no hydroxide to release.

H+ Is a Proton, and It Rides on Water

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron. Remove the electron and only the proton is left, so H+ is simply a proton, and chemists use the two words interchangeably. A bare proton is far too reactive to float alone in water. It immediately attaches to a lone pair on the oxygen of a water molecule, forming the hydronium ion, H3O+:

H+ + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq)

Lewis structures (Chapter 6) show what moves. When HCl gives its proton to water, the H+ lands on one of the two lone pairs on water's oxygen, which becomes the new O–H bond in H3O+. The electron pair that used to hold H to Cl stays behind on chlorine, so chlorine leaves with one extra electron as Cl−. Only the proton moves; no electrons go with it.

So "H+(aq)" and "H3O+(aq)" mean the same thing in a solution. This book writes H3O+ when water's role matters and H+ as a shorthand elsewhere; [H+] and [H3O+] are the same concentration.

The Brønsted–Lowry Definition

In 1923 Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry each proposed a broader definition built on the transfer of a proton:

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor: it gives away an H+.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor: it takes an H+.

Now ammonia fits. It accepts a proton from water, and the water left behind is a hydroxide ion:

NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq)

And an acid dissolving in water is a proton transfer too, with water as the base:

HNO3(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + NO3−(aq)

Every Arrhenius acid and base is also a Brønsted–Lowry acid or base; the Brønsted–Lowry definition simply covers more substances. The double arrow (⇄) in the ammonia equation means the reaction runs in both directions at once; Section 12.3 explains when to use it.

Professor Tip

Remember "BAD": Bases Accept, Acids Donate. To find the acid in a reaction, look for the reactant that loses an H from left to right; to find the base, look for the one that gains an H. Having hydrogen atoms in a formula does not make something an acid: NH3 has three H atoms and is a base.

Conjugate Acid–Base Pairs

Many acid–base reactions can also run backward. In the reverse direction, something on the product side donates a proton and something accepts it, so the products are an acid and a base too. These are called the conjugate acid and the conjugate base.

  • When an acid donates its proton, what is left is its conjugate base.
  • When a base accepts a proton, it becomes its conjugate acid.
  • A conjugate acid–base pair is two species that differ by exactly one H+. Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two pairs.

To find the pairs:

  1. Find the acid and base on the left: who donates, who accepts.
  2. Find each one's partner on the right by looking for the common piece, the species that is identical except for one H+. HF and F− share the "F"; H2O and H3O+ share the "water."
  3. Check that each pair differs by exactly one H and one unit of charge. Removing H+ lowers the charge by 1 (HF → F−); adding H+ raises it by 1 (H2O → H3O+).
  4. Read the reverse reaction to check. The conjugate acid should be able to hand its H+ back to the conjugate base and re-form the two reactants. In Figure 1, H3O+ gives its proton to F−, making H2O and HF again, so the labels are right.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Labeling acid, base, and conjugates

Problem. Identify the acid, base, conjugate acid, and conjugate base in each reaction:

(a) HBr(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + Br−(aq)

(b) CH3NH2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ CH3NH3+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Solution. (a) HBr loses H+: acid; its partner Br− is the conjugate base. H2O gains H+: base; H3O+ is its conjugate acid. (b) CH3NH2 (methylamine, a relative of ammonia) gains H+: base; CH3NH3+ is its conjugate acid. Here water loses H+: acid; OH− is its conjugate base.

Check. Each pair differs by one H and one charge: HBr/Br−, H3O+/H2O, CH3NH3+/CH3NH2, H2O/OH−. Read (b) backward: CH3NH3+ (the conjugate acid) gives its H+ to OH− (the conjugate base), re-forming CH3NH2 and H2O. The reverse reaction agrees with the labels.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Writing conjugates

Problem. Write (a) the conjugate base of HNO2; (b) the conjugate acid of F−; (c) the conjugate acid of HPO42−; (d) the conjugate base of HPO42−.

Solution. To make a conjugate base, remove one H+ (one H, charge goes down by 1). To make a conjugate acid, add one H+ (one H, charge goes up by 1).

(a) NO2−    (b) HF    (c) H2PO4−    (d) PO43−

Check. A conjugate base always has one fewer H and a charge one lower; a conjugate acid has one more H and a charge one higher. HPO42− has both a conjugate acid and a conjugate base because it can either gain or lose a proton.

Species with No H to Give, and the Proton Ladder

What is the conjugate acid of nitrate, NO3−? It has no hydrogen, so it cannot be an acid, but it can be a base. If you are unsure, react it with water on paper and let it accept a proton:

NO3− + H2O ⇄ HNO3 + OH−

The species that gained the H+ is the conjugate acid: HNO3. (In a real beaker nitrate barely does this, because HNO3 is a strong acid that gives its proton away; Section 12.3 explains why. Writing it out is just a way to find the formula.)

Species that can both gain and lose protons sit on a proton ladder. Each rung differs from the next by one H+ and one unit of charge, so you can step up (add H+, conjugate acid) or down (remove H+, conjugate base):

H3PO4  ↔  H2PO4−  ↔  HPO42−  ↔  PO43−

From HPO42−, one step up gives H2PO4−, and a second step gives phosphoric acid, H3PO4; one step down gives phosphate, PO43−. A conjugate is always just one step away.

Amphoteric Substances: Water Plays Both Roles

Look again at the equations above. With HBr and HNO3, water accepts a proton and acts as a base. With NH3 and CH3NH2, water donates a proton and acts as an acid. A substance that can act as either an acid or a base, depending on what it reacts with, is amphoteric. Which role water plays depends on its partner: if the other substance is the stronger acid, water is the base; if the other substance is the stronger base, water is the acid. Water is the most important amphoteric substance, and in Section 12.4 you will see it react with itself. Ions such as HCO3− (bicarbonate) and HPO42− are amphoteric too:

HCO3− + H2O ⇄ H2CO3 + OH−    (bicarbonate as a base)

HCO3− + H2O ⇄ CO32− + H3O+    (bicarbonate as an acid)

Bicarbonate's ability to go both ways is the heart of the blood buffer in Section 12.6.

Biology Connection: How H. pylori Survives Stomach Acid

The stomach is so acidic (Section 12.5) that very few microbes survive a trip through it. Helicobacter pylori, a spiral-shaped bacterium that infects the stomach lining of a large share of the world's people and can cause ulcers, is an exception. It makes large amounts of the enzyme urease, which breaks down urea (a waste product present in body fluids) into carbon dioxide and ammonia. Ammonia is a Brønsted–Lowry base: it accepts protons and becomes ammonium,

NH3 + H3O+ → NH4+ + H2O

so the bacterium wraps itself in a small, less acidic zone while it burrows into the protective mucus layer. The same urease activity is the basis of the "urea breath test" used to detect the infection.

Common Mistakes

  • Pairing the two reactants (acid with base) instead of pairing each reactant with its partner on the product side.
  • Changing the H count without changing the charge: the conjugate base of HSO4− is SO42−, not SO4−.
  • Calling anything with hydrogen an acid: NH3 and CH4 contain H but do not donate it in water.
  • Thinking water is always the base: it is the base toward acids and the acid toward bases.
  • Skipping the check: read the reverse reaction; the conjugate acid must be able to give its H+ back to the conjugate base.
  • Jumping two rungs: the conjugate base of H2PO4− is HPO42−, not PO43−.

Summary

  • Arrhenius: acids make H+, bases make OH− in water. Brønsted–Lowry: acids donate H+, bases accept H+.
  • H+ is a proton; in water it exists as hydronium, H3O+.
  • A conjugate acid–base pair differs by one H+; every proton-transfer reaction has two pairs. Find each pair by its common piece, and check by reading the reverse reaction.
  • Amphoteric species (water, HCO3−, HPO42−) can act as either an acid or a base.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Identify the acid, base, conjugate acid, and conjugate base: HC2H3O2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + C2H3O2−(aq).

Answer

Acid: HC2H3O2 (acetic acid); conjugate base: C2H3O2− (acetate). Base: H2O; conjugate acid: H3O+.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the conjugate acid of each: (a) NH3; (b) OH−; (c) SO42−.

Answer

Add one H+ and raise the charge by 1: (a) NH4+; (b) H2O; (c) HSO4−.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

In the reaction HSO4− + H2O ⇄ SO42− + H3O+, is water acting as an acid or a base? Explain.

Answer

As a base: it accepts a proton from HSO4− and becomes H3O+.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Write (a) the conjugate acid of Cl−; (b) the conjugate acid and the conjugate base of H2PO4−.

Answer

(a) HCl: Cl− has no H to give, so add one (Cl− + H2O ⇄ HCl + OH− on paper; it barely happens). (b) One rung up the ladder, H3PO4; one rung down, HPO42−.
