Learning Objectives

  • Calculate pH and pOH from [H3O+] and [OH−], using the correct number of decimal places.
  • Calculate [H3O+] and [OH−] from pH or pOH with the 10x function.
  • Use pH + pOH = 14.00 to move between the two scales, and find the pH of strong acid and strong base solutions.
  • Classify solutions as acidic, basic, or neutral by pH, and compare acidity across pH units.
  • Solve multi-step problems: the volume of acid or base needed for a neutralization, and the pH of a solution made from grams of a strong base.

Lemon juice has [H3O+] of about 0.0050 M; household ammonia, about 0.000000000003 M. Numbers that span that many zeros are hard to read and easy to miscopy. In 1909 the Danish chemist Søren Sørensen, working on beer brewing, proposed a shortcut that health care now uses every day: the pH scale. Instead of writing the concentration, you write (roughly) the size of its negative exponent.

The pH Scale

The pH of a solution is the negative logarithm (base 10) of its hydronium ion concentration:

\( \text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] \)

A logarithm answers the question "10 to what power gives this number?" For example, log(1000) = 3 because 103 = 1000, and log(0.001) = −3 because 10−3 = 0.001. So a solution with [H3O+] = 1 × 10−3 M has pH = −(−3) = 3. The minus sign in the definition turns the negative exponents of small concentrations into positive pH values.

Math Skill: What a Logarithm Really Asks

A base-10 log asks one question: how many 10s multiply together to make this number? For 1000: 10 × 10 = 100, and × 10 again = 1000. Three 10s, so log(1000) = 3. For a small number, count how many places the decimal point moves instead: 0.001 has its 1 in the third place after the decimal point, so it is 10−3, and log(0.001) = −3.

That makes pH easy to estimate before you touch a calculator. pH is the size of the negative exponent of [H3O+]: 1 × 10−4 M has pH 4; 1 × 10−9 M has pH 9. When the number in front is not 1, the pH falls between two whole numbers: 5.0 × 10−7 M sits between 10−7 and 10−6, so its pH is between 6 and 7 (it is 6.30).

At 25 °C, using the boundaries from Section 12.4:

  • pH < 7: acidic ([H3O+] > 1.0 × 10−7 M)
  • pH = 7.00: neutral
  • pH > 7: basic

Two features of the scale surprise many students. First, lower pH means more acidic, because a larger [H3O+] has a smaller negative exponent. Second, the scale is logarithmic: each step of one pH unit is a tenfold change in [H3O+]. A solution at pH 2 has 10 times the [H3O+] of one at pH 3; a solution at pH 10 has 1000 times less [H3O+] than one at pH 7 (10 × 10 × 10), so it is 1000 times more basic.

The table below shows why pH was invented: the concentrations are awkward to read and compare, and the pH values are not.

Significant Figures in pH

Logarithms have their own significant-figure rule: the number of decimal places in a pH equals the number of significant figures in the concentration. The digits before the decimal point come only from the power of 10, so they are not counted.

  • [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−7 M (2 significant figures) → pH = 7.00 (2 decimal places)
  • [H3O+] = 5.0 × 10−7 M (2 significant figures) → pH = 6.30
  • [H3O+] = 3.65 × 10−4 M (3 significant figures) → pH = 3.438

Warm up with four more, and predict the whole-number part first by counting the 10s: 1.00 × 10−4 M (3 significant figures) → pH = 4.000; 0.000001 M (1 significant figure) → pH = 6.0; 5.0 × 10−7 M → pH = 6.30; 6.0 × 10−12 M → pH = 11.22. Notice that 4.000 and 6.0 are the same kind of number: the zeros after the decimal point are there only to show how precisely the concentration was known.

The same rule works backward: a pH with two decimal places gives a concentration with two significant figures.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): pH of a strong acid

Problem. What is the pH of 3.5 × 10−4 M HNO3?

Solution. HNO3 is a strong acid, so [H3O+] = 3.5 × 10−4 M.

\( \text{pH} = -\log(3.5 \times 10^{-4}) = 3.4559\ldots = 3.46 \)

Check. Two significant figures in 3.5 give two decimal places. The concentration is between 10−4 and 10−3 M, so the pH must be between 3 and 4, and it is below 7 (acidic). Check by going back: 10−3.46 = 3.5 × 10−4 M, the concentration you started with.

pOH and the pH + pOH Relationship

The same "p" idea works for hydroxide: pOH = −log[OH−]. Taking −log of both sides of Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 gives a simple sum at 25 °C:

\( \text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00 \)

So if pH = 6.00, pOH = 8.00. The four quantities form a loop: from any one of them you can reach the other three, as Figure 2 shows.

For a strong base, the usual path is: find [OH−] from the formula (Section 12.4), take −log to get pOH, then subtract from 14.00 to get pH.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): pH of a strong base

Problem. What are the pOH and pH of 0.0025 M NaOH?

Solution. NaOH is a strong base with one OH−, so [OH−] = 0.0025 M = 2.5 × 10−3 M.

\( \text{pOH} = -\log(2.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.60 \qquad \text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.60 = 11.40 \)

Check. Two significant figures give two decimal places. A pH above 7 fits a base. Going the other way, [H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14 ÷ 2.5 × 10−3 = 4.0 × 10−12 M, and −log of that is also 11.40.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A base with two hydroxides

Problem. What is the pH of 0.015 M Ba(OH)2?

Solution. Ba(OH)2 → Ba2+ + 2 OH−, so [OH−] = 2 × 0.015 M = 0.030 M.

\( \text{pOH} = -\log(0.030) = 1.52 \qquad \text{pH} = 14.00 - 1.52 = 12.48 \)

Check. 0.030 has two significant figures (the leading zeros do not count), so two decimal places. Forgetting the factor of 2 would give pH 12.18, too low.

From pH Back to Concentration

To undo a logarithm, raise 10 to that power (the antilog, or inverse log):

\( [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = 10^{-\text{pH}} \qquad [\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-\text{pOH}} \)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): [H3O+] from pH

Problem. A sample of gastric fluid has pH 1.85. What are [H3O+] and [OH−]?

Solution.

\( [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = 10^{-1.85} = 0.01412\ldots = 1.4 \times 10^{-2}\ M \)

pOH = 14.00 − 1.85 = 12.15, so [OH−] = 10−12.15 = 7.1 × 10−13 M.

Check. Two decimal places in 1.85 give two significant figures. A pH between 1 and 2 means [H3O+] between 10−2 and 10−1 M, and 0.014 M is. Both answers are less than 1 M, as any concentration from an ordinary pH must be.

A Sanity Check for Antilogs

A concentration found from a pH must be a small number, and the higher the pH, the smaller [H3O+] must be. Watch what happens if you forget the minus sign for a solution at pH 12.240:

\( 10^{12.240} = 1.74 \times 10^{12}\ M \ (\text{impossible}) \qquad 10^{-12.240} = 5.75 \times 10^{-13}\ M \ (\text{correct}) \)

A trillion moles per liter is not possible in any solution, so a huge answer means you dropped the (−). The correct value is tiny, as it should be for a strongly basic solution, and it has three significant figures because 12.240 has three decimal places. If your calculator shows a long decimal such as 0.00000004 instead of scientific notation, count how many places the decimal point must move to get to the first nonzero digit (here 8) to write it as 4 × 10−8.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Mateo's blood gas

Problem. During his asthma attack, Mateo's arterial blood pH is 7.30. Find his [H3O+] and compare it with the [H3O+] at pH 7.40, the middle of the normal range.

Solution.

\( \text{pH } 7.30:\ [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = 10^{-7.30} = 5.0 \times 10^{-8}\ M \qquad \text{pH } 7.40:\ [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = 10^{-7.40} = 4.0 \times 10^{-8}\ M \)

\( \dfrac{5.0 \times 10^{-8}}{4.0 \times 10^{-8}} = 1.25 \)

His blood has about 1.25 times the normal [H3O+], roughly 25% more, from a pH drop of only 0.10.

Check. A drop of 0.10 pH unit multiplies [H3O+] by 100.10 = 1.26; the small difference from 1.25 comes from rounding the concentrations to two significant figures. A lower pH must mean more H3O+, and it does.

Putting It Together: Neutralization and pH Problems

Chapter 11's solution stoichiometry and this chapter's pH skills often meet in one problem. Watch for the buzzwords neutralize, titrate, mix, pH, and pOH, then pick the tool:

  • "What volume neutralizes…?" or "What is the molarity of…?" It is a mole problem: balance the neutralization (write water as HOH until it is balanced, polyatomic ions first), list the givens under the equation, start from the substance with both V and M (or the grams), and use the mole ratio (Section 11.5).
  • "What is the pH?" First find the molarity of H3O+ or OH− (Section 12.4), then use the loop in Figure 2. For a base, you can only get pOH first; subtract it from 14.00 at the end.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Volume of acid to neutralize a base

Problem. What volume of 0.350 M HNO3, in milliliters, neutralizes 25.0 mL of 0.150 M Ba(OH)2?

Solution. Inside–outside, with water written as HOH while balancing: Ba(OH)2 has two OH groups, so 2 HOH; that needs 2 HNO3, which gives the two nitrates in Ba(NO3)2.

2 HNO3(aq) + Ba(OH)2(aq) → Ba(NO3)2(aq) + 2 H2O(l)

Ba(OH)2 is the side with both V and M, so start there, with the volume:

\( 25.0\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{0.150\ \text{mol Ba(OH)}_2}{1000\ \text{mL}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol HNO}_3}{1\ \text{mol Ba(OH)}_2} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mL}}{0.350\ \text{mol HNO}_3} = 21.4\ \text{mL} \)

Check. Writing molarity as moles per 1000 mL lets the milliliters cancel directly. Each Ba(OH)2 needs two HNO3; without the 2 you would get 10.7 mL, half the right volume.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): From grams of a strong base to pH

Problem. 0.35 g of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2 (74.09 g/mol), is dissolved in enough water to make 2.0 L of solution. What is the pH?

Solution. Map out the process before calculating: g Ca(OH)2 → mol Ca(OH)2 → mol OH− → [OH−] → pOH → pH.

  1. Dissociation. Ca(OH)2(s) → Ca2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq), so 1 mol Ca(OH)2 = 2 mol OH−.
  2. Moles of OH−. \( 0.35\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Ca(OH)}_2}{74.09\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol OH}^-}{1\ \text{mol Ca(OH)}_2} = 9.448 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol OH}^- \)
  3. Molarity. [OH−] = 9.448 × 10−3 mol ÷ 2.0 L = 4.724 × 10−3 M, which is 4.7 × 10−3 M to two significant figures.
  4. pOH, then pH. pOH = −log(4.724 × 10−3) = 2.33; pH = 14.00 − 2.33 = 11.67.

Check. A base must give a pH above 7. Two significant figures in 0.35 g and 2.0 L give two decimal places. Moles are not molarity: stopping at step 2 and taking −log of 9.448 × 10−3 would give the wrong pH.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

An arterial blood gas (ABG) reports the pH of arterial blood along with PaCO2, the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (normally about 35 to 45 mmHg), and the bicarbonate level. Normal arterial pH is 7.35 to 7.45. Below 7.35 is acidosis (blood too acidic); above 7.45 is alkalosis (blood too basic). When the cause is breathing, the terms are respiratory acidosis or alkalosis: breathing too little lets CO2 build up (hypercapnia), which lowers pH (Section 12.6 shows why), and breathing too fast blows off CO2 and raises pH. Problems with metabolism or the kidneys cause metabolic acidosis or alkalosis. Because pH is logarithmic, small-looking numbers matter: at pH 7.25, [H3O+] = 10−7.25 = 5.6 × 10−8 M, about 1.4 times the 4.0 × 10−8 M at pH 7.40. Respiratory therapists read these values every shift and report them to the care team. (Context only; interpretation and treatment follow the provider's orders and facility protocols.)

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth enamel is made mostly of hydroxyapatite, a calcium phosphate mineral that contains hydroxide and phosphate ions, both of which react with acid. When the pH at the tooth surface falls below about 5.5, the critical pH, enamel begins to dissolve, a process called demineralization. When the pH rises again, saliva can return calcium and phosphate to the enamel (remineralization). Many soft drinks have a pH around 2.5 to 3, and sports drinks and fruit juices are often around 3 to 4. A cola at pH 2.5 has 103 = 1000 times the [H3O+] of a liquid at the critical pH of 5.5. Sipping such drinks slowly through the day keeps the tooth surface below the critical pH for long stretches, which is why dental hygienists ask patients how they drink as well as what they drink. Repeated acid exposure from drinks or stomach acid causes dental erosion; acid made by plaque bacteria causes dental caries (cavities; Section 12.6). (Context only; follow the dentist's recommendations.)

Common Mistakes

  • Using ln instead of log: check that the screen shows log( before you enter the number.
  • Dropping the minus sign: pH values of ordinary solutions are positive; a negative answer usually means a missing (−).
  • Forgetting the minus sign in 10−pH: a concentration such as 1012 M is impossible. Concentrations from a pH are small, and a high pH means a very small [H3O+].
  • Taking −log of moles instead of molarity in a grams-to-pH problem: divide by the liters of solution first.
  • Counting decimal places wrong: decimal places in pH = significant figures in the concentration (pH 3.46, not 3.5 or 3.456, for a two-figure concentration).
  • Reporting pOH as the pH for a base: subtract from 14.00 at the end.
  • Thinking pH 4 is "twice as acidic" as pH 8: it is 104 = 10,000 times higher in [H3O+].

Summary

  • pH = −log[H3O+]; pOH = −log[OH−]; pH + pOH = 14.00 at 25 °C.
  • A log counts how many 10s make a number; pH is the size of the negative exponent of [H3O+].
  • [H3O+] = 10−pH; [OH−] = 10−pOH. A concentration from a pH must be a small number.
  • Grams of a strong base → mol → mol OH− → [OH−] → pOH → pH.
  • pH < 7 acidic, 7.00 neutral, > 7 basic; each pH unit is a tenfold change in [H3O+].
  • Decimal places in pH = significant figures in the concentration.
  • Normal arterial blood pH is 7.35–7.45; below is acidosis, above is alkalosis.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Find the pH of 0.0063 M HCl.

Hint

HCl is strong, so [H3O+] equals its molarity. Take −log, and give as many decimal places as the concentration has significant figures.

Answer

HCl is strong, so [H3O+] = 6.3 × 10−3 M; pH = −log(6.3 × 10−3) = 2.2007… = 2.20 (two decimal places for two significant figures).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Find the pOH and pH of 6.0 × 10−4 M KOH.

Hint

KOH is strong with one OH−: take −log[OH−] for the pOH, then use pH + pOH = 14.00.

Answer

[OH−] = 6.0 × 10−4 M; pOH = 3.2218… = 3.22; pH = 14.00 − 3.22 = 10.78.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A rinse solution has pH 4.62. Find [H3O+] and classify it.

Hint

Undo the log with the 10x key: [H3O+] = 10−pH. Two decimal places in the pH give two significant figures. Then compare the pH with 7.

Answer

[H3O+] = 10−4.62 = 2.4 × 10−5 M (two significant figures from two decimal places). pH below 7: acidic.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A patient's arterial pH is 7.48. Is this acidosis, alkalosis, or normal? What is [H3O+]?

Hint

Compare the pH with the normal range, 7.35–7.45. Then use [H3O+] = 10−pH with the 10x key.

Answer

Above 7.45: alkalosis. [H3O+] = 10−7.48 = 3.3 × 10−8 M, less than the 4.0 × 10−8 M at pH 7.40.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

An arterial blood sample has [H3O+] = 3.16 × 10−8 M. What is the pH, and is this acidosis, alkalosis, or normal (7.35–7.45)?

Hint

pH = −log[H3O+]; three significant figures give three decimal places. Then compare the pH with 7.35–7.45.

Answer

pH = −log(3.16 × 10−8) = 7.5003… = 7.500 (three decimal places for three significant figures). Above 7.45: alkalosis. (Context only; interpretation follows the provider's orders.)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

0.20 g of NaOH (39.997 g/mol) is dissolved in enough water to make 500. mL of solution. What is the pH?

Hint

Map it out: g → mol NaOH → mol OH− → [OH−] (divide by liters) → pOH → pH.

Answer

0.20 g ÷ 39.997 g/mol = 5.000 × 10−3 mol NaOH = mol OH− (1 : 1). [OH−] = 5.000 × 10−3 mol ÷ 0.500 L = 0.010 M. pOH = −log(0.010) = 2.00; pH = 14.00 − 2.00 = 12.00.
