Learning Objectives

  • Explain evaporation, vapor pressure, and dynamic equilibrium, using the spread of kinetic energies in a liquid.
  • Use everyday cues (smell, drying, spills) to judge volatility.
  • Relate boiling point to vapor pressure and outside pressure, and describe sublimation.
  • Rank substances by boiling point, melting point, or vapor pressure from their intermolecular forces.

A puddle disappears on a warm day without ever boiling. Rubbing alcohol vanishes from your skin in seconds, while water lingers. And at a mountain campsite, water boils before it gets as hot as it does in a Fresno kitchen. All three come down to how easily molecules escape from a liquid.

Evaporation and Vapor Pressure

The molecules in a liquid have a range of kinetic energies. A few molecules at the surface move fast enough to break free of their neighbors and escape into the gas phase; this is evaporation. Because the fastest molecules leave, the average kinetic energy of the ones left behind drops, so evaporation cools the liquid. Evaporation speeds up at higher temperature and with more surface area.

Picture a graph of the molecules in a liquid: the percentage of molecules on the vertical axis and their kinetic energy on the horizontal axis. The graph is a hump. Most molecules have a middling kinetic energy, a few are slow, and a few are very fast. Draw a vertical line on the graph at the minimum kinetic energy a molecule needs to escape the liquid. Only the molecules to the right of that line can evaporate. At a low temperature, most of the hump sits to the left of the line: most molecules move too slowly to escape. At a higher temperature the whole hump shifts and spreads to the right, so more molecules pass the line, and evaporation speeds up. Because it is always the fastest molecules that leave, the ones left behind are slower on average: that is why evaporation cools.

Spreading a liquid out works for a different reason: more molecules sit at the surface, positioned to escape. Spill a glass of water across the counter and set a full glass beside it; the spill dries first. The dew on grass and cars in the early morning is another example. It condensed overnight when the air was cool, and as the day warms, more and more of its molecules gain enough energy to escape. The dew evaporates without ever boiling.

In a closed container, the escaped molecules collect above the liquid, and some of them hit the surface and condense. Soon the rates of evaporation and condensation are equal: a dynamic equilibrium, in which both processes continue but the amounts stop changing. The pressure exerted by the vapor at that point is the liquid's vapor pressure.

  • Vapor pressure increases with temperature, because more molecules have enough energy to escape.
  • Vapor pressure is lower when intermolecular forces are stronger, because molecules are held more tightly. A liquid with a high vapor pressure evaporates easily and is called volatile. At 25 °C, ethanol's vapor pressure (about 59 mmHg) is more than twice water's (about 24 mmHg): ethanol forms fewer hydrogen bonds per molecule, so it evaporates faster.

You can often judge volatility with your nose. If you can smell a substance from across the room, like perfume or nail-polish remover, its molecules are escaping into the air easily: it has a high vapor pressure. Gasoline is very volatile, which is why there is no smoking at the pump (the vapor in the air can ignite) and why you close the gas cap: in an open tank, the liquid would keep evaporating and escaping. In a sealed container, a volatile liquid does not evaporate completely; it reaches the dynamic equilibrium described above.

Boiling

A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the pressure of the gas above it. At that point bubbles of vapor can form throughout the liquid, not just at the surface. The normal boiling point is the boiling temperature when the outside pressure is exactly 1 atm (760 mmHg); for water it is 100 °C.

  • Lower outside pressure, as at high altitude, means the vapor pressure reaches the outside pressure at a lower temperature, so water boils below 100 °C and food cooks more slowly.
  • Higher outside pressure, as in a pressure cooker or an autoclave, raises the boiling point, so water stays liquid above 100 °C.

Sublimation and Deposition

Some solids have a vapor pressure high enough to pass directly into the gas phase without melting: sublimation. Dry ice (solid CO2) sublimes at −78 °C at 1 atm, and solid iodine and mothballs sublime slowly at room temperature. The reverse, gas to solid, is deposition, as when frost forms directly from water vapor on a cold windshield. Sublimation is endothermic; deposition is exothermic.

Biology Connection: Microbiology

An autoclave sterilizes lab and surgical equipment with steam under pressure. At about 15 psi above atmospheric pressure, water boils at about 121 °C, and steam at that temperature kills bacteria, fungi, and even heat-resistant bacterial spores that survive boiling at 100 °C. Typical cycles run at least 15 minutes. Microbiology labs also preserve bacterial cultures, and manufacturers preserve some vaccines and medicines, by freeze-drying (lyophilization): the sample is frozen and then put under very low pressure so the ice sublimes away, leaving a dry product that keeps for years and is restored by adding water.

Ranking Melting Points, Boiling Points, and Vapor Pressures

Stronger attractions between particles mean higher melting and boiling points and lower vapor pressure. To rank substances:

  1. Classify the solid type first. Covalent network solids and ionic compounds have the highest melting points (their particles are held by bonds); metals vary.
  2. For molecular substances, find the strongest intermolecular force in each (Section 9.6). For molecules of similar size: dispersion only < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
  3. If the strongest force is the same type, compare size: more electrons (larger molar mass) means stronger dispersion forces and a higher boiling point.

Vapor pressure ranks in the opposite order: the substance with the weakest forces has the highest vapor pressure.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Same size, different forces

Problem. Propane (CH3CH2CH3, 44.097 g/mol), dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3, 46.069 g/mol), and ethanol (CH3CH2OH, 46.069 g/mol) have nearly the same molar mass. Rank them from lowest to highest boiling point.

Solution. Propane is nonpolar: dispersion only. Dimethyl ether is polar with no O–H: dipole–dipole. Ethanol has an O–H: hydrogen bonding.

propane (−42 °C) < dimethyl ether (−24 °C) < ethanol (78 °C)

Check. With size held nearly constant, the order follows the strongest force. Ethanol, with hydrogen bonding, is the only liquid at room temperature.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Same force, different sizes

Problem. Rank methane (CH4), propane (C3H8), and pentane (C5H12) from lowest to highest boiling point. Which has the highest vapor pressure?

Solution. All three are nonpolar hydrocarbons with dispersion forces only. More carbons means more electrons and stronger dispersion forces:

CH4 (−162 °C) < C3H8 (−42 °C) < C5H12 (36 °C)

Methane, with the weakest forces, has the highest vapor pressure.

Check. This matches everyday experience: methane (natural gas) and propane are gases that must be stored under pressure, while pentane is a liquid.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Different kinds of solids

Problem. Rank by melting point: NaCl, CO2, SiO2 (quartz), H2O.

Solution. CO2 (molecular, dispersion only) < H2O (molecular, hydrogen bonding) < NaCl (ionic, 801 °C) < SiO2 (covalent network, about 1700 °C).

Check. The two molecular solids melt lowest; the network solid, held entirely by covalent bonds, melts highest.

Professor Tip

Ranking questions are two questions in one: "What type of substance is it?" and "What is its strongest force?" Write the strongest force next to each formula before you rank. Use size only as a tie-breaker between substances with the same strongest force; a small molecule that hydrogen bonds (H2O, 18 g/mol, bp 100 °C) can beat a much larger one that does not (H2S, 34 g/mol, bp −60 °C).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

An alcohol prep pad feels cold on the skin because isopropyl alcohol is volatile: it evaporates quickly, and evaporation absorbs heat from the skin. Its quick evaporation is also why the site is allowed to dry before an injection or IV start, so the alcohol has time to act and does not sting or enter the puncture. In the past, alcohol sponge baths were used to lower fevers, but alcohol vapor can be absorbed and inhaled, especially by small children, and the practice is no longer recommended; lukewarm water is used when cooling by evaporation is ordered. (Context only; follow the order and facility policy.)

Valley Connection: Swamp Coolers

Drive through older neighborhoods in Fresno, Clovis, or Bakersfield and you will see boxy evaporative coolers, often called swamp coolers, on many roofs. A fan pulls hot outdoor air through pads soaked with water. Water evaporates from the pads, absorbing its heat of vaporization from the air, and cooler, moister air blows into the house. Evaporative coolers use much less electricity than air conditioners, but they work only when the air is dry enough for water to evaporate quickly. The Valley's hot, dry summer afternoons suit them well; on muggy days, when the air is already close to saturated with water vapor, they cool much less, the same reason sweating cools you less on humid days.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy, Energy Saver, "Evaporative Coolers" (energy.gov/energysaver).

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking boiling point is fixed: it depends on the outside pressure; 100 °C is water's normal boiling point at 1 atm.
  • Ranking vapor pressure in the same order as boiling point: they run opposite.
  • Ranking by molar mass first: compare the strongest force first; use size only to break ties.
  • Treating evaporation and boiling as the same: evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the liquid when vapor pressure equals outside pressure.
  • Thinking a strong smell means strong forces: a substance you can smell from far away is escaping easily, so its forces are weak and its vapor pressure is high.

Summary

  • Vapor pressure is the pressure of a vapor in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid; it rises with temperature and falls with stronger intermolecular forces.
  • Only molecules above the minimum escape energy can evaporate; raising the temperature or the surface area speeds evaporation, and losing the fastest molecules cools the liquid.
  • A substance you can smell from across the room (perfume, gasoline) is volatile: high vapor pressure, weak forces.
  • A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the outside pressure; lower pressure lowers the boiling point.
  • Sublimation (solid → gas) absorbs heat; deposition (gas → solid) releases it.
  • Rank by solid type, then strongest intermolecular force, then size; vapor pressure runs in the reverse order.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which has the higher boiling point in each pair: (a) H2O or H2S; (b) Cl2 or Br2; (c) CH3OH or CH3F?

Hint

For each pair, find the strongest force first; if it is the same, compare the number of electrons.

Answer

(a) H2O (hydrogen bonding). (b) Br2 (more electrons, stronger dispersion). (c) CH3OH (hydrogen bonding; CH3F is only dipole–dipole).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Why does water boil at a lower temperature on a mountain than in the Valley?

Hint

A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the outside pressure. How does air pressure change with altitude?

Answer

Air pressure is lower at high altitude, so the vapor pressure of water reaches the outside pressure at a lower temperature.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Rank from highest to lowest vapor pressure at 25 °C: water, ethanol, diethyl ether (CH3CH2OCH2CH3).

Hint

Weaker forces mean a higher vapor pressure. Which can hydrogen bond with itself, and how many O–H groups does each have?

Answer

Diethyl ether (no hydrogen bonding with itself) > ethanol (one O–H, fewer hydrogen bonds) > water (most extensive hydrogen bonding).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A bottle of nail-polish remover (acetone) left open can be smelled across the room within seconds, while an open bottle of glycerol cannot be smelled at all. Which has the higher vapor pressure, and which has the stronger intermolecular forces?

Hint

Smelling a substance from across the room means its molecules escape easily. How are vapor pressure and intermolecular forces related?

Answer

Acetone has the higher vapor pressure (its molecules escape easily, so you smell them). Glycerol, with three O–H groups per molecule and extensive hydrogen bonding, has the stronger intermolecular forces and a very low vapor pressure.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Using the kinetic-energy picture, explain why a wet towel dries faster on a hot afternoon than on a cool morning.

Hint

Picture the hump of kinetic energies and the minimum energy needed to escape. What does a higher temperature do to the hump?

Answer

At the higher temperature, the spread of kinetic energies shifts to higher values, so a larger fraction of water molecules have more than the minimum energy needed to escape. More molecules evaporate each second.
