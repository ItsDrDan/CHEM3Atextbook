Learning Objectives

  • Convert among joules, kilojoules, calories, and food Calories.
  • Define system and surroundings, and give the sign of q for heat absorbed or released.
  • Classify physical and chemical changes as exothermic or endothermic.
  • Write heat as a reactant or a product in an equation, and read an energy diagram.

A nutrition label lists energy in Calories, a chemistry lab report in joules, and a heater in BTUs. They all measure the same thing. This section sets up the units and sign rules used in every heat calculation in the rest of the chapter.

Units of Energy

  • The joule (J) is the SI unit of energy: 1 J = 1 kg·m2/s2, about the energy needed to lift an apple 1 m. Chemical heats are often in kilojoules (1 kJ = 1000 J).
  • The calorie (cal) was first defined as the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of water by 1 °C. Today it is defined exactly: 1 cal = 4.184 J.
  • The food Calorie (capital C, Cal) is a kilocalorie: 1 Cal = 1 kcal = 1000 cal = 4184 J.

All three relationships are exact, so they never limit significant figures.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): A snack bar

Problem. A snack bar provides 245 Cal. How many kilojoules is that?

Solution.

\( {245\;\text{Cal}} \times \dfrac{1000\;\text{cal}}{1\;\text{Cal}} \times \dfrac{4.184\;\text{J}}{1\;\text{cal}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{kJ}}{1000\;\text{J}} = {1.03 \times 10^{3}\;\text{kJ}} \)

Check. Since 1 Cal = 4.184 kJ, the number of kilojoules is about 4 times the number of Calories. Scientific notation shows 3 significant figures, matching 245.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Joules to calories

Problem. Dissolving a sample of a salt releases 3.65 kJ of heat. Express this in calories.

Solution.

\( {3.65\;\text{kJ}} \times \dfrac{1000\;\text{J}}{1\;\text{kJ}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{cal}}{4.184\;\text{J}} = {872\;\text{cal}} \)

Check. A calorie is larger than a joule, so the number of calories (872) is smaller than the number of joules (3650).

System, Surroundings, and the Sign of q

To keep track of heat, chemists divide the world in two. The system is the part we are studying: the reaction, the melting ice, the dissolving salt. The surroundings are everything else: the water, the container, your hand, the air. Heat always flows from the hotter one to the colder one. The symbol q stands for heat, and its sign is written from the system's point of view:

  • Exothermic (heat goes out): the system releases heat, so q is negative, and the surroundings feel warmer. Examples: burning fuel, a hand warmer, freezing water, steam condensing.
  • Endothermic (heat goes in): the system absorbs heat, so q is positive, and the surroundings feel colder. Examples: an instant cold pack, melting ice, boiling water, sweat evaporating.

This is the same sign rule as ΔH in Section 8.6: negative means heat released.

Heat in the equation and energy diagrams

You can write the heat right into an equation, as if it were a substance. In an exothermic change heat comes out, so it is written as a product; in an endothermic change heat goes in, so it is written as a reactant:

\( \text{Exothermic:}\quad \text{A} \rightarrow \text{B} + \text{heat} \qquad\qquad \text{Endothermic:}\quad \text{A} + \text{heat} \rightarrow \text{B} \)

An energy diagram shows the same idea as a picture, with energy on the vertical axis. In an exothermic change the products sit lower than the reactants: the energy drops, and the difference leaves as heat (q < 0). In an endothermic change the products sit higher: the energy rises, and the difference had to be taken in (q > 0). Section 8.6 draws these diagrams for chemical reactions; they work the same way for melting, boiling, and dissolving.

Your body is a built-in detector. If it feels colder, the process is absorbing heat (endothermic); if it feels hotter, it is releasing heat (exothermic). Standing in front of an open refrigerator on a hot day cools you because heat flows from you (hot) to the cold air (heat always flows from hot to cold). Hold your hand close to your face and you feel heat coming off your skin: your body is releasing heat all the time, an exothermic system.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Exothermic or endothermic?

Problem. Classify each change and give the sign of q for the system: (a) water vapor condensing on a cold glass; (b) an instant cold pack activated on a sprained wrist; (c) ice cubes melting in a drink.

Solution. (a) Exothermic, q < 0: the vapor releases heat to the glass as it becomes liquid. (b) Endothermic, q > 0: the dissolving solid absorbs heat from the wrist, which feels cold. (c) Endothermic, q > 0: the ice absorbs heat from the drink.

Check. Ask which way the heat flows and what feels warmer or colder. The thing that feels cold to you is losing heat to the system.

Professor Tip

Pick the system first, then decide the direction. The same event can look opposite depending on the point of view: when ice melts in your hand, the ice is endothermic (q > 0), while your hand loses heat. Exam questions almost always ask about the system, the process being described.

In the Lab: Experiment 7a, Calorimetry (Part 1)

In Part 1 you dissolve two solids, citric acid and sodium carbonate, one at a time in water in a foam-cup calorimeter and record the starting temperature and the highest or lowest temperature reached. The dissolving solid is the system; the water is the surroundings. If the water's temperature goes up, heat came out of the system, so the process is exothermic; if it goes down, the process is endothermic. Your own data decide which solid is which.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Instant cold packs and hot packs are chemistry in a pouch. Squeezing a cold pack breaks an inner bag so a solid, often ammonium nitrate or urea, dissolves in water; dissolving them is endothermic, so the pack absorbs heat from the injured area. Instant hot packs dissolve a salt such as calcium chloride or magnesium sulfate, which is exothermic. Reusable gel heat packs release heat when a supersaturated solution of sodium acetate crystallizes. Because a pack can get colder or hotter than skin tolerates, packs are wrapped and checked often. (Context only; follow the order and the product directions.)

Biology Connection: Your Resting Energy Use

Even at rest, your body releases energy continuously to keep your heart beating, your ions pumped, and your temperature steady. This basal metabolic rate is roughly 1500 to 2000 Cal per day for many adults. Converting, 2.00 × 103 Cal per day is 8.37 × 106 J spread over 86,400 seconds, about 97 J every second, or roughly the power of a 100-watt light bulb. Most of it ends up as heat, which is why a crowded room warms up.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating a food Calorie as a calorie: 1 Cal = 1000 cal.
  • Multiplying when you should divide: joules → calories divides by 4.184; calories → joules multiplies.
  • Getting the sign backward: heat released by the system is negative.
  • Thinking freezing absorbs heat: freezing and condensing release heat (exothermic).
  • Putting heat on the wrong side: exothermic means heat is a product (A → B + heat); endothermic means heat is a reactant (A + heat → B).

Summary

  • 1 cal = 4.184 J (exact); 1 Cal = 1 kcal = 4184 J.
  • The system is what we study; the surroundings are everything else.
  • Exothermic: heat released, q < 0, surroundings warm. Endothermic: heat absorbed, q > 0, surroundings cool.
  • Exothermic: heat is a product and the energy diagram goes down. Endothermic: heat is a reactant and the diagram goes up. If it feels colder, it is absorbing; hotter, it is releasing.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A 12-oz can of soda provides 155 Cal. Convert this to kilojoules.

Hint

Use the exact relation 1 Cal = 4.184 kJ; the number of kilojoules should be about 4 times the number of Calories.

Answer

155 × 4.184 = 649 kJ (1 Cal = 4.184 kJ).

A food Calorie is 4.184 kJ, so 155 Cal × 4.184 kJ/Cal gives kilojoules, with three significant figures like 155 (the conversion is exact).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A process absorbs 525 J. How many calories is that, and what is the sign of q?

Hint

Divide joules by 4.184 J/cal. The system absorbs heat, so what sign does q take?

Answer

525 ÷ 4.184 = 125 cal. The system absorbs heat, so q = +525 J (endothermic).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Classify each as exothermic or endothermic: (a) dew forming on grass; (b) a candle burning; (c) dry ice turning into gas.

Hint

Ask whether the particles come together (heat released) or move apart (heat absorbed). Burning gives off heat and light.

Answer

(a) Exothermic (condensation releases heat). (b) Exothermic. (c) Endothermic (sublimation absorbs heat).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A pat of margarine provides 145 kJ of energy when it is metabolized. How many food Calories is that?

A. 34.7 Cal
B. 607 Cal
C. 0.0347 Cal

Hint

Use 4.184 kJ per Cal. A Calorie is bigger than a kilojoule, so should the number of Calories be smaller or larger than the number of kilojoules?

Answer

A. 1 Cal = 4.184 kJ, so 145 kJ ÷ 4.184 kJ/Cal = 34.7 Cal. B multiplies by 4.184 instead of dividing (a Calorie is bigger than a kilojoule, so the number of Calories must be smaller). C divides 145 by 4184 J/Cal without first changing kJ to J (145 kJ = 145,000 J); a kilojoule-to-Calorie conversion uses 4.184 kJ/Cal.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Rewrite each change with "heat" on the correct side of the arrow: (a) H2O(l) → H2O(g); (b) H2O(l) → H2O(s). On an energy diagram, which one goes uphill?

Hint

Heat goes in (a reactant) for endothermic changes and comes out (a product) for exothermic ones. Which change pulls the molecules apart?

Answer

(a) H2O(l) + heat → H2O(g) (boiling is endothermic). (b) H2O(l) → H2O(s) + heat (freezing is exothermic). Boiling, (a), goes uphill: the gas is higher in energy than the liquid.
