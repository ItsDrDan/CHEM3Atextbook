Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish temperature from heat.
  • Describe the Fahrenheit, Celsius, and Kelvin scales and absolute zero.
  • Convert temperatures among °F, °C, and K with correct significant figures.

"Is it a fever?" is one of the first questions in any clinic, and the answer depends on reading a temperature and, often, converting it between scales. In everyday speech, temperature and heat mean the same thing; in chemistry they do not.

Temperature Is Not Heat

Temperature is a measure of how hot or cold something is; on the particle level, it measures the average kinetic energy of the particles (Section 9.1). Heat is the energy that flows from a hotter object to a colder one. A bathtub of warm water and a cup of warm water can have the same temperature, but the bathtub holds far more thermal energy and can give off far more heat as it cools.

Three Temperature Scales

  • Fahrenheit (°F): used in everyday life in the United States. Water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F, 180 degrees apart.
  • Celsius (°C): used in science, medicine, and most of the world. Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C, 100 degrees apart. One Celsius degree is therefore 180/100 = 1.8 times as large as one Fahrenheit degree.
  • Kelvin (K): the SI scale, used in gas laws (Chapter 10). Its zero is absolute zero, 0 K = −273.15 °C, the lowest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible energy. A kelvin is the same size as a Celsius degree. Kelvin temperatures have no degree sign and are never negative: write 310 K, not 310 °K.

Converting Between Scales

These are the conversions on the CHEM 3A yellow sheet:

\( \text{K} = {}^\circ\text{C} + 273.15 \qquad {}^\circ\text{C} = \dfrac{{}^\circ\text{F} - 32}{1.8} \qquad {}^\circ\text{F} = 1.8\,({}^\circ\text{C}) + 32 \)

The numbers 273.15, 32, and 1.8 are exact (they come from the definitions of the scales), so they never limit significant figures. Apply the usual rules to the measured value: when you add or subtract, keep the decimal places of the measurement; when you multiply or divide, keep its significant figures (Chapter 1).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): A child's fever

Problem. A child's temperature is 103.5 °F. Convert it to °C and to K.

Solution.

\( {}^\circ\text{C} = \dfrac{103.5 - 32}{1.8} = \dfrac{71.5}{1.8} = 39.7\ {}^\circ\text{C} \qquad \text{K} = 39.7 + 273.15 = 312.9\ \text{K} \)

Check. 39.7 °C is about 2.7 °C above normal body temperature (37.0 °C), a real fever. Subtracting 32 left 71.5, which has 3 significant figures, so the Celsius answer has 3; the Kelvin answer keeps one decimal place.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Liquid nitrogen

Problem. Liquid nitrogen, used in dermatology to freeze warts, boils at 77.4 K. What is this temperature in °C and °F?

Solution.

\( {}^\circ\text{C} = 77.4 - 273.15 = -195.8\ {}^\circ\text{C} \qquad {}^\circ\text{F} = 1.8(-195.75) + 32 = -320.4\ {}^\circ\text{F} \)

Check. Both answers are far below zero, as expected for a liquefied gas. Carry the unrounded −195.75 into the second step and round only at the end.

Professor Tip

On the TI-36X Pro, type the parentheses: (103.5 − 32) ÷ 1.8. Without them the calculator divides 32 by 1.8 first and gives a wrong answer. To check a conversion, remember a few anchors: 0 °C = 32 °F, 37.0 °C = 98.6 °F, 100 °C = 212 °F, and −40 °C = −40 °F, the one temperature where the two scales agree.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Normal body temperature is about 37.0 °C (98.6 °F), though it varies by a few tenths of a degree with the person, the time of day, and where it is measured (oral, tympanic, axillary, rectal). A temperature of about 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) or higher is commonly treated as a fever (pyrexia), and a core temperature below 35.0 °C (95.0 °F) is hypothermia. Hospital charts in the United States often record Celsius while patients and families think in Fahrenheit, so nurses convert in both directions every shift. Many medications and vaccines also have storage ranges printed in Celsius, such as 2.0 °C to 8.0 °C for refrigerated vaccines. (Context only; follow facility policy for thresholds and actions.)

Biology Connection: Thermoregulation

Your body works like a thermostat. Temperature sensors in the skin and in the hypothalamus of the brain compare your temperature with a set point near 37 °C. When you are too warm, blood vessels in the skin widen (vasodilation) to release heat, and sweat glands start working. When you are too cold, skin vessels narrow (vasoconstriction) to keep heat in the core, and muscles shiver, converting the chemical energy of ATP into heat. A fever happens when the immune system raises the set point, which is why a person with a rising fever can feel chilled even as their temperature climbs.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the parentheses in (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8.
  • Subtracting 32 in the wrong direction: going from °C to °F you multiply by 1.8 and add 32.
  • Writing °K or a negative Kelvin temperature.
  • Letting 273.15, 32, or 1.8 limit significant figures; they are exact.

Summary

  • Temperature measures average kinetic energy; heat is energy in transfer.
  • K = °C + 273.15; °C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8; °F = 1.8(°C) + 32.
  • A kelvin equals a Celsius degree in size; a Celsius degree is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees. Absolute zero is 0 K.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Frozen vaccines are stored between −50.0 °C and −15.0 °C. Express this range in °F.

Hint

Use the yellow-sheet °C-to-°F formula on each end of the range: multiply first, then add 32. Keep the negative sign when you multiply.

Answer

1.8(−50.0) + 32 = −58.0 °F and 1.8(−15.0) + 32 = 5.0 °F, so −58.0 °F to 5.0 °F.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Convert a normal body temperature of 98.6 °F to kelvins.

Hint

Two steps: °C = (°F − 32)/1.8, then K = °C + 273.15. Type the parentheses on the calculator.

Answer

(98.6 − 32) ÷ 1.8 = 37.0 °C; 37.0 + 273.15 = 310.2 K.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

The temperature of a patient rises by 1.5 °C. By how many kelvins and by how many Fahrenheit degrees did it rise?

Hint

This is a change, not a reading: do not add 273.15 or 32. A kelvin equals a Celsius degree, and a Celsius degree is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees.

Answer

By 1.5 K (the degrees are the same size) and by 1.8 × 1.5 = 2.7 °F. For a change in temperature, do not add 273.15 or 32.
