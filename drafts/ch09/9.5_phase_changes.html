Learning Objectives

  • Name the six phase changes and classify each as endothermic or exothermic.
  • Use the heat of fusion and heat of vaporization to find the heat of a phase change.
  • Read a heating curve and add the heats of its steps.
  • Solve a full five-step ice-to-steam problem and a cooling (steam-to-ice) problem with the correct signs.
  • Apply the same method to substances other than water.
  • Going further (seen, not tested): read a phase diagram's regions, lines, triple point, and normal melting and boiling points.

Put a pot of water on the stove and watch the thermometer: the temperature climbs steadily, then stops at 100 °C and stays there, even though the burner is still on, until the water has boiled away. Where does that heat go?

Changes of State

A phase change (change of state) converts a substance from one state to another without changing its identity. Changes that pull particles apart absorb heat; changes that bring them together release heat.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): The six phase changes
ChangeFrom → toHeat
Melting (fusion)solid → liquidabsorbed (endothermic)
Freezingliquid → solidreleased (exothermic)
Vaporization (boiling, evaporation)liquid → gasabsorbed (endothermic)
Condensationgas → liquidreleased (exothermic)
Sublimationsolid → gasabsorbed (endothermic)
Depositiongas → solidreleased (exothermic)

The table above pairs each change with its reverse; the reverse change has the same size of heat with the opposite sign.

During a phase change the temperature stays constant. All of the added heat goes into separating the particles (raising their potential energy), not into making them move faster (Section 9.1).

Picture it at the particle level. In a simulation of solid neon, a neat cube of atoms vibrates in place as it is warmed (a slope). Near the melting point, the cube starts to fall apart: atoms spread out and slide past one another, and the solid becomes a liquid (a plateau). Warm it a little more and atoms escape into the space above as a gas. Ice behaves the same way, except that its hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open framework; as it warms, the framework expands, then the molecules start sliding past each other and it melts. Vaporizing takes the most energy of all because every molecule must be pulled completely away from its neighbors. Think of friends you do not want to leave: the stronger the friendship (the attraction), the more it takes to pull you away.

Heat of Fusion and Heat of Vaporization

  • The heat of fusion (ΔHfus) is the heat needed to melt a substance at its melting point. For water, ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol (334 J/g).
  • The heat of vaporization (ΔHvap) is the heat needed to vaporize a liquid at its boiling point. For water, ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol (2260 J/g).

The heat for a phase change is

\( q = n \times \Delta H \qquad (n = \text{moles}) \qquad \text{or} \qquad q = m \times \Delta H \ \ (\text{if } \Delta H \text{ is per gram}) \)

You can also write the heat right into the equation (Section 9.3). Many nursing and allied-health texts give these values per gram, in calories:

\( \text{H}_2\text{O(s)} + 80.\ \text{cal/g}\ (334\ \text{J/g}) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \qquad \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + 540\ \text{cal/g}\ (2260\ \text{J/g}) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O(g)} \)

Run either equation backward (freezing, condensing) and the same heat appears on the product side: it is released. The per-gram and per-mole values are the same information: 6.02 kJ/mol ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 0.334 kJ/g = 334 J/g, and 334 J/g ÷ 4.184 J/cal = 80. cal/g.

Check the units of ΔH to decide whether you need moles or grams. For freezing and condensing, use the same value with a negative sign, because heat is released. Notice that vaporizing water takes almost 7 times as much heat as melting it: separating molecules completely into a gas takes far more energy than loosening them into a liquid.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Melting ice

Problem. How much heat is needed to melt 25.0 g of ice at 0 °C? (H2O 18.015 g/mol; ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol)

Solution.

\( 25.0\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{18.015\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{6.02\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 8.35\ \text{kJ} \)

Check. Melting is endothermic, so q = +8.35 kJ. Using 334 J/g gives 25.0 × 334 = 8.35 × 103 J, the same answer.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Why steam burns are severe

Problem. How much heat is released when 15.0 g of steam condenses to liquid water at 100 °C? (ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol)

Solution.

\( 15.0\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{18.015\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{40.7\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 33.9\ \text{kJ} \qquad q = -33.9\ \text{kJ} \)

Check. Condensing releases heat, so q is negative. If this steam condensed on skin, all 33.9 kJ would go into the skin before the water even began to cool, which is why steam at 100 °C burns much worse than water at 100 °C.

Heating Curves

A heating curve plots the temperature of a substance against the heat added. It is made of two kinds of segments:

  • Sloped segments: one state warms up. Use q = mcΔT with the specific heat of that state (ice 2.09, liquid water 4.184, steam 2.01 J/(g·°C)).
  • Flat segments (plateaus): a phase change at constant temperature. Use q = nΔH.

To find the total heat for a change that crosses one or more plateaus, calculate the heat for each segment separately and add them, with all of them in the same unit.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Ice to warm water

Problem. How much heat is needed to turn 20.0 g of ice at −15.0 °C into liquid water at 25.0 °C? (cice = 2.09 J/(g·°C); cwater = 4.184 J/(g·°C); ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol; H2O 18.015 g/mol)

Solution. Three steps: warm the ice to 0 °C, melt it, warm the water to 25.0 °C.

\( q_1 = (20.0)(2.09)(15.0) = 627\ \text{J} = 0.627\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_2 = \dfrac{20.0\ \text{g}}{18.015\ \text{g/mol}} \times 6.02\ \text{kJ/mol} = 6.68\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_3 = (20.0)(4.184)(25.0) = 2092\ \text{J} = 2.09\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_\text{total} = 0.627 + 6.68 + 2.09 = 9.40\ \text{kJ} \)

Check. The melting step is the largest, as the plateau on the curve suggests. Converting every step to kJ before adding avoids mixing units.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): An ice pack on the body

Problem. To reduce swelling, a 185 g ice pack at 0 °C is placed on a sprained ankle. How much heat does it absorb as the ice melts and the meltwater warms to body temperature, 37.0 °C? (ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol; cwater = 4.184 J/(g·°C); H2O 18.015 g/mol)

Solution. Sketch it: a plateau at 0 °C (melting, q1 = nΔHfus), then a slope from 0 to 37.0 °C (warming, q2 = mcΔT).

\( q_1 = 185\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{18.015\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{6.02\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 61.8\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_2 = (185\ \text{g})(4.184)(37.0 - 0\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = 2.86 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J} = 28.6\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_\text{total} = 61.82 + 28.64 = 90.5\ \text{kJ} \)

Check. q1 came out in kJ and q2 in J, so q2 was converted before adding. Add the unrounded values (61.82 and 28.64) and round once at the end; adding the rounded 61.8 and 28.6 would give 90.4. Melting does about twice as much cooling as warming the water, which is why ice beats a pack of cold water. (Context only; follow the care plan for cold therapy.)

The full ice-to-steam problem

The hardest version of this problem crosses both plateaus: ice well below 0 °C becomes steam above 100 °C. It is long but not new. Stay organized:

  1. Sketch the curve and mark the start and end temperatures.
  2. Label every segment q1, q2, …: each slope gets mcΔT with the specific heat of that state, and each plateau gets nΔH.
  3. Find the moles once at the start; both plateaus use the same n.
  4. Convert every q to kJ, then add.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Five segments, ice to steam

Problem. How much heat is needed to turn 50.0 g of ice at −30.0 °C into steam at 120.0 °C? (cice = 2.09, cwater = 4.184, csteam = 2.01 J/(g·°C); ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol; ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol; H2O 18.015 g/mol)

Solution. Moles, once: 50.0 g ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 2.775 mol. Then the five segments:

Table \(\PageIndex{2}\): The five segments for 50.0 g of ice at −30.0 °C to steam at 120.0 °C
SegmentWhat happensEquationq (kJ)
q1 (slope)Ice warms, −30.0 → 0 °C(50.0)(2.09)(30.0) = 3135 J3.14
q2 (plateau)Ice melts at 0 °C(2.775 mol)(6.02 kJ/mol)16.7
q3 (slope)Water warms, 0 → 100 °C(50.0)(4.184)(100.0) = 20,920 J20.9
q4 (plateau)Water boils at 100 °C(2.775 mol)(40.7 kJ/mol)113
q5 (slope)Steam warms, 100 → 120.0 °C(50.0)(2.01)(20.0) = 2010 J2.01

\( q_\text{total} = 3.14 + 16.7 + 20.9 + 113 + 2.01 = 156\ \text{kJ} \)

Check. The boiling plateau, q4, is by far the largest step (about 70% of the total), just as the long plateau on the heating curve shows. The sum is rounded to the ones place because 113 kJ is known only to the ones place (addition rule).

Going the other way: cooling curves

A cooling curve is the heating curve read from right to left. Every step now releases heat, so every q is negative: steam condenses at 100 °C, liquid water cools, and water freezes at 0 °C. The plateaus are at the same temperatures as before, and the heats have the same size with the opposite sign.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Steam to ice

Problem. A geyser vents 60.0 g of steam at 100.0 °C. How many kilojoules are released as the steam condenses, the water cools, and the water freezes at 0.0 °C? (ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol; ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol; cwater = 4.184 J/(g·°C); H2O 18.015 g/mol)

Solution. n = 60.0 ÷ 18.015 = 3.331 mol. Three steps, all exothermic:

\( q_1 = -(3.331\ \text{mol})(40.7\ \text{kJ/mol}) = -136\ \text{kJ} \quad \text{(condense)} \)

\( q_2 = (60.0\ \text{g})(4.184)(0.0 - 100.0\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = -2.51 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J} = -25.1\ \text{kJ} \quad \text{(cool)} \)

\( q_3 = -(3.331\ \text{mol})(6.02\ \text{kJ/mol}) = -20.0\ \text{kJ} \quad \text{(freeze)} \)

\( q_\text{total} = -136 - 25.1 - 20.0 = -181\ \text{kJ} \)

The steam releases 181 kJ.

Check. All three signs are negative because heat leaves the water at every step. The cooling step's ΔT (final minus initial) is negative on its own, so its sign comes out of mcΔT automatically; the two plateaus need the minus sign written in.

The method works for any substance

Nothing in these steps is special to water. For another substance, use its own specific heat, melting and boiling points, and ΔH values, which a problem will give you. Watch the units of ΔH: per gram (J/g) means multiply by the mass; per mole (kJ/mol) means convert to moles first.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): Ethanol to vapor

Problem. How much heat is needed to take 20.0 g of liquid ethanol at 25.0 °C to a gas at its boiling point, 78.0 °C? For liquid ethanol, c = 2.42 J/(g·°C) and the heat of vaporization is 841 J/g.

Solution. A slope to the boiling point, then the boiling plateau. Because ΔHvap is per gram, no moles are needed:

\( q_1 = (20.0)(2.42)(78.0 - 25.0) = 2.57 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J} \qquad q_2 = (20.0\ \text{g})(841\ \text{J/g}) = 1.68 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J} \)

\( q_\text{total} = 2565 + 16{,}820 = 1.94 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J} = 19.4\ \text{kJ} \)

Check. As with water, the plateau (vaporization) is the big step. The problem stops at the boiling point, so there is no "gas warms" segment.

Professor Tip

Sketch the heating curve before you calculate. Mark the starting and ending temperatures, then list every slope and plateau in between; each one is its own q. Slopes use mcΔT (no moles), plateaus use nΔH (no ΔT). A common exam trap starts or ends exactly at 0 °C or 100 °C: decide whether the phase change happens or not.

In the Lab: Experiment 7a, Calorimetry (Part 2)

In Part 2 you drop ice into a weighed amount of water in your calorimeter and stir until it melts, then record the lowest temperature. The warm water loses heat (q = mcΔT, with a negative ΔT); the ice gains heat, first to melt (q = nΔHfus) and then to warm the meltwater. Conservation of energy says the heats add to zero, so your data let you test that law and estimate water's heat of fusion. The mass of ice comes from the increase in the calorimeter's mass.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

Air delivered through an endotracheal tube or a high-flow nasal cannula bypasses the nose, which normally warms and humidifies each breath. Dry, cool gas can thicken secretions and irritate the airways, so respiratory therapists use heated humidifiers that warm the gas toward body temperature and load it with water vapor. Vaporizing water takes a great deal of heat (40.7 kJ/mol at 100 °C, and even a little more at body temperature), which is why the humidifier needs a heater plate rather than just a water chamber. Some circuits use heated wires so the vapor does not condense ("rain out") in the cooler tubing. (Context only; follow the device settings and orders.)

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

After an extraction or periodontal surgery, patients are often told to apply cold to the cheek for the first day to limit swelling. A bag of ice works well because melting absorbs a large amount of heat, 334 J for every gram, while the ice stays at 0 °C. A gel cold pack straight from the freezer can be colder than 0 °C at first, and it absorbs heat only by warming, with no melting step, so it warms up faster. Either way, a cloth between the cold source and the skin protects tissue from cold injury. (Context only; follow the dentist's post-operative instructions.)

Biology Connection: Sweating

Evaporation is vaporization at the surface of a liquid, below its boiling point, and it is endothermic. When sweat evaporates, each gram absorbs about 2.4 kJ from the skin (slightly more than at 100 °C), cooling the blood flowing just beneath. A person exercising hard in the heat can lose more than a liter of sweat per hour, which is why rehydration matters. In humid air sweat evaporates slowly and drips off instead, removing much less heat, so heat illness is more likely on humid days.

Going Further: Phase Diagrams

Seen, Not Tested

In CHEM 3A, heating and cooling curves (q = mcΔT for the slopes and q = nΔH for the plateaus) are the main skill. Phase diagrams are here so you can see the other common way of picturing phase changes and read one if you meet it; you will not be asked to calculate anything from them.

A heating curve follows one sample as heat is added at a fixed pressure. A phase diagram is a "map" instead: it shows which phase a substance is in at every combination of pressure (vertical axis) and temperature (horizontal axis). Changing the pressure, the temperature, or both moves you from one point on the map to another.

  • Regions. The map has three regions: solid at low temperature and high pressure (upper left), gas at high temperature and low pressure (lower right), and liquid in between.
  • Lines. Each line separates two phases. A point on a line is a temperature and pressure where those two phases exist together at equilibrium, such as ice and water at 0 °C and 1 atm. Crossing a line means a phase change: solid–liquid (melting or freezing), liquid–gas (boiling or condensing), and solid–gas (sublimation or deposition), the same six changes as in Table 1.
  • Triple point. All three lines meet at one point, the triple point, the only temperature and pressure where solid, liquid, and gas all exist together. For water it is 0.01 °C and about 0.006 atm.
  • Normal melting and boiling points. Draw a horizontal line at 1 atm. Where it crosses the solid–liquid line is the normal melting point (0 °C for water); where it crosses the liquid–gas curve is the normal boiling point (100 °C for water). "Normal" means at 1 atm.

Water is unusual. For most substances, the solid–liquid line leans slightly to the right. Water's leans to the left, because ice is less dense than liquid water (that is why ice floats). Squeezing ice therefore lowers its melting point a little. The effect is small: it takes more than 100 atm of extra pressure to lower the melting point by just 1 °C, so it is only a small part of why ice skates glide.

Dry ice. For carbon dioxide, the triple point is at about 5.1 atm, well above ordinary air pressure. At 1 atm, solid CO2 never melts: it sublimes straight to a gas at about −78 °C. That is why dry ice leaves no puddle and why it is called "dry."

Common Mistakes

  • Using q = mcΔT on a plateau: during a phase change ΔT = 0; use nΔH.
  • Forgetting to convert grams to moles when ΔH is in kJ/mol.
  • Adding J and kJ in a multi-step problem.
  • Using the wrong specific heat: ice, liquid water, and steam each have their own.
  • Dropping the sign for freezing or condensing (heat released, q < 0).
  • Skipping a segment in a long problem: list every slope and plateau between the start and end temperatures before calculating.
  • Using water's values for another substance: each substance has its own c, melting and boiling points, and ΔH.

Summary

  • Melting, vaporization, and sublimation absorb heat; freezing, condensation, and deposition release it.
  • Phase change: q = nΔH (water: ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol, ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol), at constant temperature.
  • Heating curve: slopes use q = mcΔT, plateaus use q = nΔH; add the steps for the total.
  • Per gram, water needs 80. cal (334 J) to melt and 540 cal (2260 J) to vaporize.
  • A cooling curve is the heating curve run backward: every step releases heat (q < 0).
  • Going further: a phase diagram maps the phase at each pressure and temperature; lines are two phases at equilibrium, the triple point is all three, and the normal melting and boiling points are read at 1 atm.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How much heat is needed to boil away 5.00 g of water at 100 °C?

Hint

Grams → moles of water, then q = nΔHvap (40.7 kJ/mol). The water is already at 100 °C, so there is no slope step.

Answer

5.00 ÷ 18.015 = 0.2775 mol; × 40.7 kJ/mol = 11.3 kJ.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

How much heat is released when 50.0 g of liquid water freezes at 0 °C?

Hint

Grams → moles, then × ΔHfus (6.02 kJ/mol). Freezing releases heat, so q is negative.

Answer

50.0 ÷ 18.015 = 2.775 mol; × 6.02 kJ/mol = 16.7 kJ released (q = −16.7 kJ).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

On a heating curve for water, which segment is longest, and why? What is happening to the temperature along it?

Hint

Compare ΔHvap with ΔHfus for water. On a flat segment, what does the temperature do?

Answer

The boiling plateau at 100 °C, because vaporizing takes far more heat than melting or warming. The temperature stays constant while the liquid turns to gas.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Use the cooling curve for water (Figure 1 read from right to left). (a) At what temperature does water condense? (b) At 0 °C, what happens to liquid water as heat is removed? (c) At 60 °C, what state is the water in? (d) When water freezes, is heat added or removed?

Hint

Read the curve from right to left. Plateaus are phase changes; sloped parts are one state cooling. Removing heat while particles come together is which change?

Answer

(a) 100 °C. (b) It freezes (the plateau at 0 °C). (c) Liquid (between 0 and 100 °C). (d) Removed: freezing is exothermic.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

A foundry melts 125 g of aluminum at its melting point. How much heat does the melting take? (ΔHfus for aluminum = 10.7 kJ/mol; Al 26.98 g/mol)

Hint

The aluminum is already at its melting point, so there is only a plateau: grams → moles, then × ΔHfus.

Answer

125 ÷ 26.98 = 4.633 mol; × 10.7 kJ/mol = 49.6 kJ. The metal is already at its melting point, so there is no slope step.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

How many calories does it take to melt 15.0 g of ice at 0 °C, and then to vaporize the same 15.0 g of water at 100 °C? (80. cal/g and 540 cal/g)

Hint

These values are per gram, so q = m × ΔH for each step. Watch the significant figures in 80. and 540.

Answer

Melting: 15.0 × 80. = 1.2 × 103 cal (2 significant figures, from 80.). Vaporizing: 15.0 × 540 = 8.1 × 103 cal. Vaporizing takes almost 7 times as much.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{7}\) (Going Further)

Use the phase diagram of water (Figure 2). (a) What phase is water in at 1 atm and 50 °C? (b) What is true of the point where the 1 atm line meets the liquid–gas curve? (c) At a pressure below the triple point, ice is warmed until it crosses a line. What phase change happens?

Hint

Locate each point on the phase diagram: 1 atm and 50 °C; where the 1 atm line meets the liquid–gas curve; and below the triple point, which two regions touch?

Answer

(a) Liquid: 50 °C at 1 atm lies between the normal melting point (0 °C) and the normal boiling point (100 °C). (b) Liquid and gas exist together at equilibrium there; it is the normal boiling point, 100 °C. (c) Sublimation: below the triple-point pressure, the only line between solid and gas is the solid–gas curve, so ice goes straight to vapor (this is how freeze-drying works).
