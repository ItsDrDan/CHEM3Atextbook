Learning Objectives

  • Describe kinetic and potential energy and convert among temperature scales and energy units.
  • Calculate heat with q = mcΔT and q = nΔH, including heating curves and simple calorimetry.
  • Identify intermolecular forces and use them to explain and rank properties of liquids and solids.
  • Classify crystalline solids and explain vapor pressure, boiling, and sublimation.

Key Ideas

1. Energy is conserved and comes in two kinds

Kinetic energy is motion (temperature measures average KE); potential energy is position or composition (chemical energy). Energy changes form but is never created or destroyed. Attracted particles are like a spring: close together = low potential energy, pulled apart = high. When the attraction beats the kinetic energy, particles stick. (Section 9.1)

2. Three temperature scales

K = °C + 273.15; °C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8; °F = 1.8(°C) + 32. The constants are exact. A temperature change is the same in K and °C and 1.8 times larger in °F. (Section 9.2)

3. Units and signs

1 cal = 4.184 J; 1 Cal = 1 kcal. Exothermic: heat released, q < 0. Endothermic: heat absorbed, q > 0. Decide the system first. Exothermic: A → B + heat (energy diagram goes down). Endothermic: A + heat → B (goes up). If it feels colder, it is absorbing. (Section 9.3)

4. Temperature changes: q = mcΔT

ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial. Water's c = 4.184 J/(g·°C) is unusually high. In a calorimeter, heat lost by the hot object = heat gained by the cold one. Convert volume to grams first. In coffee-cup calorimetry, when two solutions are mixed, m is the total mass of the combined solution, and qreaction = −qsolution. (Section 9.4)

5. Phase changes: q = nΔH

Water: ΔHfus = 6.02 kJ/mol, ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol. On a heating curve, slopes use mcΔT and plateaus use nΔH; add every step in the same unit. For ice to steam: sketch, label q1–q5, find moles once, convert to kJ, add. Cooling curves release heat at every step. Per gram: 80. cal (334 J) to melt, 540 cal (2260 J) to vaporize. (Section 9.5)

6. Four intermolecular forces

Dispersion (all; stronger with more electrons), dipole–dipole (polar), hydrogen bonding (H on N, O, F), ion–dipole (ions with polar molecules). For similar sizes they rank in that order. Bonds are family; intermolecular forces are friends. Three things decide the strength of dispersion forces: number of electrons, then valence-shell size (these two set the polarizability; the coin-flip model), then shape. Hydrogen-bond donor = H on F, N, or O; acceptor = a lone pair on F, N, or O. (Section 9.6)

7. Liquids and solids

Stronger forces: higher melting point, boiling point, surface tension, and viscosity, and lower vapor pressure. Solids are ionic, molecular, covalent network, atomic nonbonding, or metallic; network and ionic solids melt highest. Of these types, only metals conduct as solids (graphite is a rare exception). (Section 9.7)

8. Vapor pressure and ranking

Stronger forces: lower vapor pressure, higher boiling point. A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the outside pressure. Rank by solid type, then strongest force, then size. Only molecules above the minimum escape energy evaporate, so higher temperature and more surface area speed evaporation. If you can smell it across the room, its vapor pressure is high. (Section 9.8)

Skills Checklist

Before the exam, make sure you can do each of these without notes:

  • Given a compound (such as CH4, H2O, or Br2), name its strongest intermolecular force.
  • Explain why one substance has a higher boiling or melting point than another, in terms of intermolecular forces.
  • For several substances with only dispersion forces, explain what decides which has the strongest (number of electrons, valence-shell size, shape), and define polarizability.
  • Explain vapor pressure at the particle level. Can you smell it from across the room? Then its vapor pressure is high.
  • Explain surface tension (beading up) and viscosity ("thicker" and "stickier," pours slowly), and which way each goes with stronger forces.
  • Tell evaporation from boiling, and say how to make evaporation faster or slower.
  • Fill in the strong-versus-weak property table and the solid-types table (example, what holds it together, melting point, conducts or not) from memory.
  • From a list of substances, pick the one with the highest boiling point, melting point, vapor pressure, or surface tension.
  • Solve q = mcΔT for any variable (including a density step and coffee-cup calorimetry) and a full heating or cooling curve problem.

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): A cooling cup of coffee

Problem. A 150.0 g cup of coffee cools from 185.0 °F to 140.0 °F. Treating the coffee as water, how much heat does it release?

Solution. The temperature change in Fahrenheit is 140.0 − 185.0 = −45.0 °F. A Celsius degree is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees, so ΔT = −45.0 ÷ 1.8 = −25.0 °C (no 32 for a change).

\( q = (150.0\ \text{g})(4.184)(-25.0\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = -1.57 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J} \)

Check. Negative q means heat released, as expected for cooling. Converting each temperature to °C first (85.0 °C and 60.0 °C) gives the same ΔT.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Steam to warm water

Problem. How much heat is released when 10.0 g of steam at 110.0 °C cools, condenses, and cools again to liquid water at 40.0 °C? (csteam = 2.01, cwater = 4.184 J/(g·°C); ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol; H2O 18.015 g/mol)

Solution. Three steps, all released (negative):

\( q_1 = (10.0)(2.01)(100.0 - 110.0) = -201\ \text{J} = -0.201\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_2 = -\dfrac{10.0\ \text{g}}{18.015\ \text{g/mol}} \times 40.7\ \text{kJ/mol} = -22.6\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_3 = (10.0)(4.184)(40.0 - 100.0) = -2.51 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J} = -2.51\ \text{kJ} \)

\( q_\text{total} = -0.201 - 22.6 - 2.51 = -25.3\ \text{kJ} \)

The total is rounded to tenths because q2 (−22.6 kJ) is known only to tenths.

Check. Condensing (the plateau) releases far more heat than either cooling step, which is why steam burns are so serious.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Ranking boiling points

Problem. Rank F2, HCl, HBr, and HF from lowest to highest boiling point.

Solution. F2 is nonpolar (dispersion only). HCl and HBr are polar (dipole–dipole); HBr has more electrons, so stronger dispersion forces on top. HF has H bonded to F (hydrogen bonding).

F2 (−188 °C) < HCl (−85 °C) < HBr (−67 °C) < HF (20 °C)

Check. HF is the lightest of the three hydrogen halides but boils highest: the strongest force wins before size is considered.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting parentheses in (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8, or adding 32 to a temperature change.
  • ΔT written as initial minus final, which flips the sign of q.
  • Using q = mcΔT on a plateau, or forgetting to convert grams to moles for nΔH.
  • Adding J and kJ in a multi-step problem.
  • Calling any molecule with H and O "hydrogen bonding"; the H must be on N, O, or F.
  • Ranking by molar mass before checking the strongest force; ranking vapor pressure in the same order as boiling point.
  • In coffee-cup calorimetry, using the mass of only one solution when two solutions are mixed, or forgetting that qreaction = −qsolution.
  • Counting N(CH3)3, H2, or PH3 as hydrogen bonding with itself.
