Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish intermolecular forces from chemical bonds.
  • Describe dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces, hydrogen bonding, and ion–dipole forces.
  • Explain polarizability with the coin-flip model, and rank dispersion forces by number of electrons, size, and shape.
  • Identify hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors.
  • Identify all the intermolecular forces in a substance and its strongest one, including the main force between two different substances.

Methane (CH4, 16 g/mol) is a gas that boils at −162 °C. Water (H2O, 18 g/mol) is almost the same mass, yet it is a liquid that boils at 100 °C. The difference is not in the molecules themselves but in how strongly they attract each other.

Bonds Versus Intermolecular Forces

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are attractions between separate molecules (or between ions and molecules). They are not bonds: no electrons are shared or transferred. Chemical bonds hold the atoms within a molecule together and are much stronger. When water boils, the molecules separate from each other, but each H2O molecule stays whole; only intermolecular forces are overcome. The stronger the intermolecular forces, the more energy it takes to separate the molecules, so the higher the melting and boiling points.

An analogy helps keep the two straight. Bonds are like family: you are related, you live together, and that connection is very hard to break. Intermolecular forces are like friends: you hang out and text all the time, but you can leave and go home. Even a hydrogen bond, the strongest force between neutral molecules, is only a few percent as strong as a covalent bond. Polar molecules attract each other the way two magnets do: opposite ends pull together.

This also explains the states of matter you met in Section 1.6. At a given temperature, a substance that is a gas has the weakest intermolecular forces (its particles fly apart), and a substance that is a solid has the strongest (its particles are held in place). Liquids are in between.

Dispersion Forces

Dispersion forces (London dispersion forces) exist between all atoms and molecules. Electrons move constantly, so at any instant the electron cloud of an atom or molecule may be a little uneven, creating a brief instantaneous dipole. That temporary dipole pushes on the electrons of a neighbor and creates an induced dipole, and the two attract. Collisions are one way it happens. When two helium atoms collide, it is like slamming on the brakes in a car: you lurch forward, and so do the electrons. For an instant they bunch up on one side of each atom, and the two atoms have opposite partial charges that attract, very weakly and very briefly. Dispersion forces are the only intermolecular force between nonpolar molecules (such as CH4, CO2, and N2) and between noble gas atoms.

Dispersion forces get stronger as particles get bigger. A particle with more electrons, spread over a larger volume, has a cloud that is easier to distort; it is more polarizable. So, among similar substances, the one with more electrons (usually the larger molar mass) has stronger dispersion forces and a higher boiling point.

Why more electrons means more polarizable: the coin-flip model

Here is a deliberately oversimplified model. Treat each electron like a coin flip: at any instant it is either on the left side of the atom or on the right side, 50/50. An atom has no instantaneous dipole only when its electrons happen to split exactly half and half. With 2 electrons (helium) that happens half the time. With more electrons, a perfectly even split gets less and less likely, just as flipping exactly 27 heads in 54 coin tosses is much less likely than flipping 1 head in 2 tosses. Table \(\PageIndex{2}\) shows the probabilities.

Table \(\PageIndex{2}\): The coin-flip model: chance that an atom's electrons are split exactly evenly
AtomElectrons ("coin flips")Chance of an exactly even split
He250.0%
Ne1024.6%
Ar1818.5%
Kr3613.2%
Xe5410.8%

A xenon atom is evenly balanced only about 1 time in 10; the rest of the time its electron cloud is lopsided, an instantaneous dipole ready to attract a neighbor. The tendency to form an instantaneous dipole is the polarizability. Atoms and molecules with more electrons are more polarizable, so they have stronger dispersion forces, and the boiling points in the noble-gas table climb from 4 K to 165 K. (Real electrons are not coins, but the model gets the trend right.)

Three things decide the strength of dispersion forces, in order of importance: (1) the number of electrons; (2) the size of the valence shells (electrons spread over a larger volume are easier to push around); together these set the polarizability; and (3) the shape of the molecule. Long, straight molecules touch their neighbors along more of their length than compact, branched ones, so a straight-chain molecule has slightly stronger dispersion forces than a branched one with the same formula. The simple hydrocarbons show the first factor clearly:

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): H2 or O2?

Problem. Name the strongest intermolecular force in H2 and in O2, and predict which has the higher boiling point.

Solution. Start by deciding polar or nonpolar. A bond between two identical atoms has an electronegativity difference of zero, so both molecules are nonpolar, and the strongest force in each is dispersion. Now compare polarizability. O2 has 16 electrons spread over larger orbitals; H2 has only 2. O2 is more polarizable, so it has the stronger dispersion forces and the higher boiling point.

Check. Real boiling points: H2 20 K, O2 90 K. Same force type, so the bigger molecule wins.

Dipole–Dipole Forces

A polar molecule has a permanent partial positive end (δ+) and partial negative end (δ−) because its bond dipoles do not cancel (Chapter 6: polar bonds plus an asymmetric shape; remember SNAP, "symmetric nonpolar, asymmetric polar"). Dipole–dipole forces are the attractions between the δ+ end of one polar molecule and the δ− end of another. HCl, CH2Cl2, SO2, and CH3OCH3 all have dipole–dipole forces, in addition to dispersion forces.

Hydrogen Bonding

A hydrogen bond is an especially strong dipole–dipole attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded directly to N, O, or F and a lone pair on another N, O, or F. N, O, and F are so electronegative that the H attached to them is very δ+, and it is small enough to get very close to the neighbor's lone pair. The pattern is

X–H ··· :Y    (X and Y = N, O, or F)

Two names describe the two partners. The molecule that supplies the H (the H attached to N, O, or F) is the hydrogen-bond donor. The molecule that supplies the lone pair on N, O, or F is the hydrogen-bond acceptor. In water, every molecule can be both: its O–H hydrogens donate, and the lone pairs on its O accept. Memory aids: the three atoms spell "FNO", and "see an O–H, think hydrogen bonding."

Water, ammonia (NH3), hydrogen fluoride (HF), alcohols such as methanol (CH3OH), and amines with N–H bonds all hydrogen bond with themselves. A hydrogen in a C–H bond does not count: CH3F has an F and an H, but the H is bonded to carbon, so it cannot hydrogen bond with itself. A hydrogen bond is not a covalent bond; it is a few percent as strong, but it is still the strongest force between neutral molecules.

Boiling points show how much hydrogen bonding adds. The graph below plots the boiling points of the simple hydrogen compounds of Groups 15, 16, and 17. From period 3 down, each group’s boiling point rises as the molecules get more electrons, the dispersion trend from earlier in this section. The period 2 compounds, NH3, H2O, and HF, break the pattern: they hydrogen bond, so they boil far higher than the trend predicts.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Can it hydrogen bond with itself?

Problem. Which of these can hydrogen bond with itself: (a) H2O; (b) NH3; (c) trimethylamine, N(CH3)3; (d) H2; (e) PH3?

Solution.

  • (a) Yes. H on O makes it a donor; the lone pairs on O make it an acceptor.
  • (b) Yes. H on N (donor) and a lone pair on N (acceptor).
  • (c) No. The N has a lone pair, so it can accept a hydrogen bond, but every H is bonded to C, so there is no donor. (It can accept one from water, though.)
  • (d) No. H2 has the word "hydrogen" in it but no N, O, or F, and it is nonpolar: dispersion only.
  • (e) No. P is not one of F, N, O. The P–H bond has an electronegativity difference of 0.0 (2.1 and 2.1 on the yellow sheet), so PH3 has dispersion forces only.

Check. To hydrogen bond with itself, a molecule needs both a donor (H on F, N, or O) and an acceptor (a lone pair on F, N, or O).

Ion–Dipole Forces

An ion–dipole force is the attraction between an ion and a polar molecule. When NaCl dissolves in water, the δ− oxygen ends of water molecules surround each Na+, and the δ+ hydrogen ends surround each Cl−. Ion–dipole forces are the strongest of the four, and they are why ionic compounds dissolve in water (Chapter 11).

Identifying the Forces

Every substance has dispersion forces. Work down the flowchart to find what else is present; a substance with a stronger force also has all the weaker ones below it.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Which forces?

Problem. List the intermolecular forces in each and name the strongest: (a) CH4; (b) HCl; (c) NH3; (d) CH3OCH3 (dimethyl ether); (e) KBr dissolved in water.

Solution.

  • (a) CH4 is nonpolar (C–H bonds count as nonpolar, and the shape is symmetric): dispersion only.
  • (b) HCl is polar with no H on N, O, or F: dispersion and dipole–dipole (strongest).
  • (c) NH3 has H bonded to N: dispersion, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding (strongest).
  • (d) CH3OCH3 is bent at O and polar, but every H is bonded to C: dispersion and dipole–dipole (strongest).
  • (e) K+ and Br− ions with polar water: ion–dipole (strongest).

Check. For hydrogen bonding, look for an H drawn directly on N, O, or F in the Lewis structure, not just "contains H and O."

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Stronger dispersion forces

Problem. CF4 and CBr4 are both tetrahedral and nonpolar. Which has stronger intermolecular forces and the higher boiling point?

Solution. Both have dispersion forces only. CBr4 has far more electrons (each Br has 35, each F has 9), so it is more polarizable: CBr4 has the stronger dispersion forces and the higher boiling point. (In fact CF4 is a gas at room temperature and CBr4 is a solid.)

Check. When the type of force is the same, compare size: more electrons, stronger dispersion.

Forces between two different substances

When two different substances are mixed, the main force between them is the strongest one they can both take part in. Methanol (CH3OH) and ethanol (CH3CH2OH) both have an O–H, so the dominant force between them is hydrogen bonding, and they mix completely. Methane (CH4) is nonpolar, so it can offer water only dispersion forces and does not dissolve; replace one H with F to make CH3F and the molecule becomes polar, so it can now attract water by dipole forces and dissolves far better. That is the power of substituting a single atom. Section 11.1 turns this into the rule "like dissolves like."

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): The main force in a mixture

Problem. What is the strongest intermolecular force between (a) water and ethanol; (b) hexane (C6H14) and octane (C8H18); (c) water and methane?

Solution. (a) Both have O–H: hydrogen bonding. (b) Both are nonpolar hydrocarbons: dispersion. (c) Methane can offer only dispersion, so water and methane attract by dispersion only (water's hydrogen bonds are with other water molecules, which is why methane does not dissolve well).

Check. The force between two substances can be no stronger than the best force the weaker partner can join in.

Professor Tip

Think of the forces like a subscription plan. Dispersion is the free tier that every molecule gets. Polar molecules upgrade to dipole–dipole and keep dispersion too. Hydrogen bonding is the premium tier: it comes with dipole–dipole and dispersion included. When a question asks for the strongest force, name the top tier; when it asks for all forces, list every tier the molecule has.

Biology Connection: Hydrogen Bonds Hold Life Together

The two strands of DNA are held together by hydrogen bonds between base pairs: adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, and guanine pairs with cytosine through three. Each hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can unzip for copying and reading genes, but millions of them together keep the double helix stable. Hydrogen bonds also hold proteins in their folded shapes; heat or a change in pH can break them and denature the protein, which is why a high fever is dangerous and why cooking changes egg white. Cell membranes, meanwhile, hold together mostly by dispersion forces between the long, nonpolar tails of their lipids.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling a hydrogen bond a covalent bond: it is an attraction between molecules.
  • Calling any molecule with H and O "hydrogen bonding": the H must be bonded directly to N, O, or F.
  • Forgetting dispersion forces: every molecule has them.
  • Thinking boiling breaks covalent bonds: boiling separates molecules; the molecules stay intact.
  • Counting H2, PH3, or N(CH3)3 as hydrogen bonding with itself: it needs an H on F, N, or O (a donor) and a lone pair on F, N, or O (an acceptor).
  • Comparing molar masses across different force types: the size rule decides only between substances with the same strongest force.

Summary

  • Intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules, weaker than bonds.
  • Dispersion: all molecules; stronger with more electrons. Dipole–dipole: polar molecules. Hydrogen bonding: H on N, O, or F. Ion–dipole: ions with polar molecules.
  • For similar sizes: dispersion < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bond < ion–dipole.
  • Bonds are family (hard to break); intermolecular forces are friends (you can leave).
  • Three things decide the strength of dispersion forces: more electrons and larger valence shells (higher polarizability; the coin-flip model), and longer, unbranched shapes give stronger dispersion forces.
  • Hydrogen-bond donor: H on F, N, or O. Acceptor: a lone pair on F, N, or O. See an O–H, think hydrogen bonding.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which of these can hydrogen bond with itself: CH3OH, CH3F, HF, H2, N(CH3)3?

Hint

Look for an H bonded directly to N, O, or F. An H on carbon does not count.

Answer

CH3OH (O–H) and HF (H–F). CH3F and N(CH3)3 have H only on C; H2 is nonpolar.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

What is the strongest intermolecular force in (a) CO2; (b) SO2; (c) HF?

Hint

Decide polar or nonpolar from bond polarity and shape, then check for H on N, O, or F.

Answer

(a) Dispersion (CO2 is linear and nonpolar). (b) Dipole–dipole (S–O bonds are polar and the molecule is bent; there is no H at all). (c) Hydrogen bonding.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Explain why I2 is a solid at room temperature while Cl2 is a gas, even though both are nonpolar.

Hint

Both have dispersion forces only, so compare the number of electrons in each molecule.

Answer

Both have only dispersion forces, but I2 has many more electrons (106 vs 34), so it is more polarizable and its dispersion forces are strong enough to hold it as a solid.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

In a hydrogen bond between a water molecule and an ammonia molecule, the H on water's O points at the lone pair on ammonia's N. Which molecule is the donor and which is the acceptor?

Hint

The donor supplies the H bonded to N, O, or F; the acceptor supplies the lone pair.

Answer

Water is the donor (it supplies the H bonded to O). Ammonia is the acceptor (it supplies the lone pair on N).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Use the coin-flip model to explain why krypton (36 electrons) boils at a higher temperature than neon (10 electrons).

Hint

Use the coin-flip table: is an exactly even split of electrons more or less likely with more electrons? What does a lopsided cloud do to dispersion forces?

Answer

With 36 electrons, the chance of an exactly even split is only about 13%, versus about 25% for neon's 10. Krypton's electron cloud is lopsided more often (it is more polarizable), so it forms instantaneous dipoles more often, has stronger dispersion forces, and needs a higher temperature to boil.
