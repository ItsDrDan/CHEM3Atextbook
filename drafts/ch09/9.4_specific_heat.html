Learning Objectives

  • Define specific heat capacity and compare substances by their specific heats.
  • Use q = mcΔT to find heat, mass, specific heat, or a temperature change.
  • Apply "heat lost = heat gained" to a simple calorimetry problem.

On a summer afternoon the sand at a lake burns your feet while the water stays cool, even though both have had the same sunshine all day. The difference is a property called specific heat capacity.

Specific Heat Capacity

The specific heat capacity (c), or simply specific heat, of a substance is the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of it by 1 °C. Its units are J/(g·°C), also written J g−1 °C−1. A substance with a high specific heat needs a lot of heat to warm up and releases a lot as it cools; a substance with a low specific heat changes temperature quickly.

Water's specific heat comes from the hydrogen bonds between its molecules (Section 9.6): some of the added energy goes into stretching and breaking those attractions instead of speeding the molecules up. Ice and steam have lower specific heats, about 2.09 J/(g·°C) and 2.01 J/(g·°C).

The Heat Equation: q = mcΔT

The heat absorbed or released when a substance changes temperature, without changing state, is

\( q = m \times c \times \Delta T \qquad \text{where } \Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial} \)

Here q is heat (J), m is mass (g), c is specific heat [J/(g·°C)], and ΔT is the temperature change (°C). Because a kelvin and a Celsius degree are the same size, ΔT is the same in K or °C. Always subtract final minus initial: warming gives a positive ΔT and a positive q (heat absorbed); cooling gives a negative ΔT and a negative q (heat released).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Warming IV fluid

Problem. A fluid warmer heats 245 g of IV fluid from 22.0 °C to 37.0 °C. Treating the fluid as water, how much heat does it absorb?

Solution. ΔT = 37.0 − 22.0 = 15.0 °C.

\( q = (245\ \text{g})\left(4.184\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}}\right)(15.0\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = 1.54 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J} = 15.4\ \text{kJ} \)

Check. The grams and °C cancel, leaving joules. q is positive because the fluid warms.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): A cooling skillet

Problem. A 125 g iron skillet cools from 185.0 °C to 25.0 °C. How much heat does it release? (c for iron = 0.449 J/(g·°C))

Solution. ΔT = 25.0 − 185.0 = −160.0 °C.

\( q = (125\ \text{g})(0.449)(-160.0\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = -8.98 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J} \)

Check. The negative sign means heat is released; the skillet releases 8.98 kJ.

Solving for other quantities

Rearrange the same equation for whatever is unknown: \( \Delta T = \dfrac{q}{mc} \), \( c = \dfrac{q}{m\,\Delta T} \), \( m = \dfrac{q}{c\,\Delta T} \).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Final temperature

Problem. A 45.0 g block of aluminum at 21.5 °C absorbs 525 J. What is its final temperature? (c for aluminum = 0.903 J/(g·°C))

Solution.

\( \Delta T = \dfrac{q}{mc} = \dfrac{525\ \text{J}}{(45.0\ \text{g})(0.903)} = 12.9\ {}^\circ\text{C} \qquad T_\text{final} = 21.5 + 12.9 = 34.4\ {}^\circ\text{C} \)

Check. Heat was absorbed, so the final temperature is higher than the initial one.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Identifying a metal

Problem. A 32.6 g piece of an unknown metal absorbs 296 J as it warms from 20.4 °C to 44.0 °C. Find its specific heat and identify it from Figure 1.

Solution. ΔT = 44.0 − 20.4 = 23.6 °C.

\( c = \dfrac{q}{m\,\Delta T} = \dfrac{296\ \text{J}}{(32.6\ \text{g})(23.6\ {}^\circ\text{C})} = 0.385\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}} \)

This matches copper.

Check. The units of the answer are J/(g·°C), the units of specific heat.

Calorimetry: Heat Lost = Heat Gained

A calorimeter is an insulated container, such as a foam cup with a lid, in which heat can move between objects but not escape. By conservation of energy, the heat released by the hot object equals the heat absorbed by the cold one:

\( -q_\text{hot} = q_\text{cold} \qquad \text{or} \qquad q_\text{hot} + q_\text{cold} = 0 \)

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Hot metal in water

Problem. An 80.0 g piece of metal at 98.0 °C is dropped into 50.0 g of water at 20.0 °C in a calorimeter. Both end at 40.0 °C. Find the specific heat of the metal and identify it.

Solution. Heat gained by the water:

\( q_\text{water} = (50.0\ \text{g})(4.184)(40.0 - 20.0\ {}^\circ\text{C}) = 4.18 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J} \)

So qmetal = −4.18 × 103 J, and ΔTmetal = 40.0 − 98.0 = −58.0 °C:

\( c_\text{metal} = \dfrac{q}{m\,\Delta T} = \dfrac{-4184\ \text{J}}{(80.0\ \text{g})(-58.0\ {}^\circ\text{C})} = 0.902\ \dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}} \)

This is aluminum (0.903).

Check. The two negative signs cancel, so the specific heat is positive, as it must be. The metal's temperature changed far more than the water's because its specific heat is much lower.

Professor Tip

Many students remember q = mcΔT as the "MCAT" equation (m, c, ΔT). Before you plug in, write a given/wanted list: m, c, Ti, Tf, q. Check that mass is in grams and heat is in joules (convert kJ first). Then decide the sign: is the object warming (+) or cooling (−)? This equation is only for temperature changes; during melting or boiling the temperature does not change, and Section 9.5 uses a different equation.

Clinical Connection: Nursing and Surgical Care

Because the body is mostly water, warming or cooling it takes a lot of heat, and losing heat is a real risk in surgery. Operating rooms are kept cool, anesthesia reduces the body's ability to shiver, and room-temperature IV fluids and blood products absorb heat from the patient as they warm to body temperature. Each liter of fluid infused at room temperature takes tens of thousands of joules from the body as it warms (compare Example 1). That is why perioperative teams use fluid warmers and forced-air warming blankets and monitor core temperature: even mild perioperative hypothermia (low body temperature around surgery) is linked to more bleeding and slower recovery. (Context only; follow the care plan and equipment instructions.)

Biology Connection: Water as a Thermal Buffer

About 60% of an adult's body mass is water, and water's high specific heat makes the body resist sudden temperature changes. A burst of metabolic heat from exercise warms the body only gradually, giving thermoregulation time to respond. The same property steadies the temperature of lakes, oceans, and the cells of every organism, and it is part of why coastal climates are milder than inland ones.

Valley Connection: Protecting Citrus from Frost

The citrus belt along the eastern side of the San Joaquin Valley, in Tulare, Fresno, and Kern counties, grows a large share of California's oranges and mandarins, and a hard freeze can ruin a crop in one night. Growers use chemistry to fight back. Wind machines mix warmer air from above into the cold layer near the ground on still nights, and growers irrigate before a predicted freeze. University of California Cooperative Extension advisors explain that moist soil absorbs and stores more heat during the day than dry soil and releases it at night, keeping the air around the trees slightly warmer. That stored heat comes from water's high specific heat, the same property you just used in q = mcΔT.

Sources: University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources, frost protection guidance for citrus and subtropical trees (ucanr.edu); California Department of Food and Agriculture, California agricultural statistics (cdfa.ca.gov).

Major Connection: Engineering

Engines, power plants, and data centers are cooled with water or water-based coolants because water absorbs so much heat per gram for each degree it warms. A car's coolant is a water–ethylene glycol mixture: the glycol lowers the freezing point and raises the boiling point, while the water does most of the heat carrying. Engineers size radiators and pumps with the same q = mcΔT you use here, written per second as a rate of heat flow.

Common Mistakes

  • Using Tinitial − Tfinal: ΔT is always final minus initial.
  • Mixing kJ and J: specific heats are per joule, so convert kJ to J first.
  • Using the final temperature as ΔT when the question asks for the final temperature, or the reverse.
  • Using q = mcΔT during a phase change, when the temperature is not changing.

Summary

  • Specific heat c is the heat needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C; water's is 4.184 J/(g·°C).
  • q = mcΔT with ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial; positive q means heat absorbed.
  • In a calorimeter, heat lost by the hot object = heat gained by the cold one.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How much heat is needed to warm 50.0 g of water from 25.0 °C to 80.0 °C?

Answer

q = (50.0)(4.184)(55.0) = 1.15 × 104 J (11.5 kJ).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A 15.0 g gold ring absorbs 125 J. By how much does its temperature rise? (c for gold = 0.128 J/(g·°C))

Answer

ΔT = 125 ÷ (15.0 × 0.128) = 65.1 °C.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Equal masses of water and iron absorb the same amount of heat. Which one has the larger temperature increase, and why?

Answer

The iron. Its specific heat (0.449) is much lower than water's (4.184), so the same heat raises its temperature about 9 times as much.
