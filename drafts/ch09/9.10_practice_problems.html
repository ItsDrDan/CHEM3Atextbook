9.1 Energy and Its Forms

Problem 9.1

Which of these is mainly potential energy?

A. Red blood cells moving through an artery
B. Glucose dissolved in a sports drink
C. The fast-moving water molecules in the steam inside an autoclave
D. A spinning centrifuge rotor in a clinical lab

Hint

Which choices describe something moving? Potential energy is stored energy, such as the energy in the arrangement of a molecule's atoms.

Answer

B. Glucose stores chemical potential energy in the arrangement of its atoms. Nothing is happening to that energy yet; your cells release it later in cellular respiration.

  • A is kinetic energy: the cells are moving with the blood.
  • C is kinetic energy too. The random motion of particles is thermal energy, a kind of kinetic energy; the hotter the steam, the faster its molecules move.
  • D is kinetic energy: the rotor is moving. It took electrical energy to get it spinning, but right now its energy is energy of motion.

Problem 9.2

Classify the main kind of energy in each as kinetic or potential: (a) ATP stored in a resting muscle cell; (b) almonds falling to the ground when a mechanical shaker shakes the tree at harvest; (c) water held in an elevated storage tank above an orchard; (d) the moving molecules of a warm blanket. (e) Describe the energy changes from the moment an almond lets go of its branch until it lands and stops.

Hint

For each, ask: is it moving (kinetic), or is the energy stored by position or chemical arrangement (potential)? For (e), follow the energy as the almond falls and stops; energy is never destroyed.

Answer

(a) Potential (chemical energy stored in ATP's bonds). (b) Kinetic (motion). (c) Potential (energy of position; it becomes kinetic energy when the water flows down the pipes). (d) Kinetic (thermal energy, the random motion of its molecules).

(e) The almond starts with gravitational potential energy. As it falls, potential energy turns into kinetic energy. When it hits the ground and stops, the kinetic energy becomes heat (a tiny warming of the almond and the soil) and sound. No energy is destroyed; it changes form at every step.

Problem 9.3

A car brakes from highway speed to a stop. What happens to its kinetic energy?

A. It is destroyed, because the car is no longer moving.
B. It is turned back into chemical potential energy in the gasoline.
C. It becomes temperature, which is a form of energy.
D. It becomes heat in the brakes, tires, and air, so the total energy is unchanged.

Hint

Apply the law of conservation of energy to each choice. Can energy be destroyed? Is temperature a form of energy or a measurement?

Answer

D. By the law of conservation of energy, energy changes form but is never created or destroyed. Friction in the brakes turns the car's kinetic energy into heat, which is why brakes get hot after a hard stop.

  • A breaks the law of conservation of energy. The car's kinetic energy is gone, but the energy itself has moved into the brakes and surroundings.
  • B is backward. Burning gasoline is a one-way chemical change; ordinary brakes cannot rebuild fuel molecules.
  • C confuses temperature with energy. The brakes' temperature rises because their particles gain kinetic energy, but temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy, not a form of energy itself.

Problem 9.4

Describe what happens to the kinetic energy and the potential energy of the water molecules in each case: (a) liquid water warms from 10.0 °C to 30.0 °C; (b) ice melts at 0 °C.

Hint

Temperature tracks the average kinetic energy; the distance between molecules tracks potential energy. Does the temperature change in (a)? In (b)?

Answer

(a) The temperature rises, so the molecules move faster: their average kinetic energy increases. They stay liquid and about as close together as before, so their potential energy changes very little.

(b) The temperature stays at 0 °C, so the average kinetic energy does not change. The added energy loosens the molecules from their fixed places in the ice, pulling them slightly apart against their attractions: their potential energy increases. This is why a phase change happens at a constant temperature (Section 9.5).

Problem 9.5

An outdoor swimming pool holds water at 27.0 °C, and a mug of tea is at 70.0 °C. (a) In which do the water molecules have the higher average kinetic energy? (b) If both cool to 20.0 °C, which releases more heat? Explain how both answers can be true.

Hint

(a) Temperature measures the average kinetic energy per particle. (b) Heat released depends on mass too (q = mcΔT): compare how much water each holds.

Answer

(a) The tea. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles, and the tea is hotter.

(b) The pool. It cools by only 7.0 °C, but it holds millions of times more water than the mug, and the heat released depends on the mass as well as on the temperature change (q = mcΔT, Section 9.4).

Temperature is an average per particle; the thermal energy a sample can give off depends on how many particles there are. That is the difference between temperature and heat.

9.2 Temperature and Temperature Scales

Problem 9.6

Mateo Herrera, 34, is an irrigation technician on a Fresno County almond orchard. He has asthma. On a July afternoon he is checking drip lines while the air temperature reaches 104 °F. (a) Convert 104 °F to °C and to K. (b) Compare the air temperature with a normal body temperature of 37.0 °C. Which way does heat flow between the air and his body? (c) Explain, in terms of energy and molecules, why sweating cools him. (d) Why does sweating cool him better on a dry Valley afternoon than on a humid day?

Hint

(a) °C = (°F − 32)/1.8, then add 273.15; watch significant figures after subtracting 32. (b) Heat flows from hot to cold. (c) Evaporation absorbs heat. (d) Humid air already holds water vapor.

Answer

(a) \( {}^\circ\text{C} = \dfrac{104 - 32}{1.8} = \dfrac{72}{1.8} = 40.\;{}^\circ\text{C} \qquad \text{K} = 40. + 273.15 = 313\;\text{K} \)

Subtracting the exact 32 leaves 72, which has no decimal places and two significant figures, so the Celsius answer is 40. °C (the decimal point shows that the zero counts). Adding 273.15 keeps the ones place: 313 K.

(b) The air (40. °C) is warmer than his body (37.0 °C), so heat flows from the air into his body. On a day like this he cannot lose heat to the air by simply being cooler than it; evaporating sweat becomes his main way to shed heat. The day is also well above the 95 °F mark at which Cal/OSHA's high-heat procedures apply (chapter introduction).

(c) Evaporation is endothermic. The water molecules in sweat that escape into the air are the fastest ones, and they need energy to break away from their hydrogen-bonded neighbors. That energy, the heat of vaporization (about 2.4 kJ per gram at skin temperature), comes from his skin, so the skin and the blood just beneath it cool. The molecules left behind have a lower average kinetic energy, which is a lower temperature.

(d) Sweat evaporates quickly into dry air. In humid air, which already holds a lot of water vapor, evaporation is slow, so more sweat drips off without evaporating, and dripping sweat carries away very little heat. (Context only; this is not medical guidance.)

Problem 9.7

A patient's temperature is 38.6 °C. What is it in °F?

A. 3.7 °F
B. 53.4 °F
C. 101.5 °F
D. 127.1 °F

Hint

Use °F = 1.8(°C) + 32. A fever in °F should be a little above 98.6 °F.

Answer

C.

\( {}^\circ\text{F} = 1.8(38.6) + 32 = 69.5 + 32 = 101.5\;{}^\circ\text{F} \)

Multiplying by the exact 1.8 keeps three significant figures (69.5), and adding the exact 32 keeps one decimal place. The answer makes sense: 38.6 °C is above the 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) commonly used as the fever threshold.

  • A uses the °F-to-°C formula in the wrong direction: (38.6 − 32)/1.8. A temperature of 3.7 °F would be well below freezing.
  • B divides by 1.8 instead of multiplying (38.6/1.8 + 32). A Fahrenheit degree is smaller than a Celsius degree, so the Fahrenheit number grows by a factor of 1.8.
  • D adds 32 before multiplying, 1.8(38.6 + 32). The formula multiplies first, then adds 32.

Problem 9.8

A hiker rescued from a cold night in the Sierra Nevada has a core body temperature of 33.2 °C. (a) Convert this temperature to °F and to K. (b) Is it below the commonly used hypothermia threshold of 35.0 °C (95.0 °F)?

Hint

°F = 1.8(°C) + 32 and K = °C + 273.15; in the Kelvin sum, keep the decimal places of the measurement. Then compare with 35.0 °C.

Answer

(a) \( {}^\circ\text{F} = 1.8(33.2) + 32 = 59.8 + 32 = 91.8\;{}^\circ\text{F} \qquad \text{K} = 33.2 + 273.15 = 306.4\;\text{K} \)

The answers are 91.8 °F and 306.4 K (the sum 306.35 rounds to one decimal place, like 33.2).

(b) Yes. 33.2 °C is 1.8 °C below 35.0 °C, and 91.8 °F is 3.2 °F below 95.0 °F; the two differences agree because 1.8 × 1.8 °C = 3.2 °F. (Context only; follow facility policy for thresholds and actions.)

Problem 9.9

Hospitals store oxygen for patients as a liquid in large insulated tanks. Liquid oxygen boils at −183 °C. What is this temperature in kelvins?

A. 90. K
B. 456 K
C. −90. K
D. −183 K

Hint

K = °C + 273.15; keep the negative sign of the Celsius value. Can a Kelvin temperature ever be negative?

Answer

A.

\( \text{K} = -183 + 273.15 = 90.\;\text{K} \)

The sum 90.15 is rounded to the ones place, like −183, and the decimal point in "90." shows that the zero is significant.

  • B adds 183 to 273.15, dropping the negative sign. A temperature below 0 °C must be below 273.15 K.
  • C subtracts in the wrong order (−183 + 273.15 is positive, not negative). Kelvin temperatures are never negative, since 0 K is absolute zero.

Problem 9.10

After a dose of a fever reducer, a child's temperature drops by 2.5 °F. By how many degrees Celsius and by how many kelvins did it drop?

Hint

This is a change in temperature, not a reading: do not subtract 32 or add 273.15. A Celsius degree is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees; a kelvin is the same size as a Celsius degree.

Answer

For a change in temperature, use only the size of the degrees; do not subtract 32 or add 273.15. A Celsius degree is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees, so

\( \Delta T = \dfrac{2.5\;{}^\circ\text{F}}{1.8} = 1.4\;{}^\circ\text{C} = 1.4\;\text{K} \)

The temperature dropped by 1.4 °C, which is also 1.4 K, because a kelvin is the same size as a Celsius degree. Using the full formula, (2.5 − 32)/1.8 = −16.4 °C, would convert a temperature of 2.5 °F, not a change. (Context only; this is not dosing guidance.)

Problem 9.11

Rank these temperatures from coldest to hottest: 25.0 °C, 80.0 °F, 305 K.

Hint

Convert all three to the same scale (°C is easiest), then compare.

Answer

Convert everything to °C:

  • 80.0 °F: (80.0 − 32)/1.8 = 48.0/1.8 = 26.7 °C
  • 305 K: 305 − 273.15 = 31.85, which rounds to the ones place as 32 °C

Coldest to hottest: 25.0 °C < 80.0 °F < 305 K. The biggest number (305) is the hottest here, but only after converting can you tell; 80.0 °F is a warm day, while 80.0 °C would be close to boiling water.

9.3 Heat, Units of Energy, and the Direction of Heat Flow

Problem 9.12

A medium banana provides about 105 Cal of food energy. How many kilojoules is that?

A. 0.439 kJ
B. 25.1 kJ
C. 439 kJ
D. 4.39 × 105 kJ

Hint

A food Calorie is 1000 cal, and 1 Cal = 4.184 kJ. Should the number of kilojoules be bigger or smaller than the number of Calories?

Answer

C.

\( 105\;\text{Cal} \times \dfrac{4184\;\text{J}}{1\;\text{Cal}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{kJ}}{1000\;\text{J}} = 439\;\text{kJ} \)

Since 1 Cal = 4.184 kJ, the number of kilojoules is a little more than 4 times the number of Calories.

  • A treats the food Calorie as a small calorie: 105 cal × 4.184 J/cal = 439 J = 0.439 kJ. A food Calorie is 1000 cal.
  • B divides by 4.184 instead of multiplying. A kilojoule is smaller than a Calorie, so there must be more kilojoules than Calories.
  • D is the answer in joules (4.39 × 105 J) labeled as kilojoules; the last step, dividing by 1000 J/kJ, was skipped.

Problem 9.13

An instant hot pack releases 12.5 kJ of heat as its salt dissolves. (a) Express this heat in calories and in food Calories. (b) What is the sign of q for the dissolving salt (the system)?

Hint

(a) kJ → J, then ÷ 4.184 J/cal; 1 Cal = 1000 cal. (b) The sign of q is written from the system's point of view: heat released is negative.

Answer

(a) \( 12.5\;\text{kJ} \times \dfrac{1000\;\text{J}}{1\;\text{kJ}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{cal}}{4.184\;\text{J}} = 2.99 \times 10^{3}\;\text{cal} = 2.99\;\text{Cal} \)

(a) 2.99 × 103 cal, which is 2.99 Cal (1 Cal = 1000 cal). Scientific notation keeps three significant figures, like 12.5.

(b) The pack releases heat, so the process is exothermic and q = −12.5 kJ. Your hand, part of the surroundings, absorbs that heat and feels warm.

Problem 9.14

Which is the largest amount of energy?

A. 2.50 kJ
B. 500. cal
C. 0.750 Cal
D. 2.80 × 103 J

Hint

Convert every choice to joules (1 cal = 4.184 J; 1 Cal = 4184 J), then compare. The biggest number is not always the most energy.

Answer

C. Convert each to joules: 0.750 Cal × 4184 J/Cal = 3138 J, or 3.14 × 103 J, the largest.

  • A is 2.50 kJ = 2500 J (2.50 × 103 J).
  • B has the biggest number, but calories are larger than joules by only a factor of 4.184: 500. cal × 4.184 J/cal = 2092 J (2.09 × 103 J), the smallest of the four.
  • D is 2.80 × 103 J, more than A and B but less than C.

Problem 9.15

Which process releases heat to its surroundings?

A. Sweat evaporating from the skin
B. An instant cold pack being activated
C. Water from sprinklers freezing on almond blossoms on a frosty night
D. Dry ice subliming in a box used to ship vaccines

Hint

Name the process in each choice. Does it pull particles apart or bring them together, and which of those releases heat to the surroundings?

Answer

C. Freezing is exothermic (q < 0 for the water): as the molecules lock into the ice structure, they release heat to the blossoms and the air around them. Some growers use this on purpose to protect blossoms on frosty nights.

  • A is endothermic: evaporating sweat absorbs heat from the skin, which is why it cools you.
  • B is endothermic: the dissolving solid absorbs heat from its surroundings, so the pack feels cold.
  • D is endothermic: sublimation (solid to gas) separates the CO2 molecules completely, which takes energy. Dry ice keeps the box cold by absorbing heat from its contents.

Problem 9.16

For each process, name the system, say whether it is exothermic or endothermic, and give the sign of q for the system: (a) a bag of IV fluid warming in a fluid warmer; (b) a cup of hot coffee cooling on the counter at a nurses' station; (c) rubbing alcohol evaporating from a patient's arm. (d) A patient with an ice pack says, "The cold is flowing into my ankle." Restate this correctly in terms of heat.

Hint

The system is the thing that changes. If it warms or turns to gas, it absorbed heat (q > 0); if it cools, q < 0. For (d), which way does heat really flow?

Answer
  • (a) System: the IV fluid. It absorbs heat from the warmer: endothermic, q > 0.
  • (b) System: the coffee. It releases heat to the cup and the air: exothermic, q < 0.
  • (c) System: the evaporating alcohol. It absorbs heat from the skin as it vaporizes: endothermic, q > 0. The arm, part of the surroundings, loses that heat and feels cool.

(d) "Cold" is not a thing that flows. Heat flows out of the ankle into the ice pack, because heat always moves from the warmer object to the colder one. The ankle feels cold because it is losing heat; the ice absorbs that heat and melts.

9.4 Specific Heat Capacity

Problem 9.17

A warm compress is made by soaking a cloth in 225 g of water heated from 21.0 °C to 45.0 °C. How much heat did the water absorb, in joules and in kilojoules?

Hint

ΔT = final − initial; then q = mcΔT with c = 4.184 J/(g·°C). Divide by 1000 for kilojoules.

Answer

ΔT = 45.0 − 21.0 = 24.0 °C.

\( q = (225\;\text{g})\left(4.184\;\dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}}\right)(24.0\;{}^\circ\text{C}) = 2.26 \times 10^{4}\;\text{J} = 22.6\;\text{kJ} \)

The water absorbed 2.26 × 104 J (22.6 kJ). The grams and degrees cancel, leaving joules, and q is positive because the water warmed. (Context only; follow facility policy for compress temperatures.)

Problem 9.18

A 455 g length of copper pipe cools from 65.0 °C to 22.5 °C. What is q for the pipe?

A. +7.44 × 103 J
B. −7.44 × 103 J
C. −3.94 × 103 J
D. −8.09 × 104 J

Hint

Use q = mcΔT with c for copper, 0.385 J/(g·°C), and ΔT = final − initial. A cooling object releases heat, so what sign should q have?

Answer

B. ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial = 22.5 − 65.0 = −42.5 °C.

\( q = (455\;\text{g})\left(0.385\;\dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}}\right)(-42.5\;{}^\circ\text{C}) = -7.44 \times 10^{3}\;\text{J} \)

The negative sign means the pipe releases 7.44 kJ of heat as it cools.

  • A has the right size but the wrong sign; it comes from subtracting initial minus final. A cooling object releases heat, so q is negative.
  • C uses the final temperature, 22.5 °C, as if it were ΔT.
  • D uses the specific heat of water (4.184) instead of copper's (0.385). Use the specific heat of the substance whose temperature is changing.

Problem 9.19

A 75.0 g silver spoon at 22.0 °C is placed in a pot of hot soup and absorbs 1.25 kJ of heat. What is the spoon's final temperature? (c for silver = 0.235 J/(g·°C))

Hint

Change kJ to J first, because c is in joules. Solve q = mcΔT for ΔT, then add ΔT to the starting temperature.

Answer

Convert kJ to J first, because the specific heat is in joules: 1.25 kJ = 1250 J.

\( \Delta T = \dfrac{q}{mc} = \dfrac{1250\;\text{J}}{(75.0\;\text{g})(0.235\;\text{J/(g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C)})} = 70.9\;{}^\circ\text{C} \qquad T_\text{final} = 22.0 + 70.9 = 92.9\;{}^\circ\text{C} \)

The spoon ends at 92.9 °C. A modest amount of heat raises its temperature a lot because silver's specific heat is low, which is why a metal spoon left in hot soup is too hot to hold.

9.5 Heat of Phase Changes and Heating Curves

Problem 9.25

On the heating curve for water, heat is still being added while ice melts at 0 °C, but the temperature does not change. Where does that heat go?

A. It breaks the O–H covalent bonds inside the water molecules.
B. Nowhere; since ΔT = 0, q = mcΔT shows that no heat is absorbed.
C. It makes the molecules move faster, raising their kinetic energy.
D. It overcomes the attractions holding the molecules in the ice, raising their potential energy.

Hint

On a plateau the temperature, and so the average kinetic energy, stays the same. Does melting break bonds inside H2O molecules, or separate the molecules from each other?

Answer

D. During a phase change, the added heat goes into separating the molecules against their intermolecular attractions (hydrogen bonds in ice). Their potential energy rises while their average kinetic energy, and so the temperature, stays the same. The heat is q = nΔHfus.

  • A confuses intermolecular forces with bonds. Melting separates whole H2O molecules from each other; each molecule keeps its O–H bonds.
  • B uses q = mcΔT on a plateau. That equation only describes temperature changes; a phase change absorbs heat even though ΔT = 0.
  • C would make the temperature rise, since temperature measures average kinetic energy. On a plateau it does not.

(d) Why not simply warm the sample until the water evaporates?

Hint

(a) Solid straight to gas. (b) Does separating molecules completely take in or give off heat? (c) Compare the ice's vapor pressure with the surrounding pressure. (d) What would warming do to the sample?

Answer

(a) Sublimation, solid → gas without melting.

(b) Endothermic, so q > 0 for the ice: separating the molecules completely takes energy. (Freeze-dryers supply gentle heat to keep the process going.)

(c) Ice has a small vapor pressure even below 0 °C. When the pressure around the sample is lowered below that vapor pressure, water molecules leave the ice faster than they return, so the ice sublimes away while the sample stays frozen.

(d) Warming the sample enough to evaporate the water would first melt it and then hold it at higher temperatures for a long time. Heat can denature proteins by breaking the hydrogen bonds that hold their shape (Section 9.6), and it can kill the microbes being preserved. Freeze-drying removes the water while everything stays cold.

Problem 9.50

Rank from lowest to highest melting point: KCl, N2, SiC, CH3OH. Explain your reasoning.

Hint

Classify each solid first (molecular, ionic, covalent network). Molecular solids melt lowest and covalent networks highest; compare the two molecular solids by their strongest force.

Answer

Classify the solids first, then compare forces within a type:

  • N2: molecular, nonpolar, dispersion only.
  • CH3OH: molecular, hydrogen bonding (O–H).
  • KCl: ionic (K+ and Cl−, held by ionic bonds).
  • SiC: covalent network (covalent bonds throughout).

N2 < CH3OH < KCl < SiC

The two molecular solids melt lowest because only intermolecular forces must be overcome, and hydrogen bonding puts methanol above nitrogen. Melting KCl requires separating ions held by ionic bonds, and melting SiC requires breaking covalent bonds, so the network solid is highest.

Problem 9.51

(c) Which segment needs the most heat, and what fraction of the total is it? (d) Express the total in food Calories.

\( q_4 = \dfrac{40.0\;\text{g}}{18.015\;\text{g/mol}} \times 40.7\;\text{kJ/mol} = 90.4\;\text{kJ} \)

(b) \( q_\text{total} = 1.00 + 13.4 + 16.7 + 90.4 + 1.21 = 122.7\;\text{kJ} \)

(b) The total is 122.7 kJ (the sum keeps the tenths place of the least precise terms).

(c) Boiling (q4): 90.4/122.7 = 0.737, about 74% of the total, the longest segment on the curve.

(d) \( 122.7\;\text{kJ} \times \dfrac{1000\;\text{J}}{1\;\text{kJ}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{Cal}}{4184\;\text{J}} = 29.3\;\text{Cal} \)

Problem 9.52

A pot holding 1.20 × 103 g of soup (treat it as water) cools on the stove from 140.0 °F to 95.0 °F. (a) Convert both temperatures to °C. (b) Find q for the soup in kJ. (c) Express the heat released in food Calories. (d) Show that you get the same ΔT in °C by converting the change of 45.0 °F directly.

Hint

(a) °C = (°F − 32)/1.8. (b) q = mcΔT with ΔT = final − initial. (c) Divide kJ by 4.184 kJ/Cal. (d) For a change, just divide the °F change by 1.8.

Answer

(a) \( \dfrac{140.0 - 32}{1.8} = \dfrac{108.0}{1.8} = 60.00\;{}^\circ\text{C} \qquad \dfrac{95.0 - 32}{1.8} = \dfrac{63.0}{1.8} = 35.0\;{}^\circ\text{C} \)

(a) 60.00 °C (108.0 has four significant figures) and 35.0 °C.

(b) \( q = (1.20 \times 10^{3}\;\text{g})(4.184)(35.0 - 60.00\;{}^\circ\text{C}) = (1.20 \times 10^{3})(4.184)(-25.0) = -1.26 \times 10^{5}\;\text{J} = -126\;\text{kJ} \)

(b) q = −126 kJ; the negative sign means the soup releases heat. The subtraction gives −25.0 (tenths place), and three significant figures limit the product.

(c) 1.2552 × 105 J ÷ 4184 J/Cal = 30.0 Cal released.

(d) ΔT = 45.0 °F ÷ 1.8 = 25.0 °C, the same size as 60.00 − 35.0. For a change, divide by 1.8 but do not subtract 32.

Problem 9.53

In a lab modeled on Part 2 of Experiment 7a, a student adds ice at 0.0 °C to warm water in a foam-cup calorimeter, stirs until all the ice melts, and records the lowest temperature. The table below gives the data.

(b) −8.95 × 103 J (the water lost 8.95 kJ); (c) 1.27 × 103 J; (d) 7.69 × 103 J; (e) 340. J/g, or 6.13 kJ/mol, a 1.8% error. Problem 9.54

A nursing student compares three liquids on the back of a hand: water (H2O), isopropyl alcohol (rubbing alcohol, (CH3)2CHOH), and acetone (CH3COCH3). (a) Name the strongest intermolecular force in each. (b) Rank them from lowest to highest boiling point. (c) Rank them from highest to lowest vapor pressure at skin temperature. (d) Which disappears from the skin first, and which lingers longest? (e) Why does each liquid feel cool as it evaporates?

Hint

Find each liquid's strongest force: two O–H, one O–H, or a C=O with no O–H. Stronger forces mean a higher boiling point, a lower vapor pressure, and slower evaporation. Evaporation absorbs heat.

Answer

(a) Water: hydrogen bonding (two O–H). Isopropyl alcohol: hydrogen bonding (one O–H). Acetone: dipole–dipole (polar C=O, but all H on carbon).

(b) Acetone < isopropyl alcohol < water (about 56 °C, 82 °C, and 100 °C). Acetone has no hydrogen bonding. Isopropyl alcohol hydrogen bonds, but each molecule has only one O–H; water forms the most hydrogen bonds per molecule, even though it is the smallest molecule of the three.

(c) The reverse order: acetone > isopropyl alcohol > water.

(d) Acetone evaporates first; water lingers longest.

(e) Evaporation is endothermic. The escaping molecules absorb their heat of vaporization from the skin, and the fastest molecules leave first, lowering the average kinetic energy of what remains. Heat flows from the skin into the evaporating liquid, so the skin cools.

Problem 9.55

Compare a burn from steam with a burn from boiling water. (a) How much heat is released when 2.50 g of steam at 100.0 °C condenses on skin and the water then cools to 37.0 °C? (b) How much heat is released when 2.50 g of liquid water at 100.0 °C cools to 37.0 °C? (c) About how many times as much heat does the steam deliver? (d) Explain the difference using the heating curve.

Hint

(a) Two steps: condense (q = nΔHvap, released), then cool the water (mcΔT). (b) The cooling step only. (c) Divide (a) by (b). (d) Which plateau does steam pass through?

Answer

(a) Two steps, both releasing heat:

\( q_\text{condense} = -\left(\dfrac{2.50\;\text{g}}{18.015\;\text{g/mol}} \times 40.7\;\text{kJ/mol}\right) = -5.65\;\text{kJ} \)

\( q_\text{cool} = (2.50\;\text{g})(4.184)(37.0 - 100.0\;{}^\circ\text{C}) = (2.50)(4.184)(-63.0) = -659\;\text{J} = -0.659\;\text{kJ} \)

\( q_\text{total} = -5.65 + (-0.659) = -6.31\;\text{kJ} \)

(a) 6.31 kJ released (q = −6.31 kJ for the steam). (b) 0.659 kJ released (q = −0.659 kJ), the cooling step alone.

(c) 6.31 ÷ 0.659 = about 9.6 times as much heat.

(d) Steam must first come down the long boiling plateau, releasing its whole heat of vaporization into the skin at 100 °C, before it can begin to cool along the liquid slope. Problem 9.56

In a foam-cup calorimeter, 25.0 mL of hydrochloric acid (density 1.02 g/mL) is mixed with 25.0 mL of sodium hydroxide solution (density 1.04 g/mL). The temperature rises from 22.3 °C to 28.9 °C. Using c = 4.184 J/(g·°C) for the mixture and the total mass of the solution, what is qreaction?

A. −1.4 kJ
B. +1.4 kJ
C. −0.70 kJ
D. −6.2 kJ

Hint

Mass = each volume × its density, added. ΔT = final − initial; qsolution = mcΔT and qreaction = −qsolution. Watch the significant figures in ΔT.

Answer

A. Total mass: 25.0 × 1.02 = 25.5 g and 25.0 × 1.04 = 26.0 g, so m = 51.5 g. ΔT = 28.9 − 22.3 = 6.6 °C (two significant figures after the subtraction).

\( q_\text{solution} = (51.5\;\text{g})(4.184)(6.6\;{}^\circ\text{C}) = 1.4 \times 10^{3}\;\text{J} \qquad q_\text{reaction} = -q_\text{solution} = -1.4\;\text{kJ} \)

The solution warmed, so the reaction released heat: it is exothermic.

  • B is qsolution, not qreaction. The signs are opposite: heat gained by the solution was lost by the reaction.
  • C uses the mass of only one of the two solutions (25.5 g). The thermometer reads the whole mixture, so m is the total mass.
  • D uses the final temperature, 28.9 °C, as ΔT. ΔT is final minus initial.

Problem 9.63

A puddle of water and a puddle of rubbing alcohol are left on a sidewalk. (a) Using the spread of kinetic energies in a liquid, explain why both evaporate faster at 35 °C than at 15 °C. (b) Which puddle disappears first, and why? (c) Would spreading the water puddle thinner make it disappear faster or slower?

Hint

(a) Picture the hump of kinetic energies and the minimum energy needed to escape. (b) Which liquid forms fewer hydrogen bonds per molecule? (c) Where must a molecule be to escape?

Answer

(a) Only molecules with more than the minimum kinetic energy needed to escape can evaporate. At 35 °C the distribution of kinetic energies shifts to higher values, so a larger fraction of molecules is above that minimum and evaporation is faster.

(b) The rubbing alcohol. Isopropyl alcohol has only one O–H per molecule, so it forms fewer hydrogen bonds than water; its forces are weaker and its vapor pressure is higher (it is more volatile, and you can smell it).

(c) Faster. A thinner, wider puddle has more surface area, so more molecules are at the surface in position to escape.
