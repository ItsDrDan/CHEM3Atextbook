Learning Objectives

  • Define energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy.
  • Identify the kind of energy in everyday and chemical examples.
  • State the law of conservation of energy and describe energy changing form.
  • Explain, with the spring model, why particles "stick" when their attraction is stronger than their kinetic energy.

Food, gasoline, sunlight, a stretched rubber band, and a cup of hot coffee all have something in common: they can make something happen. Chemistry cares about energy because every physical and chemical change either takes it in or gives it out.

What Is Energy?

Energy is the capacity to do work or to supply heat. Work is moving something against a force, such as lifting a box or pushing a piston. Heat is energy that flows from a hotter object to a colder one because of their difference in temperature. Energy comes in two basic kinds.

Kinetic energy

Kinetic energy (KE) is the energy of motion. A rolling ball, flowing water, and a moving car all have kinetic energy, and so do atoms and molecules, which are always moving. The faster something moves and the more mass it has, the more kinetic energy it has. The random motion of the particles in a substance is called thermal energy, and temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of those particles (Section 9.2).

Potential energy

Potential energy (PE) is stored energy, energy due to position or composition.

  • Macroscopic potential energy: water held behind a dam, a book on a high shelf, or a compressed spring can release energy when it moves.
  • Chemical potential energy: the energy stored in the arrangement of atoms, in chemical bonds and in the attractions between particles. Food, gasoline, and batteries store chemical potential energy. Particles that attract each other have lower potential energy when they are close and higher potential energy when they are pulled apart, which is why separating them (melting a solid, boiling a liquid) always takes energy.

Why Cold Particles "Stick"

Temperature measures kinetic energy, so cooling a liquid slows its particles down. But slowing down alone does not explain why a cold liquid eventually turns into a solid. Slow particles could, in principle, just keep drifting around slowly. The missing piece is that cold particles do not only move slower; they stick. The attraction between particles, called an intermolecular force (Section 9.6), is a kind of potential energy.

A spring is a good model. A stretched spring stores a lot of potential energy (let go, and it snaps back). A spring that has relaxed back together stores very little. Two particles that attract each other behave the same way:

  • Close together: low potential energy. This is the "relaxed" position, the bottom of an energy well. The particles are as comfortable as they can be.
  • Pulled apart: high potential energy. It takes energy to pull them out of the well, and the farther apart they go, the more energy it takes.

Now compare the two kinds of energy. Kinetic energy (motion) tries to carry particles away from each other; attraction (potential energy) tries to hold them together. If the attraction is stronger than the particles' kinetic energy, the particles stick, and you get a liquid or a solid. Heat the sample, the kinetic energy grows, and eventually the particles break free: the solid melts or the liquid boils. The stronger the attraction, the deeper the well, and the more energy it takes to pull the particles apart. Weak attractions, such as the dispersion forces between small atoms like helium (Section 9.6), are so shallow that the particles separate easily; strong ones such as hydrogen bonds need much more energy.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Kinetic or potential?

Problem. Identify the main kind of energy in each: (a) a cyclist coasting downhill; (b) a granola bar; (c) steam molecules moving in a kettle; (d) water stored behind Pine Flat Dam.

Solution. (a) Kinetic, motion. (b) Potential, chemical energy stored in the food's molecules. (c) Kinetic, the random motion of the molecules (thermal energy). (d) Potential, energy of position.

Check. Ask, "Is it moving right now?" If yes, kinetic. If the energy is waiting to be released, potential.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): A quick study check

Problem. Kinetic or potential? (a) skateboarding down a ramp; (b) a banana in your backpack; (c) raking leaves; (d) propane in a barbecue tank.

Solution. (a) Kinetic: you and the board are moving. (b) Potential: chemical energy stored in the food. (c) Kinetic: your arms and the rake are moving. (d) Potential: chemical energy stored in the fuel until it burns.

Check. Food and fuel are the classic "potential" answers; anything described with an action verb (skating, raking) is kinetic.

Conservation of Energy

The law of conservation of energy (the first law of thermodynamics) says that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only change from one form to another or move from one object to another. When a car burns gasoline, the chemical potential energy of the fuel becomes the kinetic energy of the car and a great deal of heat. The total is unchanged; only the forms change.

The same law applies on the particle level. When a substance is heated, the added energy either makes its particles move faster (kinetic energy rises, so the temperature rises) or pulls them farther apart (potential energy rises, as in melting or boiling). Sections 9.4 and 9.5 calculate each of these.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Following the energy

Problem. Describe the energy changes when you eat breakfast and then walk to class.

Solution. The food's chemical potential energy is released in your cells during respiration. Part of it moves your muscles (kinetic energy of your body), and the rest becomes heat that keeps you warm. No energy is lost from the universe; it changes form.

Check. Every step names where the energy came from and where it went.

Professor Tip

When a temperature changes, think kinetic energy: the particles are speeding up or slowing down. When the state changes (melting, boiling), think potential energy: the particles are moving apart or coming together, and the temperature stays the same while it happens. This one idea explains the whole heating curve in Section 9.5.

Biology Connection: Energy Changes Form in Your Body

Your body is an energy converter. Digestion breaks food into small molecules such as glucose; cellular respiration releases their chemical potential energy and stores part of it in ATP; ATP powers muscle contraction (kinetic energy), nerve signals, and the building of new molecules. Much of the energy ends up as heat, which is why you warm up during exercise. Conservation of energy is the reason weight change depends on the balance between the energy you take in as food and the energy you use.

Valley Connection: Power from the Kings River

East of Fresno, Pine Flat Dam holds back the Kings River in a reservoir in the Sierra Nevada foothills. The stored water has gravitational potential energy. When water is released through the powerhouse at the base of the dam, it falls, gains kinetic energy, and spins turbines that drive generators, turning that energy into electricity. The same released water then flows downstream to irrigate farms across the Kings River service area. The dam was built by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for flood control and irrigation storage, and the hydroelectric plant just below it is owned and run by the Kings River Conservation District. Every step, from water in the reservoir to electricity in a Valley home, follows the law of conservation of energy; some energy always becomes heat along the way.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing heat and temperature: heat is energy that flows; temperature measures average kinetic energy.
  • Saying energy is "used up": it changes form (often to heat), but the total is conserved.
  • Thinking food or fuel has kinetic energy: the energy is stored (chemical potential energy) until it reacts.
  • Thinking cold particles only slow down: they also stick. Freezing happens when the attraction between particles (potential energy) wins over their kinetic energy.
  • Getting the spring backwards: particles that attract have low potential energy close together and high potential energy pulled apart, so separating them takes energy.

Summary

  • Energy is the capacity to do work or supply heat.
  • Kinetic energy is energy of motion; temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles.
  • Potential energy is stored energy of position or composition, including chemical energy.
  • Energy is conserved: it changes form or moves, but is never created or destroyed.
  • Attracted particles are like a spring: close together = low potential energy, pulled apart = high. If attraction beats kinetic energy, the particles stick (liquid or solid).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify each as mainly kinetic or potential energy: (a) a charged phone battery; (b) wind turning a turbine; (c) a stretched IV tubing clamp spring; (d) warm air rising from a heater.

Hint

Moving now: kinetic. Stored by position, stretch, or chemical arrangement: potential.

Answer

(a) Potential (chemical). (b) Kinetic. (c) Potential (position). (d) Kinetic (moving, fast-moving molecules).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A sample of water at 20 °C is warmed to 60 °C without boiling. What happens to the average kinetic energy of its molecules?

Hint

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles.

Answer

It increases. A higher temperature means the molecules are moving faster on average.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A student says, "When a log burns, its energy is destroyed." Correct the statement.

Hint

Recall the law of conservation of energy. Into what forms does the wood's chemical potential energy change?

Answer

The energy is not destroyed. The chemical potential energy of the wood changes into heat and light that spread into the surroundings; the total energy is conserved.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

When steam condenses to liquid water, does the average distance between the water molecules increase or decrease? Does their potential energy go up or down, and is energy taken in or given off?

Hint

Use the summary grid: molecules that move closer together lose potential energy. Where does that energy go?

Answer

The distance decreases (the molecules come together), so the potential energy goes down, and that energy is given off as heat. This is why steam burns are so severe.
