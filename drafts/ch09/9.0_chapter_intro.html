Chemistry in the Community: Heat on the Job

On a July afternoon in the San Joaquin Valley, the air in an orchard or a vineyard can climb past 100 °F. The people who prune, pick, irrigate, and pack the Valley's crops work through that heat, and their bodies keep their core near 37 °C mainly one way: by sweating. Each gram of sweat that evaporates from the skin carries away more than 2 kJ of heat, one of the largest heat effects of any common liquid.

California treats heat as a workplace hazard. Under Cal/OSHA's heat illness prevention standard for outdoor work (California Code of Regulations, Title 8, Section 3395), employers must provide water, rest, and shade, have shade up whenever the temperature is above 80 °F, and follow extra high-heat procedures at 95 °F or above. Those rules rest on the chemistry in this chapter: how temperature is measured, how much heat it takes to warm or cool something, why evaporation cools, and why water, held together by strong hydrogen bonds, is so good at absorbing heat.

Source: California Department of Industrial Relations, Cal/OSHA Heat Illness Prevention (dir.ca.gov/dosh/heatillnessinfo.html), and Title 8, Section 3395.

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Describe kinetic and potential energy and the law of conservation of energy (Section 9.1).
  • Convert temperatures among the Fahrenheit, Celsius, and Kelvin scales (Section 9.2).
  • Convert among joules, calories, and food Calories, and classify changes as exothermic or endothermic (Section 9.3).
  • Use specific heat capacity (q = mcΔT) to find heat, temperature change, mass, or specific heat (Section 9.4).
  • Calculate the heat of melting, freezing, boiling, and condensing, and read a heating curve (Section 9.5).
  • Identify the intermolecular forces in a substance (Section 9.6).
  • Relate intermolecular forces to the properties of liquids and classify crystalline solids (Section 9.7).
  • Explain vapor pressure, boiling, and sublimation, and rank substances by melting or boiling point (Section 9.8).

Why This Chapter Matters

Every health career uses heat and temperature: reading a fever, warming IV fluids, preventing hypothermia in surgery, humidifying a ventilator's air, sterilizing instruments in an autoclave. Intermolecular forces explain why water boils at 100 °C while methane boils far below zero, why DNA holds its shape, and why rubbing alcohol feels cold on the skin. This chapter brings together the polarity you learned in Chapter 6 and the energy ideas from Chapter 8, and it prepares you for gases (Chapter 10) and solutions (Chapter 11).
