Learning Objectives

  • Relate surface tension, viscosity, and capillary action to intermolecular forces.
  • Distinguish crystalline from amorphous solids.
  • Fill in a property table for a substance with strong or weak intermolecular forces.
  • Classify crystalline solids as ionic, molecular, covalent network, atomic nonbonding, or metallic and predict their melting points and whether they conduct.

Liquids and solids are the condensed states: their particles touch, so intermolecular forces control how they behave. A water strider walking on a pond, honey creeping off a spoon, and a diamond scratching glass are all intermolecular (or interatomic) forces you can see.

Properties of Liquids

  • Surface tension is the energy needed to increase the surface area of a liquid. Molecules inside a liquid are pulled equally in all directions, but those at the surface are pulled only inward and sideways, so the surface acts like a stretched skin. Stronger intermolecular forces give higher surface tension. Water's surface tension, from hydrogen bonding, is high enough to support a water strider and to make drops bead up. Fill a glass just past the brim and the water bulges above the rim without spilling. Astronauts on the International Space Station show it best: in near-weightlessness, with nothing to flatten it, water wrung out of a towel forms a wobbling skin around their hands and floats as a ball.
  • Viscosity is a liquid's resistance to flow. Stronger intermolecular forces, and long molecules that tangle, make a liquid more viscous. Glycerol, with three O–H groups per molecule, is far more viscous than water; motor oil, made of long hydrocarbon chains with large dispersion forces, is more viscous than gasoline. Viscosity drops as temperature rises, because faster molecules overcome their attractions more easily. Race maple syrup, honey, ketchup, and mustard down a tilted plate and the thickest, stickiest one (the one with the strongest attractions between its large molecules) barely moves.
  • Capillary action is a liquid rising in a narrow tube. It happens when the liquid's attraction to the tube walls (adhesion) is stronger than its attraction to itself (cohesion). Water rises in a thin glass tube and wets paper towels this way; it also helps move water up through plant tissues.

Clinics use capillary action every day. For a fingerstick blood test, a health worker touches a thin glass capillary tube to the drop of blood. Adhesion between the blood, which is mostly water, and the glass draws the blood up the tube with no suction.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Comparing liquids

Problem. Which liquid in each pair has the higher surface tension or viscosity? (a) surface tension: water or hexane (C6H14); (b) viscosity: ethanol (CH3CH2OH) or glycerol (C3H5(OH)3).

Solution. (a) Water: it hydrogen bonds, while nonpolar hexane has only dispersion forces. (b) Glycerol: both hydrogen bond, but each glycerol molecule has three O–H groups and forms many more hydrogen bonds.

Check. Identify the strongest force first; if it is the same, compare how many of those attractions each molecule can make, or its size.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Which is most viscous?

Problem. Which liquid would have the highest viscosity: CH4, C2H6, C3H8, CF4, or CCl4 (each compared as a liquid)?

Solution. All five are nonpolar (symmetric tetrahedral carbons, or C–H chains), so all have dispersion forces only. Compare electrons: CH4 10, C2H6 18, C3H8 26, CF4 42, CCl4 74. CCl4 has the most electrons, so it is the most polarizable, has the strongest dispersion forces, and is the most viscous.

Check. Each Cl alone has 17 electrons, almost as many as a whole C2H6 molecule.

Biology Connection: Surfactant in the Lungs

Each alveolus, the tiny air sac where gas exchange happens, is lined with a thin film of water. Water's high surface tension would pull these small sacs closed when you breathe out. Cells in the alveoli secrete pulmonary surfactant, a mix of lipids and proteins that spreads at the water–air surface and lowers the surface tension, keeping the alveoli open with much less effort. Babies born very prematurely may not yet make enough surfactant, which causes neonatal respiratory distress syndrome; one treatment is surfactant given directly into the airway.

Crystalline and Amorphous Solids

In a crystalline solid, the particles are arranged in a regular, repeating three-dimensional pattern called a crystal lattice; table salt, ice, quartz, and metals are crystalline. In an amorphous solid, such as glass, rubber, or most plastics, the particles have no long-range order. Crystalline solids melt sharply at one temperature, while amorphous solids soften gradually over a range.

Table 2 summarizes each type. Diamond, quartz, and solid noble gases are all called atomic solids because their lattices are made of single atoms, but they could hardly be more different. In a covalent network (covalent atomic) solid the atoms are joined by covalent bonds, so it melts very high. In an atomic nonbonding solid, such as solid argon or solid xenon, the atoms are held only by dispersion forces, so it melts far below room temperature. Of these types, only metals conduct as solids (graphite is a rare exception), because their electrons are free to move.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Classifying solids

Problem. Classify each solid: (a) KBr; (b) solid CO2 (dry ice); (c) quartz, SiO2; (d) copper wire; (e) table sugar, C12H22O11. Which one has the lowest melting point?

Solution. (a) Ionic (metal + nonmetal). (b) Molecular. (c) Covalent network. (d) Metallic. (e) Molecular. Dry ice has the lowest melting point (it sublimes at −78 °C): its nonpolar molecules are held only by dispersion forces.

Check. SiO2 and CO2 look alike on paper, but CO2 is a small molecule while quartz is an endless network of Si–O bonds. Network solids are the main exception to "nonmetals form molecular solids."

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Three quick solid questions

Problem. (a) Which solid has the highest melting point: CH3OH(s), CaCl2(s), HCl(s), N2(s), or CCl4(s)? (b) Which is an atomic nonbonding solid: Br2(s), NH3(s), Kr(s), K(s), or SiO2(s)? (c) Which conducts electricity: KCl(s), ice, dry ice, Cu(s), or CaO(s)?

Solution. (a) Name what holds each together: CH3OH, hydrogen bonding; CaCl2, ionic bonds; HCl, dipole–dipole forces; N2 and CCl4, dispersion forces. Ionic bonds are far stronger than any intermolecular force, so CaCl2 melts highest. (b) Kr(s): single noble-gas atoms held by dispersion forces. Br2 and NH3 are molecular, K is metallic, and SiO2 is a covalent network. (c) Cu(s), the only metal. KCl and CaO are ionic, so their ions are locked in place until they melt or dissolve; ice and dry ice are molecular.

Check. For (a), a metal-plus-nonmetal compound in a list of molecular substances is almost always the highest melting.

Professor Tip

To classify a solid, look at the formula. A metal alone: metallic. A metal with a nonmetal (or a polyatomic ion): ionic. Nonmetals only: molecular, unless it is one of the few network solids to memorize: diamond and graphite (C), quartz (SiO2), and silicon carbide (SiC). Then predict: network and ionic solids melt high, molecular solids melt low.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Many X-ray and nuclear medicine detectors depend on ionic crystals called scintillators, which give off a flash of visible light when they absorb an X-ray or gamma ray. Gamma cameras used in nuclear medicine have traditionally used a large crystal of sodium iodide with a trace of thallium, NaI(Tl), and many digital X-ray detectors use needle-shaped crystals of cesium iodide, CsI(Tl). Light sensors behind the crystal turn the flashes into an image. Because NaI crystals absorb moisture from the air and crack with sudden temperature changes, the detector is sealed and protected, one reason equipment rooms are temperature-controlled. (Context only; follow the department's equipment procedures.)

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth enamel is the hardest tissue in the body. It is about 96% mineral, mostly hydroxyapatite, an ionic crystalline solid with the formula Ca5(PO4)3OH. Acids made by plaque bacteria dissolve hydroxyapatite (demineralization). Fluoride from toothpaste and varnish can replace the OH− ions to form fluorapatite, Ca5(PO4)3F, which resists acid better; this is the chemistry behind the fluoride varnish applied in the operatory. (Context only; follow the dentist's treatment plan.)

Common Mistakes

  • Calling SiO2 a molecular solid: quartz is a covalent network.
  • Saying solid ionic compounds conduct: the ions must be free to move (melted or dissolved).
  • Linking viscosity only to mass: strong attractions (such as many O–H groups) matter as much as size.
  • Saying strong forces give a high vapor pressure: strong forces hold molecules in the liquid, so vapor pressure is low.
  • Treating all atomic solids alike: diamond (covalent network) melts very high (above 3500 °C), while solid argon (nonbonding) melts at −189 °C.

Summary

  • Stronger intermolecular forces mean higher surface tension and viscosity.
  • Capillary action occurs when adhesion to the tube is stronger than cohesion.
  • Crystalline solids: ionic (ions), molecular (molecules, IMFs), covalent network (atoms, covalent bonds), atomic nonbonding (noble-gas atoms, dispersion), metallic (cations in a sea of electrons).
  • Atomic nonbonding solids (solid noble gases) are held only by dispersion forces and melt very low.
  • Network and ionic solids have high melting points; molecular and atomic nonbonding solids have low ones. Of these types, only metals conduct as solids (graphite is a rare exception).
  • Strong intermolecular forces: high melting point, boiling point, viscosity, and surface tension, and low vapor pressure.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify each solid as ionic, molecular, covalent network, or metallic: (a) MgO; (b) ice; (c) diamond; (d) aluminum foil.

Hint

Metal + nonmetal: ionic. Metal alone: metallic. Separate molecules: molecular. Atoms covalently bonded throughout: covalent network.

Answer

(a) Ionic. (b) Molecular. (c) Covalent network. (d) Metallic.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A white solid melts at 801 °C, does not conduct as a solid, and conducts when dissolved in water. What type of solid is it?

Hint

Use the property clues: a high melting point, and conduction only when melted or dissolved. Which type of solid has charged particles locked in place?

Answer

Ionic (it is NaCl).

Ionic solids have strong ionic bonds (a high melting point) and ions locked in a lattice, so they conduct only when melting or dissolving frees the ions to move.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Why does honey flow more slowly when it is cold?

Hint

Viscosity depends on how easily molecules overcome their attractions. What happens to molecular speed when honey cools?

Answer

At lower temperature the molecules move more slowly and cannot overcome their attractions (many hydrogen bonds between sugar and water molecules) as easily, so the viscosity is higher.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Liquid A has only weak dispersion forces; liquid B hydrogen bonds. Fill in "higher" or "lower" for liquid A compared with B: boiling point, vapor pressure, viscosity, surface tension.

Hint

Use the property table: weak forces give low values, except for the one row that runs the other way.

Answer

Boiling point: lower. Vapor pressure: higher. Viscosity: lower. Surface tension: lower. (Vapor pressure is the one that goes the opposite way.)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

For each solid, name the type, what holds it together, and whether it conducts as a solid: (a) Ne(s); (b) MgCl2(s); (c) Zn(s); (d) quartz, SiO2(s).

Hint

Classify first: noble-gas atoms, metal + nonmetal, metal alone, or a network of covalent bonds. Conducting needs charges that are free to move.

Answer

(a) Atomic nonbonding; dispersion forces; no. (b) Ionic; ionic bonds; no (yes when melted or dissolved). (c) Metallic; metallic bonding; yes. (d) Covalent network; covalent bonds; no.
