Learning Objectives

  • State Le Chatelier's principle and use it to predict the direction of a shift.
  • Predict the effect of adding or removing a reactant or product.
  • Predict the effect of a temperature change by treating heat as a reactant or product, and explain why only temperature changes K.
  • Predict the effect of adding a soluble salt by first writing how it dissociates, and find the ion that actually shifts the equilibrium.
  • Predict the effect of a temperature change when the equation shows only the word "heat."
  • Predict the effect of a pressure or volume change (smaller or larger) on a gas equilibrium by counting moles of gas, including reactions with solids.
  • Explain why catalysts and added inert gases do not shift an equilibrium, and apply these ideas to hemoglobin, blood CO2, tooth enamel, and the Haber process.

A system at equilibrium is balanced, but it is not stuck. Breathe faster, climb a mountain, eat a candy bar, or turn up the heat, and equilibria in your body and around you respond. In 1884 the French chemist Henri Le Chatelier described how they respond, and his rule is the most useful idea in this chapter: it lets you predict the direction of a change without any math.

Le Chatelier's Principle

Le Chatelier's principle: when a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a change in conditions (a stress), the system shifts in the direction that partly relieves the stress, until a new equilibrium is reached.

"Shifting" means that for a while, one direction runs faster than the other:

  • Shift right (toward products): the forward reaction runs faster for a while; more products form and reactants are used up.
  • Shift left (toward reactants): the reverse reaction runs faster for a while; more reactants form and products are used up.

Once the rates are equal again, the system is at a new equilibrium with new concentrations. The stresses you need to know are changes in concentration, temperature, and pressure or volume (for gases). Catalysts are a fourth case: they do not cause a shift at all.

Professor Tip: Stress and Response

You already know Le Chatelier's principle from everyday life. Your instructor announces a test for next class: your heart races (the stress), and you respond by studying. A theme park posts wait times on a big board, and crowds drift toward the short lines until the waits even out. A GPS app sees a traffic jam and reroutes drivers onto other roads. In each case something pokes the system, and the system responds in the way that eases the poke. Think of every Le Chatelier question as poking the reaction with a stick and asking, "Which way do you move to get comfortable again?"

Changing Concentration: Add or Remove a Substance

Adding a reactant means more reactant collisions, so the forward rate jumps and the system shifts right, using up some of what was added. Removing a product means fewer reverse collisions, so the forward reaction again wins for a while and the system shifts right, making more of what was removed. The rule is the same for every species:

Add a substance → the system shifts away from it. Remove a substance → the system shifts toward it.

The shift only partly undoes the change. If you add O2 to an equilibrium mixture, the shift uses up some of it, but the new equilibrium still has more O2 than before. Changing a concentration does not change K; the system simply rearranges until the ratio equals K again. And as in Section 13.3, adding or removing a pure solid or pure liquid causes no shift, because solids and liquids do not appear in K.

Professor Tip

Remember "Add → Away, Take → Toward" (the A's go together and the T's go together). If it helps, ask what you would do: if you checked your bank account and the money were gone (taken), you would work to earn more of it; a reaction that loses a substance works to make more of it, so it shifts toward it. Find the substance in the equation. If it is added, the arrow of the shift points away from it; if it is removed (taken), the arrow points toward it. Then follow the shift to answer "what happens to the other substances": everything on the side the system shifts toward goes up, and everything on the other side goes down.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Adding and removing substances

Problem. For the reaction

2 SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 SO3(g)

predict the direction of the shift for each change, and what happens to [SO2]: (a) O2 is added; (b) SO3 is removed; (c) SO3 is added.

Solution. The table summarizes the shift for each change.

Shifts for Example 1
ChangeShiftEffect on [SO2]
(a) Add O2 (a reactant)Right, away from O2Decreases (it is used up)
(b) Remove SO3 (a product)Right, toward SO3Decreases
(c) Add SO3 (a product)Left, away from SO3Increases (more reactants form)

Check. In (a) and (b) the system shifts toward products, so reactants such as SO2 go down; in (c) it shifts toward reactants, so SO2 goes up.

Adding a Salt: Find the Ion That Matters

In the lab, you rarely add a lone ion. You add a soluble salt, and the salt breaks apart into its ions (solubility rules, Chapter 7). Only an ion that appears in the equilibrium causes a shift; an ion that is already part of the equilibrium is called a common ion. The other ion is a spectator. So before you predict anything, write the dissociation.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): The iron thiocyanate demonstration

Problem. Pale yellow iron(III) ions and colorless thiocyanate ions form a blood-red complex ion:

Fe3+(aq) + SCN−(aq) ⇄ FeSCN2+(aq, red)

Predict the shift and the color change when each solid is stirred into the equilibrium mixture: (a) sodium thiocyanate, NaSCN; (b) iron(III) nitrate, Fe(NO3)3; (c) sodium nitrate, NaNO3.

Solution. Write each dissociation first. All sodium salts and all nitrates are soluble.

NaSCN(s) → Na+(aq) + SCN−(aq)    Fe(NO3)3(s) → Fe3+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq)    NaNO3(s) → Na+(aq) + NO3−(aq)

(a) SCN− is the common ion, a reactant. Add → away: the system shifts right, more FeSCN2+ forms, and the solution turns deeper red. Na+ is a spectator. (b) Fe3+ is also a reactant: shifts right, deeper red. (c) Neither Na+ nor NO3− is in the equation: no shift, and the color stays the same.

Check. Finding the common ion is the whole problem. If the salt had supplied FeSCN2+, a product, the shift would have been to the left, away from it.

Le Chatelier in the Body: Hemoglobin and Oxygen

Red blood cells carry oxygen bound to the protein hemoglobin (Hb). In simplified form, oxygen loading is an equilibrium:

Hb(aq) + O2(aq) ⇄ HbO2(aq)

(Each hemoglobin actually carries up to four O2 molecules, but the one-O2 version shows the chemistry.) In the alveoli, the tiny air sacs of the lungs, the partial pressure of oxygen is high, so O2 is "added": the equilibrium shifts right and hemoglobin loads up as HbO2. In a working muscle, cells are using O2, so it is "removed": the equilibrium shifts left and HbO2 releases its oxygen exactly where it is needed. One equilibrium, run in opposite directions in two places, delivers oxygen to every tissue.

Altitude is another stress. At a mountain resort, the air has a lower partial pressure of O2, so less HbO2 forms and a visitor feels short of breath. Over several weeks, the body of someone who lives at altitude makes more hemoglobin. More Hb, a reactant, shifts the equilibrium back to the right, and more oxygen is carried again.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

A pulse oximeter clipped to a finger reads SpO2, the percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen; a healthy adult at sea level usually reads about 95–100%. That number is a direct look at the Hb + O2 ⇄ HbO2 equilibrium in arterial blood. When a patient has hypoxemia (low oxygen in the blood), for example during an asthma attack or pneumonia, less O2 reaches the alveoli, less HbO2 forms, and SpO2 falls. Supplemental oxygen raises the oxygen in the alveoli, which shifts the equilibrium back toward HbO2. In the tissues, the opposite matters: active muscle is warmer and makes more CO2 and acid, conditions that help hemoglobin release its oxygen where it is needed most. Nurses watch SpO2 together with breathing rate, color, and mental status, because each reflects how well this equilibrium is working. (Context only; oxygen is given as ordered and per facility policy.)

Biology Connection: Anatomy & Physiology: Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

Carbon monoxide (CO), a colorless, odorless gas from faulty furnaces, generators, and car exhaust, binds hemoglobin at the same site as oxygen, but more than 200 times more tightly:

Hb(aq) + CO(aq) ⇄ HbCO(aq)

Even a small amount of CO in the air ties up a large share of hemoglobin as HbCO, and that hemoglobin cannot carry oxygen, so tissues are starved of O2. Le Chatelier's principle explains why CO poisoning is treated with high concentrations of oxygen, sometimes in a hyperbaric (high-pressure) chamber: a large excess of O2 competes for hemoglobin and helps shift it away from HbCO. A standard pulse oximeter cannot tell HbCO from HbO2, so it can read falsely normal in CO poisoning.

Le Chatelier in the Body: CO2, Breathing, and Blood pH

The carbon dioxide your cells make dissolves in blood and sets up a linked pair of equilibria:

CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq) ⇄ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)

Your breathing rate controls the CO2 on the far left. Breathing faster or deeper than the body needs (hyperventilation) removes CO2, so the system shifts left, toward CO2. That uses up H+ ions, so the blood becomes less acidic and its pH rises (respiratory alkalosis). Breathing too slowly or shallowly, or through blocked airways (hypoventilation), lets CO2 build up, so the system shifts right, making more H+, and the pH falls (respiratory acidosis). Normal arterial blood pH is about 7.35 to 7.45, and the body works hard to keep it there; the bicarbonate in this equilibrium is also the main blood buffer you meet in Chapter 12.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Holding your breath

Problem. A swimmer holds her breath underwater for 45 seconds. Predict the direction of the shift in the CO2/bicarbonate equilibrium and the effect on [H+] and blood pH.

Solution. Her cells keep making CO2, but none is breathed out, so CO2 is added to the blood. Add → away: the system shifts right. More H+ forms, so [H+] increases and the blood pH decreases slightly.

Check. This is a short-term version of hypoventilation, which causes acidosis; the rising CO2 is also what makes you feel the urge to breathe.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

Respiratory therapists read this equilibrium every day on an arterial blood gas (ABG), which reports blood pH, PaCO2 (the partial pressure of CO2 in arterial blood, normally about 35–45 mmHg), and bicarbonate. A high PaCO2 (hypercapnia) with a low pH points to hypoventilation: the lungs are not removing CO2 fast enough, as in severe asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or an opioid overdose that slows breathing. A low PaCO2 with a high pH points to hyperventilation, as in anxiety, pain, or early in an asthma attack. For a patient on a mechanical ventilator, the provider's orders set the breathing rate and volume; raising the rate removes more CO2, and the ABG shows the shift to the left as a lower PaCO2 and a higher pH. The kidneys respond too, more slowly, by holding on to or getting rid of bicarbonate over hours to days. (Context only; ventilator changes are made only as ordered and per protocol.)

Patient Case: Mateo's Blood Gas

Back to the chapter opener. In the first hour of his asthma attack, Mateo is breathing fast. Fast breathing removes CO2 faster than his cells make it, so the equilibrium shifts left: H+ combines with HCO3−, [H+] drops, and his ABG shows a PaCO2 below 35 mmHg and a pH slightly above 7.45, a mild respiratory alkalosis. Now suppose the attack drags on. His airways narrow further, air is trapped in his lungs, and his breathing muscles tire. CO2 can no longer get out, it builds up, the equilibrium shifts right, and [H+] climbs. A PaCO2 that rises back to "normal" or above during an asthma attack is a warning sign that clinicians watch for closely, because it can mean the patient is tiring. Mateo responds to his bronchodilator, a medicine that opens the airways, and his blood gas returns to normal. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Le Chatelier in the Mouth: Tooth Enamel

Tooth enamel is made mostly of the mineral hydroxyapatite, Ca5(PO4)3OH, which is in equilibrium with its ions in saliva:

Ca5(PO4)3OH(s) ⇄ 5 Ca2+(aq) + 3 PO43−(aq) + OH−(aq)

The forward direction, enamel dissolving, is demineralization; the reverse, enamel re-forming, is remineralization. After you eat sugar, bacteria in dental plaque make acids. The H+ from those acids reacts with OH− (and with PO43−), removing them. Take → toward: the equilibrium shifts right, and enamel dissolves. Between meals, saliva neutralizes the acid and supplies Ca2+ and phosphate ions. Adding those products shifts the equilibrium left, and enamel rebuilds. Tooth decay happens when the acid attacks win more often than the repairs.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Dental hygienists use this equilibrium every time they talk with patients about diet and fluoride. When plaque acid drops the pH at the tooth surface below the critical pH of about 5.5, demineralization outpaces remineralization; repeated attacks leave chalky white spots, the first visible sign of dental caries (tooth decay). Frequent snacking and sipping sugary or acidic drinks keep the equilibrium shifted toward dissolving for more of the day. Fluoride, from toothpaste, water, or a professionally applied fluoride varnish, helps remineralization and can replace OH− in the mineral to form fluorapatite, Ca5(PO4)3F, which resists acid better than hydroxyapatite. Patients with xerostomia (dry mouth), a side effect of many medications, have less saliva to supply Ca2+ and phosphate and to neutralize acid, so the equilibrium stays shifted toward demineralization and their caries risk rises. (Context only; follow the dentist's treatment plan.)

Changing Temperature

To predict the effect of temperature, write heat into the equation as if it were a substance, using ΔH (Chapter 8):

  • Endothermic (ΔH positive): heat is absorbed, so it is a reactant, written on the left.
  • Exothermic (ΔH negative): heat is released, so it is a product, written on the right.

Then apply "add → away, take → toward." Raising the temperature adds heat; lowering it removes heat. Ask again what you would do: if you got cold, you would put on a layer or turn up the heater to make heat. A reaction that is cooled does the same thing; it shifts toward the side that makes heat.

Keep the matching words together, because a problem may give you any one of them and expect you to know the rest:

  • Endothermic = heat absorbed = heat written as a reactant = ΔH positive (+).
  • Exothermic = heat released = heat written as a product = ΔH negative (−).

ΔH is measured in kilojoules (kJ). Sometimes there is no number at all, and the equation simply shows the word "heat" on one side. Read the side: "heat" on the left means endothermic; on the right, exothermic.

Temperature is special in one way: it is the only stress that changes the value of K. Adding substances or squeezing a gas changes the concentrations, and the system shifts until the ratio equals the same K again. A temperature change gives the reaction a new K. For an endothermic reaction, K gets larger as the temperature rises; for an exothermic reaction, K gets smaller.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Heating an endothermic reaction

Problem. Colorless N2O4 gas and brown NO2 gas are in equilibrium in a sealed tube:

N2O4(g) ⇄ 2 NO2(g)    ΔH = +57.2 kJ

The tube is placed in hot water. Which way does the equilibrium shift, what happens to the color, and what happens to K?

Solution. ΔH is positive, so heat is a reactant: 57.2 kJ + N2O4(g) ⇄ 2 NO2(g). Heating adds heat, a reactant, so the system shifts right. More brown NO2 forms, so the gas becomes darker brown. Because temperature changed, K increases.

Check. In an ice bath the opposite happens: heat is removed, the system shifts left toward heat, and the color fades. This is a classic classroom demonstration.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Cooling an exothermic reaction

Problem. For 2 SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 SO3(g), ΔH = −198 kJ. Predict the shift and the change in K when the temperature is lowered.

Solution. ΔH is negative, so heat is a product: 2 SO2 + O2 ⇄ 2 SO3 + heat. Cooling removes heat, a product. Take → toward: the system shifts right, making more SO3, and K increases.

Check. Exothermic reactions favor products at lower temperatures. (The catch, as the Engineering box below shows, is that low temperatures also make reactions slow.)

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): When the equation just says "heat"

Problem. At the high temperatures in a car engine or a lightning bolt, nitrogen and oxygen react:

N2(g) + O2(g) + heat ⇄ 2 NO(g)

(a) Is the reaction endothermic or exothermic, and what is the sign of ΔH? (b) Predict the shift when the temperature is raised, and what happens to K.

Solution. (a) Heat is written on the left, as a reactant, so the reaction is endothermic and ΔH is positive. (b) Raising the temperature adds heat, a reactant. Add → away: the system shifts right, more NO forms, and K increases.

Check. This matches Section 13.3: K for this reaction is tiny at 25 °C, but much larger in a hot engine, which is why engines make NO.

Changing Pressure or Volume (Gases)

For reactions with gases, squeezing the mixture into a smaller volume raises the pressure (Boyle's law, Chapter 10). The system relieves that stress by shifting toward the side with fewer moles of gas, because fewer gas particles push with less pressure. Expanding the volume, which lowers the pressure, shifts it toward the side with more moles of gas. To decide, count the coefficients of the gases only, on each side. A quick way to ask it: "Where is it less crowded?" When the container is squeezed, the system moves to the side that takes up less room.

Collision theory (Section 13.1) gives another way to see it, using a simple picture in which the molecules in the balanced equation must all meet. In a squeezed container, molecules are packed closer and collide more often, but the side that needs more molecules to come together to react gains the most from the crowding. For N2 + 3 H2, four molecules must meet to react, while on the other side only two NH3 molecules must meet; crowding helps the four-molecule side more, so the forward reaction wins for a while. (Real reactions happen in a series of simpler steps, but this picture predicts the right direction.) In a larger container, the reverse is true: it is rarer for many molecules to find each other at once, so the side with fewer molecules to bring together reacts relatively faster, and the system shifts toward the side with more moles of gas.

Remember that pressure and volume move in opposite directions (Boyle's law, Chapter 10): smaller volume = higher pressure, and larger volume = lower pressure. Students often mix up the direction here, so translate a volume change into a pressure change before you decide.

Two cases cause no shift:

  • When both sides have the same number of moles of gas, as in H2(g) + I2(g) ⇄ 2 HI(g) (2 and 2), neither direction lowers the pressure.
  • Adding an inert gas, such as argon, to a rigid container raises the total pressure but does not change the concentration of any gas in the reaction, so nothing shifts.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): Counting moles of gas

Problem. Predict the shift when the volume of each container is decreased (pressure increased).

(a) N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇄ 2 NH3(g)

(b) CaCO3(s) ⇄ CaO(s) + CO2(g)

(c) H2(g) + Cl2(g) ⇄ 2 HCl(g)

Solution. (a) Left: 1 + 3 = 4 mol gas; right: 2 mol gas. Shift toward fewer moles: right. (b) Left: 0 mol gas (solid only); right: 1 mol gas. Shift left. (c) 2 mol gas on each side: no shift.

Check. Solids and liquids are not counted, just as they are left out of K. Increasing the volume would reverse the answers to (a) and (b) and still give no shift for (c).

Example \(\PageIndex{8}\): Four checkpoints on one reaction, including a larger volume

Problem. The first step in making nitric acid for fertilizer burns ammonia:

4 NH3(g) + 5 O2(g) ⇄ 4 NO(g) + 6 H2O(g)    ΔH = −906 kJ

Predict the shift for each change: (a) O2 is added; (b) H2O vapor is added; (c) the volume of the container is doubled; (d) the temperature is raised.

Solution. (a) Add a reactant: shift right, toward products. (b) Add a product: shift left, toward reactants. (c) Doubling the volume lowers the pressure. Count moles of gas: left, 4 + 5 = 9; right, 4 + 6 = 10. Lower pressure shifts toward more moles of gas: right. (d) ΔH is negative, so heat is a product. Adding heat shifts the system away from it: left, and K decreases.

Check. Only (d) changes K. In (c), the two sides differ by just one mole of gas, but that is enough to set the direction; the size of the difference does not matter for the direction.

Catalysts Do Not Shift Equilibrium

A catalyst lowers the activation energy for both the forward and reverse reactions (Section 13.1), so it speeds both up by the same factor. Go back to the hiking picture: a trail through a mountain pass is easier than the summit for hikers coming from either side of the mountain. Nobody is pushed toward one valley; everyone just gets across sooner. The system reaches equilibrium sooner, but the equilibrium amounts and K are the same as without the catalyst. An enzyme such as carbonic anhydrase does not decide which way CO2 reacts in your blood; it just lets the reaction keep up with whatever direction the conditions favor.

Example \(\PageIndex{9}\): A solid in the equation

Problem. In a hot furnace, carbon reacts with carbon dioxide in an endothermic reaction:

C(s) + CO2(g) ⇄ 2 CO(g)    ΔH = +172.5 kJ

Predict the shift for each change: (a) CO is removed; (b) the temperature is lowered; (c) some of the solid carbon is removed; (d) the pressure is increased by squeezing the container.

Solution. First, mark the solid and put heat in the equation: ΔH is positive, so heat + C(s) + CO2(g) ⇄ 2 CO(g). (a) Take → toward: shift right. (b) Lowering the temperature removes heat, a reactant; the system shifts toward heat: left, and K decreases. (c) C is a pure solid, not in K: no shift. Less work for you. (d) Gas moles: left, 1 (the solid does not count); right, 2. Higher pressure shifts toward fewer moles of gas: left.

Check. K = [CO]2 / [CO2], with no carbon in it, which is why removing carbon does nothing and why the solid is not counted in (d).

Example \(\PageIndex{10}\): Many stresses, one reaction

Problem. For the Haber process,

N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇄ 2 NH3(g)    ΔH = −92.2 kJ

predict the effect on the amount of NH3 at equilibrium of (a) adding H2; (b) raising the temperature; (c) increasing the pressure by decreasing the volume; (d) adding an iron catalyst; (e) removing NH3 as it forms.

Solution. (a) Add a reactant: shift right, NH3 increases. (b) Exothermic, so heat is a product; adding heat shifts left, NH3 decreases (and K decreases). (c) 4 mol gas on the left, 2 on the right: shift toward fewer moles, right, NH3 increases. (d) No change in the equilibrium amount; equilibrium is reached faster. (e) Take → toward: shift right, so more NH3 keeps forming.

Check. Only (b) changes K. Four of the five changes are things an engineer can use to make more ammonia.

Major Connection: Engineering

Most nitrogen fertilizer, including much of what feeds Valley orchards and row crops, starts as ammonia from the Haber process (Example 10). The chemical engineer's problem is a conflict between equilibrium and rate. Low temperature favors NH3 at equilibrium, but at low temperature the reaction is far too slow. So plants compromise: roughly 400–450 °C for a useful rate, very high pressures (on the order of 150–300 atm) to push the equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas, an iron catalyst to reach equilibrium quickly, and continuous removal of NH3 by cooling it to a liquid, with the unreacted N2 and H2 recycled. Each choice is Le Chatelier or collision theory at industrial scale.

Common Mistakes

  • Shifting toward an added substance: adding pushes the system away; removing pulls it toward.
  • Saying K changes when a concentration or the pressure changes: only temperature changes K.
  • Putting heat on the wrong side: negative ΔH (exothermic) means heat is a product; positive ΔH (endothermic) means heat is a reactant.
  • Counting solids and liquids when deciding a pressure shift, or shifting when a solid is added or removed.
  • Getting the volume direction backward: a larger volume means a lower pressure, which shifts toward more moles of gas.
  • Shifting for the spectator ion: when a salt is added, write its dissociation and look only for the ion that appears in the equilibrium.
  • Saying a catalyst shifts the equilibrium toward products: it speeds up both directions equally.
  • Mixing up the CO2 case: fast breathing removes CO2, shifts left, lowers [H+], and raises pH.

Summary

  • Le Chatelier's principle: a system at equilibrium shifts to partly relieve a stress.
  • Add a substance → shift away from it; remove a substance → shift toward it. Solids and liquids cause no shift. When a salt is added, only its common ion counts.
  • Treat heat as a reactant (endothermic, ΔH +) or product (exothermic, ΔH −), whether the equation shows a ΔH value or just the word "heat"; temperature is the only stress that changes K.
  • Less volume (more pressure) shifts toward fewer moles of gas; more volume (less pressure) shifts toward more moles of gas; equal moles, inert gases, and catalysts cause no shift.
  • In the body: high O2 in the lungs loads hemoglobin and low O2 in tissues unloads it; CO ties up hemoglobin; breathing rate sets CO2 and so blood pH; acid shifts enamel toward dissolving, and saliva and fluoride shift it back.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

For CO(g) + 2 H2(g) ⇄ CH3OH(g), ΔH = −90.5 kJ, predict the shift for: (a) adding CO; (b) removing CH3OH; (c) raising the temperature; (d) increasing the volume.

Answer

(a) Right (away from added CO). (b) Right (toward removed CH3OH). (c) Exothermic, heat is a product: adding heat shifts left, and K decreases. (d) 3 mol gas on the left, 1 on the right; more volume shifts toward more moles: left.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Iron(III) ions and thiocyanate ions form a blood-red complex ion: Fe3+(aq) + SCN−(aq) ⇄ FeSCN2+(aq). What happens to the red color when (a) more Fe3+ is added; (b) a substance is added that removes SCN− from solution?

Answer

(a) Add a reactant: shift right, more FeSCN2+, a deeper red. (b) Remove a reactant: shift left (toward SCN−), less FeSCN2+, the red fades.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A patient on opioid pain medicine is breathing only 6 times per minute. Predict the shift in CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq) ⇄ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq), and the effect on blood pH.

Answer

Slow breathing lets CO2 build up (CO2 added), so the system shifts right. [H+] increases and blood pH decreases: respiratory acidosis.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Use Le Chatelier's principle to explain why sipping a sugary soda all afternoon is harder on tooth enamel than drinking the same soda with lunch.

Answer

Each sip feeds plaque bacteria, which make acid. The H+ removes OH− (and phosphate) from the enamel equilibrium, so it shifts toward dissolving (demineralization) again and again. With one drink at lunch, saliva has the rest of the afternoon to neutralize acid and add Ca2+ and phosphate, shifting the equilibrium back toward remineralization.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

For 2 NO2(g) ⇄ N2O4(g), which change would change the value of K: adding NO2, compressing the gas, adding a catalyst, or cooling the mixture?

Answer

Only cooling the mixture. The other changes cause a shift (or no shift, for the catalyst) but leave K the same.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

For 2 H2S(g) + 3 O2(g) ⇄ 2 SO2(g) + 2 H2O(g), predict the shift when the volume of the container is tripled.

Answer

Tripling the volume lowers the pressure. Gas moles: left, 2 + 3 = 5; right, 2 + 2 = 4. Lower pressure shifts toward more moles of gas: left.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{7}\)

For the equilibrium in Exercise 2, a student adds solid potassium thiocyanate, KSCN. (a) Write its dissociation. (b) Which ion causes a shift, and which way? (c) What does the student see?

Answer

(a) KSCN(s) → K+(aq) + SCN−(aq). (b) SCN−, the common ion, is a reactant, so the system shifts right (add → away); K+ is a spectator. (c) A deeper red color, from more FeSCN2+.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{8}\)

For heat + CaCO3(s) ⇄ CaO(s) + CO2(g): (a) Is ΔH positive or negative? (b) Predict the shift when the container is cooled. (c) Predict the shift when more CaO(s) is added.

Answer

(a) Heat is a reactant, so the reaction is endothermic and ΔH is positive. (b) Cooling removes heat, a reactant: shift left, toward heat, and K decreases. (c) CaO is a pure solid: no shift.
