Learning Objectives

  • Recognize Ka and Kb as equilibrium constants for weak acids and weak bases, and use their size to compare strengths.
  • Write a solubility product (Ksp) expression for a slightly soluble ionic compound, decide from Ksp whether it is soluble, and use Ksp to compare solubility.
  • Use Le Chatelier's principle to explain the common-ion effect qualitatively.

Section 13.3 used one letter, K, for every equilibrium. Chemists add a subscript to name the kind of reaction. The rules for writing the expression never change: products over reactants, coefficients as exponents, no pure solids or liquids. Only the label does.

Ka and Kb: How Weak Is a Weak Acid?

In Chapter 12, strong acids such as HCl ionize completely, but weak acids ionize only partly: they set up an equilibrium with water. The equilibrium constant for a weak acid giving up H+ to water is the acid ionization constant, Ka. For acetic acid, the acid in vinegar:

CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + CH3COO−(aq)

\( K_a = \dfrac{[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+][\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-]}{[\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}]} = 1.8 \times 10^{-5} \quad \text{at 25}\ {}^\circ\text{C} \)

Water, the solvent, is left out as usual. A Ka much smaller than 1 means that only a small fraction of the acid molecules ionize. The larger the Ka, the stronger the weak acid: hydrofluoric acid (Ka about 6.8 × 10−4) is a stronger acid than acetic acid. Weak bases have a matching base ionization constant, Kb. For ammonia, NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq), Kb is about 1.8 × 10−5; you wrote this expression in Example 2 of Section 13.3. Even water has its own constant, Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C, from Chapter 12.

Ksp: Solubility as an Equilibrium

The green-sheet solubility rules (Chapter 7) call compounds such as AgCl "insoluble." In fact a tiny amount dissolves, and in a saturated solution the solid is in equilibrium with its ions:

AgCl(s) ⇄ Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq)     \( K_{sp} = [\text{Ag}^+][\text{Cl}^-] = 1.8 \times 10^{-10} \)

The solid is left out, so the solubility product, Ksp, is just the product of the ion concentrations, each raised to its coefficient. For magnesium hydroxide, the active ingredient in milk of magnesia:

Mg(OH)2(s) ⇄ Mg2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq)     \( K_{sp} = [\text{Mg}^{2+}][\text{OH}^-]^2 \)

A smaller Ksp generally means a less soluble compound, although comparisons are only fair between compounds that break into the same number of ions.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Writing Ksp

Problem. Write the dissolving equation and the Ksp expression for calcium fluoride, CaF2.

Solution.

CaF2(s) ⇄ Ca2+(aq) + 2 F−(aq)     \( K_{sp} = [\text{Ca}^{2+}][\text{F}^-]^2 \)

Check. The solid does not appear, and the coefficient 2 on F− becomes an exponent.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Is it soluble? Reading Ksp

Problem. (a) Copper(II) hydroxide has a Ksp of about 1.6 × 10−19 (one commonly listed value). Is Cu(OH)2 soluble in water? (b) Rank these salts from most to least soluble: PbSO4 (Ksp about 2 × 10−8), AgCl (1.8 × 10−10), and AlPO4 (about 1 × 10−20).

Solution. (a) Write the equilibrium and compare Ksp with 1: Cu(OH)2(s) ⇄ Cu2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq). A Ksp about 19 powers of ten below 1 means the equilibrium lies far to the left, toward the solid. Cu(OH)2 is insoluble, which matches the green-sheet rule that most hydroxides are insoluble. (b) Each salt breaks into two ions, so their Ksp values can be compared directly. Largest Ksp, most soluble: PbSO4 > AgCl > AlPO4.

Check. All three are "insoluble" by the solubility rules; Ksp tells you how insoluble. Solving for the actual ion concentrations from Ksp is a general chemistry skill and is not covered here.

Professor Tip: A Roman Numeral Is a Charge

Before writing a Ksp, build the formula correctly. In "lead(II) iodide," the (II) is the charge on lead, 2+, not a subscript. Crisscross the charges to get PbI2. Then, when it dissolves, the subscript 2 on iodine becomes a coefficient: PbI2(s) ⇄ Pb2+(aq) + 2 I−(aq). Never write "I2" as an ion in the products; that is iodine molecules, not two iodide ions.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Barium sulfate, BaSO4, is the contrast medium in a "barium swallow" or barium enema: it blocks X-rays, so the outline of the esophagus, stomach, or intestines shows up on the image. Dissolved barium ions are toxic, so why is it safe to drink? Because BaSO4 has a very small Ksp, about 1.1 × 10−10: BaSO4(s) ⇄ Ba2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) lies so far to the left that almost no Ba2+ dissolves, and the solid passes through the digestive tract. A soluble barium compound would never be used. (Context only; contrast is given as ordered and per facility protocol.)

The Common-Ion Effect

What happens if you add a soluble chloride, such as NaCl, to a saturated solution of AgCl? The Cl− is a common ion: it is already part of the AgCl equilibrium. Adding a product shifts the equilibrium left (add → away), so more solid AgCl forms and less silver stays dissolved. In general, a slightly soluble salt is less soluble in a solution that already contains one of its ions. The same idea applies to weak acids: adding acetate ion to acetic acid shifts its equilibrium left and lowers [H3O+], which is part of how buffers (Chapter 12) work.

Biology Connection: Anatomy & Physiology: Kidney Stones

The most common kidney stones are calcium oxalate, CaC2O4, a slightly soluble salt (Ksp roughly 10−9). In urine, CaC2O4(s) ⇄ Ca2+(aq) + C2O42−(aq). When urine is concentrated, for example in someone who drinks too little water on hot Valley days, or when it carries a lot of calcium or oxalate, the ion concentrations rise and the equilibrium shifts left, toward solid crystals that can grow into stones. Drinking enough fluid dilutes the ions and keeps the equilibrium on the dissolved side.

Summary

  • Ka and Kb are K for weak acids and weak bases reacting with water; a larger Ka means a stronger weak acid.
  • Ksp is K for a slightly soluble salt dissolving; the solid is left out, so Ksp is the product of the ion concentrations with coefficients as exponents. A Ksp far below 1 means insoluble; for salts that break into the same number of ions, a larger Ksp means more soluble.
  • Common-ion effect: adding an ion already in the equilibrium shifts it away from that ion, lowering solubility or ionization.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write Ka for HF(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H3O+(aq) + F−(aq).

Hint

Products over reactants, as for any K. Water is the solvent, a pure liquid, so cross it out.

Answer

Ka = [H3O+][F−] / [HF]. Water is the solvent and is left out.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the dissolving equation and Ksp for Mg(OH)2. Would Mg(OH)2 be more or less soluble in a solution that already contains NaOH?

Hint

Write the solid breaking into one Mg2+ and two OH−; leave out the solid, and the 2 becomes an exponent. Then: OH− is a common ion, so Add → Away.

Answer

Mg(OH)2(s) ⇄ Mg2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq); Ksp = [Mg2+][OH−]2. Less soluble: OH− is a common ion, so adding it shifts the equilibrium left, toward the solid.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Lead(II) iodide is a bright yellow solid with Ksp about 9.8 × 10−9. (a) Write its formula, its dissolving equation, and its Ksp expression. (b) Is it soluble?

Hint

(a) Lead(II) means Pb2+; crisscross the charges, then write the solid dissolving into its ions. (b) Compare Ksp with 1.

Answer

(a) Pb2+ and I− give PbI2. PbI2(s) ⇄ Pb2+(aq) + 2 I−(aq); Ksp = [Pb2+][I−]2. (b) No: Ksp is far below 1, so the equilibrium lies toward the solid. PbI2 is insoluble, as the green-sheet exception for lead(II) halides predicts.
