Learning Objectives

  • Write the forward and reverse reactions for a reversible reaction, using the equilibrium arrow ⇄.
  • Describe dynamic equilibrium: the forward and reverse rates are equal, and concentrations stay constant.
  • Read rate-versus-time and concentration-versus-time graphs and tell when equilibrium is reached.
  • Use the two-beaker water-transfer model to explain why equal rates can come with unequal amounts.

In Chapter 7 you wrote reactions with a single arrow, as if every reactant particle turned into product and the story ended. Many reactions do not work that way. As soon as products form, some of them collide and turn back into reactants. In most reactions the reactants are never completely used up, because a reverse reaction is running at the same time.

Reversible Reactions

A reversible reaction runs in both directions: the forward reaction (reactants → products, read left to right) and the reverse reaction (products → reactants, read right to left). We show this with a double, half-headed arrow, ⇄. For example, hydrogen and iodine gases react to form hydrogen iodide, and hydrogen iodide molecules also collide and break back apart:

H2(g) + I2(g) ⇄ 2 HI(g)

Forward: H2(g) + I2(g) → 2 HI(g)     Reverse: 2 HI(g) → H2(g) + I2(g)

For the reverse reaction to happen, the products must stay in contact with each other. Equilibrium is reached only in a closed system, where nothing can escape. If a product leaves, for example a gas bubbling out of an open beaker, the reverse reaction cannot keep up and the forward reaction runs to completion.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Forward and reverse reactions

Problem. Write the forward and reverse reactions for

N2O4(g) ⇄ 2 NO2(g)

Solution. Forward: N2O4(g) → 2 NO2(g). Reverse: 2 NO2(g) → N2O4(g).

Check. The reverse reaction is the forward reaction read right to left; coefficients and phases do not change.

Reaching Equilibrium

Picture a sealed flask that starts with only H2 and I2. At first, the forward reaction is fast, because there are many reactant particles to collide, and the reverse reaction does not happen at all, because there is no HI yet. As reactants are used up, the forward rate slows down. As HI builds up, the reverse rate speeds up. Eventually the two rates become equal (Figure 1).

That moment is chemical equilibrium: the state in which the forward and reverse reactions run at the same rate, so the concentrations of reactants and products no longer change. It is called dynamic equilibrium because nothing stops. Molecules of HI are still forming and still breaking apart every instant; they just do both at the same rate, so there is no net change.

A good picture is a ski hill on a busy afternoon. Skiers ride the lift up and ski down all day. Once the number riding up each minute equals the number skiing down, the number of people at the top stays the same, even though individual skiers are always moving. The people at the top and the people at the bottom do not have to be equal in number; only the two rates are equal.

Two jugglers passing clubs back and forth show the same idea.

Constant Does Not Mean Equal

At equilibrium, the concentrations are constant, but they are usually not equal to each other. Depending on the reaction, the equilibrium mixture may be mostly products, mostly reactants, or a fair amount of both. Figure 3 shows one reaction in which, at equilibrium, the product concentration ends up about twice the reactant concentration. On a concentration-versus-time graph, equilibrium is where every line goes flat.

It also does not matter which side you start from. A sealed flask that starts with only HI will break down until it reaches an equilibrium mixture of H2, I2, and HI. At the same temperature, that mixture obeys the same equilibrium constant (Section 13.3) as one reached from the H2 and I2 side.

A Model You Can Try: Two Tubs and Two Beakers

A classroom demonstration that many chemistry teachers use makes equal rates something you can see. You need two large tubs and two beakers. One tub, labeled "reactants," starts full of water; the other, "products," starts empty. Each round, one person dips a beaker into the reactant tub and pours whatever it scoops into the product tub (the forward reaction), while a second person does the same thing from the product tub back into the reactant tub (the reverse reaction).

  • Equal beakers. At first the forward beaker comes up full and the reverse beaker comes up empty, so water moves mostly forward. As the product tub fills, the reverse beaker scoops more each round; as the reactant tub drains, the forward beaker scoops less. After a number of rounds, both beakers carry the same amount each trip, and the levels in the tubs stop changing, even though water is still moving both ways. That is dynamic equilibrium.
  • Unequal beakers. Now repeat with a large beaker (300 mL) for the forward direction and a small one (150 mL) for the reverse. It looks as if the reactant tub must end up empty, but it does not. As the reactant tub gets shallow, the big beaker can no longer fill; eventually each beaker carries about the same amount per trip, around 140 mL in the run shown in the Video Lesson below, and the levels stop changing. This time the product tub holds much more water than the reactant tub.

The second run is the whole point of "constant does not mean equal." At equilibrium, the two rates (milliliters moved per trip) are equal, but the two amounts (water in each tub) are not. The bigger forward beaker is like a reaction that favors products: it ends with more product, but it still reaches a balance, and the reverse reaction never stops.

Professor Tip

Two sentences cover most equilibrium questions: at equilibrium, the rates are equal, and at equilibrium, the concentrations are constant. If an answer choice says "the concentrations of reactants and products are equal," it is almost always the wrong one. Equal rates, constant amounts.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): True or false at equilibrium

Problem. For a reaction at equilibrium, decide whether each statement is true or false: (a) the forward and reverse reactions have stopped; (b) the forward rate equals the reverse rate; (c) the concentrations of reactants and products are equal; (d) the concentrations of reactants and products are not changing.

Solution. (a) False: both reactions continue (dynamic). (b) True: that is the definition. (c) False in general: they can be very different. (d) True: equal rates mean no net change.

Check. Statements about rates use "equal"; statements about amounts use "constant."

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Reading a concentration graph

Problem. In Figure 3, (a) about when is equilibrium reached? (b) Before that point, are the forward and reverse rates equal? (c) At equilibrium, which is present in larger concentration?

Solution. (a) At about the dashed vertical line, where both curves become flat. (b) No: the reactant is still falling and the product still rising, so the forward rate is faster than the reverse rate. (c) The product: its flat line is higher, about twice the reactant's.

Check. Flat lines mean constant concentrations, which happens only when the rates are equal.

Biology Connection: Anatomy & Physiology: One Reaction, Two Directions

Your blood carries most of its carbon dioxide as bicarbonate ion, thanks to a reversible reaction inside red blood cells:

CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq)

In your tissues, where cells make CO2, the reaction runs mostly forward, and the carbonic acid then forms H+ and HCO3−. In your lungs, where CO2 is breathed out, the same reaction runs mostly in reverse and releases CO2 from the blood. The enzyme carbonic anhydrase, one of the fastest enzymes known, speeds up both directions, so the blood can switch between them in the second or so a red blood cell spends in a lung capillary. You will see in Section 13.4 how the body uses this equilibrium to control blood pH.

Common Mistakes

  • Saying the reactions stop at equilibrium: both keep running, at the same rate.
  • Saying the concentrations are equal at equilibrium: they are constant, not equal.
  • Thinking equilibrium can be reached in an open container when a product escapes: it needs a closed system.
  • Thinking the side with the "bigger beaker" (the faster starting reaction) wins completely: the reverse reaction speeds up as products build, and the rates still end up equal.

Summary

  • A reversible reaction runs forward and in reverse; the arrow ⇄ shows both.
  • Starting from reactants, the forward rate falls and the reverse rate rises until they are equal: dynamic equilibrium.
  • At equilibrium, rates are equal and concentrations are constant (flat on a graph), but the concentrations are usually not equal.
  • Equilibrium can be reached from either side, in a closed system.
  • In the two-beaker model, equal transfer rates leave the water levels constant but not equal, just like concentrations at equilibrium.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the forward and reverse reactions for CH4(g) + H2O(g) ⇄ CO(g) + 3 H2(g).

Hint

The forward reaction reads left to right; the reverse is the same equation read right to left, with the same coefficients and phases.

Answer

Forward: CH4(g) + H2O(g) → CO(g) + 3 H2(g). Reverse: CO(g) + 3 H2(g) → CH4(g) + H2O(g).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Fill in each blank with "equal" or "not equal": (a) Before equilibrium is reached, the forward and reverse rates are ____. (b) At equilibrium, the forward and reverse rates are ____. (c) At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products are usually ____.

Hint

Equilibrium is defined by rates, not by amounts. Remember: equal rates, constant amounts.

Answer

(a) not equal (the forward rate is faster when you start with reactants). (b) equal. (c) not equal; they are constant.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A sealed flask that starts with only NO2 gas eventually contains both NO2 and N2O4, and its color stops changing. Explain what is happening at the molecular level once the color is steady.

Hint

Equilibrium is dynamic. Once the color is steady, have the two reactions stopped, or are they running at the same rate?

Answer

Pairs of NO2 molecules are still combining to form N2O4, and N2O4 molecules are still splitting into NO2, but at the same rate. The amounts, and so the color, stay constant: dynamic equilibrium.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

In the two-tub demonstration with a 300 mL forward beaker and a 150 mL reverse beaker, the water levels eventually stop changing. (a) At that point, is the forward beaker carrying more water per trip, less, or the same as the reverse beaker? (b) Which tub holds more water? (c) Which part of the model stands for the rates, and which for the concentrations?

Hint

The levels stop changing only when each trip forward is matched by a trip back. Then ask which tub the bigger beaker favors, and what the beakers and the tubs each stand for.

Answer

(a) The same: the levels stop changing only when each trip forward is matched by an equal trip back. (b) The product tub. (c) The amount moved per trip stands for the rates (equal at equilibrium); the water level in each tub stands for the concentrations (constant, but not equal).
