Key Ideas

1. Collision theory and rates

Reactions need effective collisions: the right orientation and at least the activation energy, Ea. Higher concentration, higher temperature, more surface area, and a catalyst all raise the rate. Catalysts (including enzymes) lower Ea, are not used up, and do not change ΔH. Rate effects show up in daily life, from refrigerated food to car batteries that lose range in the cold. (Section 13.1)

2. Dynamic equilibrium

In a reversible reaction (⇄) in a closed system, the forward rate falls and the reverse rate rises until they are equal. At equilibrium, rates are equal and concentrations are constant, but usually not equal. In the two-tub water-transfer model, the beakers end up carrying equal amounts per trip while the tubs hold different amounts. (Section 13.2)

3. Equilibrium constants

K = [products]coefficients / [reactants]coefficients, leaving out pure solids and liquids. Plug in equilibrium concentrations to find K (no units). Different starting mixtures at the same temperature give the same K. K > 1 favors products; K < 1 favors reactants; K close to 1, both. Writing a reaction in reverse flips the expression, and K becomes 1/K. (Section 13.3)

4. Le Chatelier's principle

A stressed equilibrium shifts to partly relieve the stress. Add → away; take → toward. When a salt is added, write its dissociation and find the common ion. Heat is a reactant (endothermic, ΔH +) or product (exothermic, ΔH −), even when the equation just says "heat"; only temperature changes K. Smaller volume (higher pressure) shifts toward fewer moles of gas; larger volume, toward more. Solids are never counted. Catalysts and inert gases cause no shift. In the body: hemoglobin loads O2 in the lungs and unloads it in tissues; fast breathing removes CO2 and raises blood pH; acid shifts enamel toward dissolving. (Section 13.4)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Rates versus equilibrium

Problem. A student adds a platinum catalyst to a mixture of SO2 and O2 and also heats it. For 2 SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 SO3(g), ΔH = −198 kJ, what does each change do to (a) how fast equilibrium is reached and (b) the amount of SO3 at equilibrium?

Solution. The catalyst: (a) faster, (b) no change. Heating: (a) faster (more effective collisions), (b) less SO3, because the reaction is exothermic and adding heat shifts it left.

Check. Rate questions use collision theory; "how much" questions use Le Chatelier's principle and K. Heating can make a reaction faster and less complete at the same time.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Write K and calculate it

Problem. For CO(g) + 2 H2(g) ⇄ CH3OH(g), an equilibrium mixture contains [CO] = 0.150 M, [H2] = 0.300 M, and [CH3OH] = 0.0270 M. Write K, calculate it, and say which side is favored.

Solution.

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{CH}_3\text{OH}]}{[\text{CO}][\text{H}_2]^2} = \dfrac{0.0270}{(0.150)(0.300)^2} = \dfrac{0.0270}{0.0135} = 2.00 \)

K is close to 1, so the mixture has sizable amounts of both reactants and product.

Check. The H2 term is squared because its coefficient is 2. Three significant figures in each value give three in K.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A full Le Chatelier workout

Problem. For CaCO3(s) ⇄ CaO(s) + CO2(g), ΔH = +178 kJ, predict the shift for: (a) adding CaCO3(s); (b) removing CO2; (c) heating; (d) decreasing the volume.

Solution. (a) Solid: no shift. (b) Take → toward: right. (c) Endothermic, heat is a reactant; adding heat shifts right (and K increases). (d) 0 mol gas on the left, 1 on the right; smaller volume shifts toward fewer moles: left.

Check. K = [CO2], so only things that change CO2, or the temperature, matter. This is why lime kilns run hot and vent their CO2.

Common Mistakes

  • Saying the concentrations are equal at equilibrium (they are constant), or that the reactions stop (they continue at equal rates).
  • Writing K upside down, forgetting exponents, or including solids, pure liquids, or solvent water.
  • Shifting toward an added substance; the rule is add → away, take → toward. For an added salt, shifting for the spectator ion instead of the common ion.
  • Getting the volume direction backward: a larger volume is a lower pressure and shifts toward more moles of gas.
  • Putting heat on the wrong side, or saying K changes with concentration or pressure (only temperature changes K).
  • Saying a catalyst increases the amount of product (it only gets to equilibrium faster).
  • Confusing a large K (how far a reaction goes) with a fast rate (how quickly it gets there).
