Learning Objectives

  • Use collision theory to explain what must happen for molecules to react.
  • Read an energy diagram and identify the activation energy and the enthalpy change.
  • Predict how concentration, temperature, surface area, and a catalyst change a reaction rate.
  • Explain qualitatively how enzymes act as catalysts in living things.
  • Use collision theory to explain everyday rate effects, such as a car battery losing range in the cold.

Milk left on the counter spoils in a day; in the refrigerator it lasts a week or more. A fever makes your heart beat faster and your body burn fuel faster. A spark makes gasoline vapor explode, while the same gasoline sits in a can for months. All of these are about the rate of a reaction: how fast reactants turn into products. Before you can understand equilibrium, you need a picture of what makes reactions fast or slow.

Reaction Rate

The reaction rate is how fast a reactant is used up or a product is formed, usually measured as a change in concentration per unit of time (for example, mol/L per second). A fast reaction, such as the burning of methane in a gas stove, is over in a fraction of a second; a slow one, such as the rusting of a fence, takes years. In this course you will describe rates qualitatively: faster or slower, and why.

Collision Theory

Recall from Chapter 10 that the particles of a gas or liquid are always moving and bumping into each other. Collision theory says that a reaction happens only when reactant particles collide, and that not every collision works. An effective collision, one that actually forms products, needs two things:

  1. The right orientation. The particles must hit with the right parts touching, so that the right bonds can break and form.
  2. Enough energy. The particles must hit hard enough to reach at least the activation energy, Ea: the minimum energy needed to break bonds and start the reaction.

A simple case is nitrogen and oxygen forming nitric oxide, N2(g) + O2(g) → 2 NO(g). In a simple picture, the two molecules have to meet lined up side by side, "like a sandwich," so that each nitrogen atom can grab an oxygen atom. An end-on bump, or a gentle one, just sends them bouncing apart.

Anything that raises the number of effective collisions per second speeds up the reaction. That one sentence explains every rate factor in this section.

Activation Energy and Energy Diagrams

An energy diagram follows the energy of the particles as a reaction proceeds. Reactants must climb an energy hill before they can slide down to products. The height of the hill above the reactants is the activation energy. The difference between the starting and ending energy is the enthalpy change, ΔH, that you met in Chapter 8: products below reactants means the reaction releases heat (exothermic, ΔH negative); products above reactants means it absorbs heat (endothermic, ΔH positive).

A high hill means few collisions have enough energy, so the reaction is slow. A low hill means many collisions do, so the reaction is fast. This is why gasoline does not burn on its own at room temperature even though the reaction is very exothermic: its activation energy is high until a spark supplies it.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Reading an energy diagram

Problem. On an energy diagram, the reactants sit at 50 kJ, the top of the hill is at 130 kJ, and the products sit at 20 kJ. (a) What is the activation energy? (b) What is ΔH? (c) Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic?

Solution. (a) Ea = top of hill − reactants = 130 kJ − 50 kJ = 80 kJ. (b) ΔH = products − reactants = 20 kJ − 50 kJ = −30 kJ. (c) Exothermic: the products are lower in energy, so heat is released.

Check. The activation energy is always measured from the reactants up to the top of the hill, so it is always positive; ΔH can be positive or negative.

Four Ways to Change a Reaction Rate

The table below sums up the four factors and the collision-theory reason for each.

Factors that change reaction rate
ChangeEffect on rateCollision-theory reasonEveryday example
Higher concentration of a reactant (or higher pressure of a gas)FasterMore particles in the same space collide more often.A splint glows in air but bursts into flame in pure oxygen.
Higher temperatureFasterParticles move faster, so they collide more often and a larger fraction of collisions have at least Ea.Food cooks faster at higher heat; food spoils more slowly in a refrigerator.
More surface area (smaller pieces of a solid)FasterMore particles of the solid are exposed and available to collide.A crushed antacid tablet works faster than a whole one; flour dust can explode.
Adding a catalystFasterA catalyst provides a different path with a lower activation energy, so more collisions are effective. It is not used up.The catalytic converter in a car; enzymes in your body.

Think of a five-year-old after a pile of birthday cake: running and bouncing off everything in the room. Heated molecules behave the same way. They move faster, hit each other more often, and hit harder. Temperature has an especially strong effect. A rough rule of thumb for many reactions near room temperature is that the rate about doubles for every 10 °C rise, though the real factor depends on the reaction. Lowering the temperature works the other way, which is the whole idea behind refrigerating food, cooling harvested fruit, and storing many medications in the cold.

Major Connection: Engineering

A car battery is a chemical reaction in a box, so it follows the same rate rules. Electric-vehicle makers tell drivers that a battery works by chemical reactions whose speed drops as the temperature drops, so the usable capacity, and the driving range, goes down for a while on a cold morning. Hybrid owners often see lower gas mileage in winter too, partly for this reason. Very hot weather causes problems too, because heat speeds up the unwanted side reactions that wear a battery out. That is why battery engineers design heating and cooling systems to hold the pack near a comfortable temperature, a mild-weather "sweet spot" much like a Valley spring day.

Professor Tip

When a question asks why a change speeds up a reaction, always answer in terms of collisions: more collisions per second, harder collisions, or (for a catalyst) a lower energy hill so more collisions succeed. "The molecules have more energy" is only half an answer for temperature; the full answer is that they collide more often and more of those collisions have at least the activation energy.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Predicting rate changes

Problem. Zinc metal reacts with hydrochloric acid to give hydrogen gas. Predict whether each change makes the reaction faster, slower, or no different, and give the reason: (a) using 3 M HCl instead of 1 M HCl; (b) using zinc powder instead of one zinc pellet of the same mass; (c) running the reaction in an ice bath.

Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)

Solution. (a) Faster: more H+ ions per liter, so more collisions with the zinc each second. (b) Faster: the powder has much more surface area, so more zinc atoms are exposed to the acid. (c) Slower: the particles move more slowly, collide less often, and fewer collisions have at least the activation energy.

Check. Each answer names a change in the number of effective collisions, which is what collision theory requires.

Catalysts and Enzymes

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. It works by offering an alternate path with a lower activation energy, like a hiking trail that goes through a mountain pass instead of over the summit. Because the catalyst comes out of the reaction unchanged, a small amount can be used over and over. A catalyst does not change ΔH: the starting and ending energies are the same, only the hill is lower (Figure 2).

The catalysts in living things are enzymes, mostly proteins, each shaped to bind one particular reactant (its substrate) and hold it in just the right orientation. Enzymes can speed reactions by factors of millions or more. Without them, the reactions that digest your food or copy your DNA would be far too slow to keep you alive at body temperature.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): What a catalyst changes

Problem. The enzyme catalase speeds up the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide:

2 H2O2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + O2(g)    ΔH = −189 kJ

Which of these does catalase change: (a) the rate; (b) the activation energy; (c) ΔH; (d) the amount of catalase at the end?

Solution. Catalase raises (a) the rate by lowering (b) the activation energy. It does not change (c) ΔH, which is still −189 kJ, and (d) the catalase is still there at the end, unchanged.

Check. A catalyst changes the path (the hill), not the start and end points (the reactants and products).

Biology Connection: Microbiology: Refrigeration and Catalase

Bacteria grow by running thousands of enzyme-catalyzed reactions, so their growth follows the same rules as any reaction rate. The U.S. Department of Agriculture calls 40 °F to 140 °F (about 4 °C to 60 °C) the "danger zone," where bacteria in food can multiply quickly; keeping a refrigerator at 40 °F or below slows their reactions enough to keep food safe longer. Cold slows growth but does not kill most bacteria, which is why food is cooked to a safe temperature as well. In the microbiology lab, the catalase test uses the reaction in Example 3: a drop of hydrogen peroxide on a colony that makes catalase bubbles at once with O2, a quick way to help tell groups of bacteria apart.

Biology Connection: Anatomy & Physiology: Fever and Enzyme Rates

Your body keeps its core temperature near 37 °C through thermoregulation. During a fever, the higher temperature speeds up enzyme-catalyzed reactions, so metabolic rate (the total rate of the body's chemical reactions) goes up: heart rate and breathing rate rise, and the body uses more oxygen and fuel. Some immune responses also work faster. But enzymes are proteins, and their shape matters. If the temperature climbs too high, the shapes begin to change (denature), and the enzymes slow down or stop. That is one reason a very high body temperature is dangerous. Enzymes have an optimum temperature, and for human enzymes it is close to body temperature.

Valley Connection: Cooling the Harvest

The San Joaquin Valley's fresh peach harvest runs from mid-May to early October, and from the moment a peach is picked, a clock is running. A harvested fruit is still alive: its cells keep running respiration, the enzyme-catalyzed reactions that burn sugars and release CO2 and heat. The faster respiration runs, the faster the fruit softens, loses flavor, and heads toward decay.

Postharvest guidelines from the University of California, Davis, put numbers on the effect of temperature. Peaches release about 2–3 mL of CO2 per kilogram per hour at 0 °C, about 8–12 mL at 10 °C, and about 32–55 mL at 20 °C. In other words, a peach sitting at 20 °C respires roughly 10 to 20 times faster than one at 0 °C. That is why packers cool fruit with cold water (hydrocooling) or cold forced air soon after harvest, and why the guidelines recommend holding peaches near 0 °C, between −1 and 0 °C. Cold also slows microbes: the guidelines note that keeping fruit below 5 °C is very effective against Rhizopus rot, a mold that thrives on ripe peaches at 20 to 25 °C.

There is a twist. Many peach varieties suffer chilling injury (mealy, browned flesh) when held between about 2 and 8 °C, a range that is cold but not cold enough. Storage workers, packinghouse managers, and the truckers who haul fruit across the country all manage temperature carefully, because in postharvest work, reaction rates are money.

Source: C. H. Crisosto and A. A. Kader, "Peach: Postharvest Quality Maintenance Guidelines," Department of Pomology, University of California, Davis (class.ucanr.edu); UC Davis Postharvest Technology Center (postharvest.ucdavis.edu).

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking every collision causes a reaction: only collisions with the right orientation and at least the activation energy do.
  • Saying a catalyst changes ΔH or is used up: it lowers Ea only, and it is still there at the end.
  • Measuring Ea from the bottom of the graph or from the products: measure from the reactants to the top of the hill.
  • Thinking cold kills bacteria: it slows their reactions; it does not sterilize.
  • Thinking a battery "loses charge" in the cold: the energy is still stored; the reactions that deliver it just run more slowly until the battery warms up.

Summary

  • Reactions happen through effective collisions: the right orientation and at least the activation energy.
  • On an energy diagram, Ea is the hill above the reactants; ΔH is products minus reactants.
  • Higher concentration, higher temperature, more surface area, and a catalyst all speed up a reaction by raising the number of effective collisions per second.
  • Catalysts, including enzymes, lower Ea, are not used up, and do not change ΔH.
  • Rate effects are everywhere: refrigerated food, fever, cooled fruit, and car batteries that lose range in the cold.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Reactants sit at 120 kJ, the top of the hill at 185 kJ, and the products at 160 kJ. Find Ea and ΔH, and classify the reaction.

Answer

Ea = 185 − 120 = 65 kJ. ΔH = 160 − 120 = +40 kJ. The products are higher in energy, so the reaction is endothermic.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Why does a chewable antacid tablet that is chewed neutralize stomach acid faster than one swallowed whole?

Answer

Chewing breaks the tablet into small pieces with much more surface area, so more of the base is exposed to collisions with H+ ions each second.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A student says, "Raising the temperature speeds up a reaction only because the molecules collide more often." What is missing?

Answer

At a higher temperature, a larger fraction of the collisions also have at least the activation energy. That effect is usually the bigger one.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A sample of chicken salad sits at 30 °C instead of 4 °C. Using collision theory, explain why bacteria in it grow faster.

Answer

Bacterial growth depends on enzyme-catalyzed reactions. At 30 °C the molecules move faster, collide more often, and more collisions have at least Ea, so the reactions, and the growth, are faster. 30 °C is also inside the USDA danger zone of 4 to 60 °C.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

An electric car parked outside overnight in winter shows a shorter driving range in the morning than it did the evening before, even though it was not driven. Use collision theory to explain why, and predict what happens to the range once the battery warms up.

Answer

The battery delivers energy through chemical reactions. In the cold, the particles move more slowly, collide less often, and fewer collisions have at least Ea, so the reactions run more slowly and the battery cannot deliver its full capacity. The stored energy is not gone: as the battery warms, the reactions speed up and much of the range comes back.
