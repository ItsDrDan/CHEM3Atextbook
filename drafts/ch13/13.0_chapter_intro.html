Patient Case: Mateo Breathes Too Fast

On a hot June morning, months before the severe attack in Chapter 12, Mateo Herrera is checking drip lines in a dusty almond orchard when his asthma flares. His airways narrow, his chest feels tight, and he starts breathing fast and shallow. At urgent care, a respiratory therapist draws an arterial blood gas (ABG), a blood sample from an artery that measures its oxygen, carbon dioxide, and pH. His blood carbon dioxide is lower than normal, and his blood pH is slightly higher than normal.

Why would an asthma attack make blood less acidic? The answer is a chain of reversible reactions:

CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq) ⇄ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)

Fast breathing blows off CO2, and removing CO2 pushes this system to the left, which uses up H+ ions, so the pH rises. If Mateo tires, CO2 can build up instead, and the system shifts the other way. In this chapter you will learn the rule that predicts these shifts, Le Chatelier's principle, and use it to explain Mateo's blood gas in Section 13.4. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Use collision theory to explain how concentration, temperature, surface area, and catalysts change reaction rates (Section 13.1).
  • Describe dynamic equilibrium and read rate and concentration graphs for a reversible reaction (Section 13.2).
  • Write equilibrium constant (K) expressions, calculate K from equilibrium concentrations, and interpret large and small K values (Section 13.3).
  • Use Le Chatelier's principle to predict how a system at equilibrium responds to changes in concentration, temperature, and pressure (Section 13.4).

Why This Chapter Matters

Your body runs on reactions that go both ways. Hemoglobin picks up oxygen in your lungs and drops it off in your muscles; carbon dioxide turns into bicarbonate in your tissues and back into carbon dioxide in your lungs; tooth enamel dissolves a little after every sugary snack and rebuilds between meals. Each of these is an equilibrium, and each one shifts when conditions change. Nurses read those shifts on a pulse oximeter, respiratory therapists read them on a blood gas, and dental hygienists see them as white spots on enamel. Outside the clinic, the same ideas explain why refrigeration keeps food safe, why fruit is cooled right after harvest, and how the ammonia in Valley fertilizer is made. This chapter builds on reactions and enthalpy (Chapters 7 and 8), temperature and kinetic energy (Chapter 9), gases (Chapter 10), and concentration (Chapter 11), and it supports the acid–base and buffer ideas in Chapter 12.
