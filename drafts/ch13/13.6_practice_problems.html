13.1 Collision Theory and Reaction Rates

Problem 13.1

Nitric oxide from car exhaust reacts with ozone in the air:

NO(g) + O3(g) → NO2(g) + O2(g)

In a flask of the two gases, the molecules collide billions of times per second, yet only a small fraction of those collisions form products. Why?

A. The molecules can react only when they collide with a catalyst, and the flask has none.
B. At room temperature gas molecules are not moving, so they collide only when the flask is shaken.
C. A collision forms products only if the molecules hit with the right orientation and with at least the activation energy.
D. As soon as a few NO2 molecules form, the reaction reaches equilibrium and the collisions stop.

Hint

Recall what collision theory says an effective collision needs. Which choice states that, and which choices contradict how gas particles and equilibrium behave?

Answer

C. Collision theory says an effective collision needs two things: the right orientation (the right atoms touching, so the right bond can break and form) and enough energy to reach at least Ea. Most collisions miss on one or both counts, and the molecules simply bounce apart unchanged.

  • A is wrong because a catalyst is not required. It only lowers the activation energy so that more collisions succeed; many reactions run without one.
  • B is wrong because gas particles are always moving (Chapter 10). Even at room temperature they collide constantly.
  • D mixes up two ideas. Collisions never stop, and at equilibrium (Section 13.2) the forward and reverse reactions keep running at equal rates.

Problem 13.2

On an energy diagram for a reaction, the reactants sit at 85 kJ, the top of the hill is at 210 kJ, and the products sit at 140 kJ. (a) What is the activation energy? (b) What is ΔH? (c) Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic? (d) With a catalyst, the top of the hill is at 160 kJ. What are Ea and ΔH for the catalyzed path?

Hint

Ea = top of hill − reactants; ΔH = products − reactants. For (d), a catalyst lowers only the hill, not the starting or ending energies.

Answer

(a) Ea = top of hill − reactants = 210 kJ − 85 kJ = 125 kJ.

(b) ΔH = products − reactants = 140 kJ − 85 kJ = +55 kJ.

(c) The products are higher in energy than the reactants, so heat is absorbed: endothermic.

(d) Ea = 160 kJ − 85 kJ = 75 kJ, lower than before. ΔH is still +55 kJ: a catalyst lowers the hill but does not move the starting or ending energies.

Problem 13.3

On an energy diagram, the reactants sit at 75 kJ, the top of the hill is at 160 kJ, and the products sit at 10 kJ. What is the activation energy of the forward reaction?

A. 150 kJ
B. 85 kJ
C. 160 kJ
D. 65 kJ

Hint

Activation energy is always measured from the reactants up to the top of the hill, not from zero and not from the products.

Answer

B. Activation energy is always measured from the reactants up to the top of the hill: Ea = 160 kJ − 75 kJ = 85 kJ.

  • A measures from the products (160 − 10). That is the hill for the reverse reaction, not the forward one.
  • C reads the height of the hill from the bottom of the graph (zero) instead of from the reactants.
  • D is the size of ΔH (10 − 75 = −65 kJ, an exothermic reaction), the difference between reactants and products, not the hill.

Problem 13.5

A student drops marble chips (calcium carbonate) into hydrochloric acid and watches the bubbles of carbon dioxide:

CaCO3(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

Which change makes the bubbling slower?

A. Warming the acid from 20 °C to 40 °C
B. Using a few large chips instead of many small chips of the same total mass
C. Using 2.0 M HCl instead of 1.0 M HCl
D. Grinding the chips into a powder before adding them

Hint

Go through the rate factors: temperature, concentration, and surface area. For each choice, does the change speed the reaction up or slow it down?

Answer

B. The reaction happens only where acid touches the surface of the solid. A few large chips expose much less surface area than many small chips of the same mass, so fewer CaCO3 units are available to collide with the acid each second, and the reaction slows.

  • A speeds the reaction up: hotter particles collide more often and harder, and more collisions have at least Ea.
  • C speeds it up: more H+ ions per liter means more collisions with the solid per second.
  • D speeds it up a great deal: a powder has far more surface area than chips.

Problem 13.6

A patient's temperature rises from 37.0 °C to 39.5 °C during a fever. (a) Use collision theory to explain why her metabolic rate, and with it her heart rate and breathing rate, goes up. (b) The body's reactions are catalyzed by enzymes. Explain why a very high body temperature, above about 41 °C, slows some of these reactions instead of speeding them up. (c) What do the words "optimum temperature" mean for an enzyme?

Hint

(a) Apply both effects of a higher temperature on collisions. (b) Enzymes are proteins that need their exact shape. (c) Think of the balance between faster collisions and losing that shape.

Answer

(a) Higher temperature means faster-moving molecules. The substrate molecules collide with enzymes more often, and a larger fraction of those collisions have enough energy to react, so the enzyme-catalyzed reactions of metabolism run faster. The body then uses oxygen and fuel faster, so the heart pumps faster and she breathes faster to keep up.

(b) Enzymes are proteins, and they work only when they hold their exact shape. At too high a temperature the shape begins to change (the enzyme denatures), the substrate no longer fits, and the catalyzed path with its lower activation energy is lost. The reaction falls back toward its slow, uncatalyzed rate.

(c) The optimum temperature is the temperature at which the enzyme works fastest: warm enough for frequent, energetic collisions, but not so hot that the enzyme loses its shape. For most human enzymes it is close to body temperature. (Context only; this is not guidance on treating a fever.)

Problem 13.7

A package of ground beef is forgotten in a car on a hot afternoon and warms to 33 °C. In the refrigerator it was at 3 °C. (a) Use the rule of thumb that rates about double for every 10 °C to estimate how many times faster the bacteria's enzyme-catalyzed reactions run in the car. (b) Is 33 °C inside the U.S. Department of Agriculture's "danger zone" of 40 °F to 140 °F (about 4 °C to 60 °C)? (c) A friend says, "It's fine; the cold in the refrigerator killed the bacteria anyway." What is wrong with this?

Hint

(a) Count the 10 °C steps between the two temperatures, and double once per step. (b) Compare with 4 to 60 °C. (c) Does cold kill bacteria, or slow their reactions?

Answer

(a) The temperature rose by 33 °C − 3 °C = 30 °C, which is three 10 °C steps. Each step doubles the rate: 2 × 2 × 2 = about 8 times faster. (This is a rough estimate; real bacterial growth depends on the species.)

(b) Yes. 33 °C is well inside 4 °C to 60 °C, where bacteria in food can multiply quickly.

(c) Cold slows the bacteria's reactions; it does not kill most of them. Once the meat warms up, their reactions speed up again. That is why food is both kept cold and cooked to a safe temperature.

Problem 13.8

Fine dust from almond hulling, grain handling, or flour milling can explode if it is stirred into the air and meets a spark, but a solid pile of the same material only smolders. Which statement best explains the difference?

A. The dust has far more surface area, so far more of the fuel is exposed to collisions with O2 at the same time.
B. Grinding a material into dust changes it into a new substance with a lower activation energy.
C. Dust particles are hotter than a solid pile because they float in warm air.
D. A cloud of dust contains more total fuel than a pile of the same mass.

Hint

Grinding is a physical change. Which rate factor changes when a solid becomes a fine dust?

Answer

A. Breaking a solid into tiny particles exposes an enormous surface area. Each particle is surrounded by air, so huge numbers of fuel–O2 collisions happen at once, and the burning spreads through the cloud almost instantly. In a pile, only the outside surface touches air.

  • B is wrong because grinding is a physical change. The dust is the same substance with the same activation energy; there is simply much more of it exposed.
  • C is wrong because the dust starts at the same temperature as its surroundings. The spark, not the dust, supplies the activation energy.
  • D is wrong because the problem compares the same mass of material. The amount of fuel is the same; only how much of it can collide with oxygen at once is different.

Problem 13.9

A patient who uses oxygen at home through a nasal cannula is told never to smoke or light candles near the oxygen, because materials burn much faster and more fiercely in oxygen-enriched air. Use collision theory to explain why a higher concentration of O2 makes burning faster.

Hint

Burning is fuel reacting with O2. Which rate factor changes in oxygen-enriched air, and what does that do to the number of collisions per second?

Answer

Burning is a reaction between the fuel (hair, clothing, bedding, a cigarette) and O2. Air near the cannula has a higher concentration of O2, so more O2 molecules are in contact with the fuel's surface, and there are many more fuel–O2 collisions per second. More collisions per second means more effective collisions per second, so the fuel ignites more easily and burns faster and hotter. It is the same reason a glowing splint bursts into flame in pure oxygen. (Context only; follow the oxygen supplier's and the care team's safety instructions.)

Problem 13.10

Ozone near the ground is not released directly by cars or farms. It forms when sunlight drives reactions between nitrogen oxides (from vehicles, trucks, and engines) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). The San Joaquin Valley's ozone levels are usually highest on hot, sunny summer afternoons. (a) Using collision theory, explain how high temperatures help ozone form faster. (b) A student writes, "Heat lowers the activation energy of the ozone reactions." Correct the statement.

Hint

(a) Apply both effects of a higher temperature on collisions. (b) What is the only thing that changes the height of the hill?

Answer

(a) At higher temperatures the molecules in the air move faster, so they collide more often, and a larger fraction of those collisions have at least the activation energy. The reactions that make ozone run faster, so more ozone builds up during a hot afternoon (strong sunlight on long summer days, and still air that traps the gases, add to the effect).

(b) Temperature does not change the activation energy; only a catalyst gives a path with a lower hill. Heating raises the number of molecules that have enough energy to get over the same hill.

13.2 Dynamic Equilibrium

Problem 13.11

Which statement is true for every reaction at chemical equilibrium?

A. The concentrations of the reactants and products are equal.
B. The forward and reverse reactions have both stopped.
C. The forward reaction is faster than the reverse reaction.
D. The forward and reverse rates are equal, so the concentrations stay constant.

Hint

Is equilibrium defined by amounts or by rates? Do the reactions stop, and does “constant” mean the same as “equal”?

Answer

D. Equilibrium is defined by equal rates. Because each species is made as fast as it is used up, there is no net change, and the concentrations stay constant: equal rates, constant amounts.

  • A is the most common trap. The concentrations are constant, but they are usually not equal; the mixture can be mostly products, mostly reactants, or a fair amount of both.
  • B is wrong because equilibrium is dynamic. Both reactions keep running; they just run at the same rate.
  • C describes a reaction that is still approaching equilibrium from the reactant side, not one that has reached it.

Problem 13.12

A sealed bottle of sparkling water has been sitting on a shelf for a week, and the pressure inside has stopped changing:

CO2(g) ⇄ CO2(aq)

Which statement about the bottle is correct?

A. No CO2 molecules move between the gas space and the water anymore.
B. All of the CO2 has dissolved in the water.
C. CO2 molecules dissolve in the water and escape from it at the same rate.
D. The concentration of CO2 in the water is equal to the concentration of CO2 in the gas space.

Hint

The pressure stopped changing, so the bottle is at equilibrium. What is still happening at the molecular level at equilibrium? Does constant mean equal?

Answer

C. This is a dynamic equilibrium (a physical one: the CO2 changes phase, not identity). Molecules are constantly dissolving and escaping, but at the same rate, so the amount in each phase, and so the pressure, stays constant.

  • A says the rates are zero. At equilibrium the rates are equal, not zero.
  • B is wrong because there is still CO2 gas in the space above the water; that gas is what keeps the pressure up.
  • D confuses constant with equal. The two concentrations stay constant, but nothing makes them the same number.

Problem 13.13

Ethylene and hydrogen react to form ethane:

C2H4(g) + H2(g) ⇄ C2H6(g)

(a) Write the forward and reverse reactions. (b) A sealed flask starts with only C2H6. At the very start, which rate is zero, and why? (c) As time goes on, how do the forward and reverse rates change? (d) Once equilibrium is reached, does the flask still contain C2H6?

Hint

(a) Read the equation each way. (b) With only C2H6, which direction has nothing to collide? (c) How do the rates change as substances build up or get used? (d) Is anything used up completely?

Answer

(a) Forward: C2H4(g) + H2(g) → C2H6(g). Reverse: C2H6(g) → C2H4(g) + H2(g).

(b) The forward rate is zero: there is no C2H4 or H2 yet, so there is nothing to collide and react in the forward direction. The reverse reaction is the only one running, and it is at its fastest.

(c) As C2H6 breaks apart, the reverse rate slows (less C2H6 left), and as C2H4 and H2 build up, the forward rate rises. The two rates become equal at equilibrium.

(d) Yes. At equilibrium all three gases are present at constant concentrations. Equilibrium can be reached from either side.

Problem 13.14

A reversible reaction is started in a sealed flask with only reactants. On a graph of rate versus time, which description of the reverse rate is correct?

A. It starts high and falls to zero at equilibrium.
B. It starts at zero, rises, and levels off at the same value as the forward rate.
C. It stays at zero until equilibrium is reached, then jumps up to match the forward rate.
D. It starts at zero and keeps rising until it is larger than the forward rate.

Hint

With only reactants at the start, the reverse reaction has no products to collide. How does its rate change as products build up, and where does it end?

Answer

B. With no products at the start, the reverse reaction cannot happen, so its rate is zero. As products build up, more reverse collisions happen and the reverse rate rises, while the forward rate falls. At equilibrium the two curves meet and stay level together.

  • A describes the forward rate's shape, and even the forward rate does not reach zero; it levels off.
  • C is wrong because the reverse reaction starts as soon as the first product molecules form; it rises gradually, not in a jump.
  • D is wrong because once the rates are equal there is no net change, so nothing pushes the reverse rate any higher.

Problem 13.15

Two sealed flasks of the same size are held at the same high temperature. Flask 1 starts with 0.100 mol of CO and 0.100 mol of H2O vapor. Flask 2 starts with 0.100 mol of CO2 and 0.100 mol of H2. The reaction is

CO(g) + H2O(g) ⇄ CO2(g) + H2(g)

(a) Which reaction, forward or reverse, runs faster at first in each flask? (b) Once equilibrium is reached, which gases are in flask 2? (c) The two flasks contain the same atoms in the same amounts. How do their equilibrium mixtures compare?

Hint

(a) Each flask can run only the direction its starting gases allow. (b) Equilibrium contains everything in the equation. (c) Compare the atoms in each flask; equilibrium can be reached from either side.

Answer

(a) Flask 1 has only reactants, so the forward reaction runs faster at first. Flask 2 has only products, so the reverse reaction runs faster at first.

(b) All four: CO, H2O, CO2, and H2. The reverse reaction makes CO and H2O until the forward and reverse rates are equal.

(c) They end up with the same equilibrium mixture. Both flasks hold 0.100 mol of C, 0.200 mol of O, and 0.200 mol of H in the same volume at the same temperature, so approaching from the left or from the right leads to the same place, with the same K.

Problem 13.16

Water evaporates both from an open pot on a counter and from a capped bottle that is half full. Which can reach a liquid–vapor equilibrium, H2O(l) ⇄ H2O(g)?

A. Only the open pot, because the vapor has room to spread out
B. Both, because evaporation happens in both containers
C. Neither, because evaporation is a physical change, not a chemical reaction
D. Only the capped bottle, because it is a closed system

Hint

Equilibrium needs the reverse process, condensation, to keep pace with evaporation. In which container can the vapor collect above the liquid?

Answer

D. In the capped bottle, vapor collects above the liquid and some of it condenses back. Soon molecules evaporate and condense at the same rate, and the amount of vapor stays constant. In the open pot, the vapor drifts away, so condensation cannot keep up, and the water eventually evaporates completely.

  • A has it backward: letting the vapor escape is exactly what prevents equilibrium.
  • B is wrong because evaporation alone is not equilibrium; the reverse process, condensation, has to keep pace, and that needs a closed system.
  • C is wrong because physical changes such as evaporation and dissolving can reach dynamic equilibrium too, as long as the system is closed.

Problem 13.17

In water, pink hexaaquacobalt(II) ions react with chloride ions to form blue tetrachlorocobaltate(II) ions:

[Co(H2O)6]2+(aq, pink) + 4 Cl−(aq) ⇄ [CoCl4]2−(aq, blue) + 6 H2O(l)

A solution of these ions sits on the bench, and after a few minutes its purple color stops changing. A student says, "The color stopped changing, so the reaction is over, and there must be equal amounts of the pink and blue ions." Correct both parts of the statement.

Hint

Equal rates, constant amounts. Does a steady color mean the reactions stopped? And does constant mean equal?

Answer

The reaction is not over. The color is steady because the system is at dynamic equilibrium: pink ions are still turning into blue ions, and blue ions are still turning back into pink ones, at the same rate, so the amounts stay constant.

The amounts are constant, not necessarily equal. A purple color tells you both ions are present in noticeable amounts, but the equilibrium concentrations of the two ions do not have to be the same; that depends on K and on the other concentrations. Equal rates, constant amounts.

13.3 Equilibrium Constants and Expressions

Problem 13.18

Nitrosyl chloride breaks down into nitric oxide and chlorine:

2 NOCl(g) ⇄ 2 NO(g) + Cl2(g)

Which is the equilibrium constant expression?

A. \( K = \dfrac{[\text{NOCl}]^2}{[\text{NO}]^2[\text{Cl}_2]} \)
B. \( K = \dfrac{[\text{NO}][\text{Cl}_2]}{[\text{NOCl}]} \)
C. \( K = \dfrac{[\text{NO}]^2[\text{Cl}_2]}{[\text{NOCl}]^2} \)
D. \( K = \dfrac{2[\text{NO}][\text{Cl}_2]}{2[\text{NOCl}]} \)

Hint

Products over reactants, with each coefficient as an exponent (not a multiplier).

Answer

C. Products over reactants, with each coefficient as an exponent: NO and NOCl each have a coefficient of 2, and Cl2 has 1 (not written).

  • A is upside down: reactants over products.
  • B leaves out the exponents, as if every coefficient were 1.
  • D multiplies by the coefficients instead of raising to their powers. (The 2's would also cancel, giving B again.)

Problem 13.19

Hydrogen gas reduces magnetite, an iron ore, to iron metal:

Fe3O4(s) + 4 H2(g) ⇄ 3 Fe(s) + 4 H2O(g)

Which is the equilibrium constant expression?

A. \( K = \dfrac{[\text{H}_2\text{O}]^4}{[\text{H}_2]^4} \)
B. \( K = \dfrac{[\text{Fe}]^3[\text{H}_2\text{O}]^4}{[\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4][\text{H}_2]^4} \)
C. \( K = \dfrac{1}{[\text{H}_2]^4} \)
D. \( K = \dfrac{[\text{H}_2]^4}{[\text{H}_2\text{O}]^4} \)

Hint

Circle the phase labels and cross out (s) and (l). Then write products over reactants with exponents. Is the water here a liquid or a gas?

Answer

A. Fe3O4 and Fe are pure solids, so they are left out. Water here is a gas (g), not a liquid, so it stays in. Products over reactants, coefficients as exponents.

  • B includes the two solids. Their concentrations do not change, so they never appear in K.
  • C drops the water as if it were a liquid. Always read the phase label: H2O(g) stays in; H2O(l) would be left out.
  • D is upside down: reactants over products.

Problem 13.20

Write the equilibrium constant expression for each reaction.

(a) 2 CO(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 CO2(g)

(b) CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq)

(c) NH4Cl(s) ⇄ NH3(g) + HCl(g)

(d) 4 NH3(g) + 5 O2(g) ⇄ 4 NO(g) + 6 H2O(g)

Hint

For each: cross out every (s) and (l), then write products over reactants with coefficients as exponents. If nothing is left on the bottom, the expression has no denominator.

Answer

(a) \( K = \dfrac{[\text{CO}_2]^2}{[\text{CO}]^2[\text{O}_2]} \)

(b) \( K = \dfrac{[\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3]}{[\text{CO}_2]} \)

(c) \( K = [\text{NH}_3][\text{HCl}] \)

In (b), water is the solvent, a pure liquid, so it is left out; this is the first step of the blood CO2 equilibrium in Section 13.4. Problem 13.21

A reaction has this equilibrium constant expression:

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]}{[\text{NO}_2]^2} \)

Which balanced equation does it belong to?

A. 2 NO(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 NO2(g)
B. 2 NO2(g) ⇄ 2 NO(g) + O2(g)
C. NO2(g) ⇄ NO(g) + O2(g)
D. 2 NO2(g) ⇄ 2 NO(g) + 2 O(g)

Hint

The species on top are the products and those on the bottom are the reactants; the exponents are the coefficients. Check that the equation balances.

Answer

B. The species on top (NO and O2) are the products, the species on the bottom (NO2) is the reactant, and the exponents are the coefficients: 2 NO2 ⇄ 2 NO + O2. Check that it balances: 2 N and 4 O on each side.

  • A is the reverse reaction. Its K would be the reciprocal, [NO2]2 / ([NO]2[O2]).
  • C is not balanced (2 O on the left, 3 on the right), and its K would have no exponents.

Problem 13.22

At a certain high temperature, an equilibrium mixture for the reaction below contains [CO] = 0.0400 M, [H2O] = 0.0500 M, [CO2] = 0.0600 M, and [H2] = 0.0600 M.

CO(g) + H2O(g) ⇄ CO2(g) + H2(g)

What is K?

A. 0.556
B. 1.33
C. 0.00360
D. 1.80

Hint

Write K (every coefficient is 1): multiply the products on top, multiply the reactants on the bottom, then divide.

Answer

D. All four species are gases, and every coefficient is 1:

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{CO}_2][\text{H}_2]}{[\text{CO}][\text{H}_2\text{O}]} = \dfrac{(0.0600)(0.0600)}{(0.0400)(0.0500)} = \dfrac{0.00360}{0.00200} = 1.80 \)

Three significant figures in each concentration give three in K. K is close to 1, so the mixture has sizable amounts of both reactants and products, as the numbers show.

  • A is reactants over products, 0.00200 ÷ 0.00360. That is K for the reverse reaction.
  • B adds the concentrations, (0.0600 + 0.0600) ÷ (0.0400 + 0.0500), instead of multiplying them.
  • C stops after the numerator and never divides by the reactants.

Problem 13.23

At a high temperature, hydrogen sulfide breaks down:

2 H2S(g) ⇄ 2 H2(g) + S2(g)

An equilibrium mixture contains [H2S] = 0.0800 M, [H2] = 0.0120 M, and [S2] = 0.00600 M. (a) Write K. (b) Calculate K. (c) Are reactants or products favored?

Hint

(a) Products over reactants, with coefficients as exponents. (b) Square both [H2] and [H2S]. (c) Compare K with 1.

Answer

(a) \( K = \dfrac{[\text{H}_2]^2[\text{S}_2]}{[\text{H}_2\text{S}]^2} \)

(b) \( K = \dfrac{(0.0120)^2(0.00600)}{(0.0800)^2} = \dfrac{(0.000144)(0.00600)}{0.00640} = \dfrac{8.64 \times 10^{-7}}{0.00640} = 1.35 \times 10^{-4} \)

(b) K = 1.35 × 10−4 (three significant figures). Forgetting to square the H2 and H2S terms would give 9.00 × 10−4, a different and wrong answer.

(c) K is much less than 1, so reactants are favored: most of the sulfur stays as H2S, just as the concentrations show (0.0800 M H2S against only 0.0120 M H2).

Problem 13.24

Solid ammonium hydrogen sulfide is placed in a sealed flask and partly breaks down into two gases:

NH4HS(s) ⇄ NH3(g) + H2S(g)

At equilibrium, [NH3] = [H2S] = 0.0110 M, and some solid is still present. What is K?

A. 1.21 × 10−4
B. 0.0220
C. 8.26 × 103

13.4 Le Chatelier's Principle

Problem 13.28

Nitric oxide and bromine are at equilibrium with nitrosyl bromide in a sealed flask:

2 NO(g) + Br2(g) ⇄ 2 NOBr(g)

More Br2 is added, and the temperature is kept constant. What happens?

A. The equilibrium shifts left, toward the added Br2; K does not change.
B. The equilibrium shifts right; K does not change.
C. The equilibrium shifts right, and K increases.
D. Nothing happens, because the system was already at equilibrium.

Hint

Is Br2 a reactant or a product, and which way does adding it push the system? Did this stress change the temperature?

Answer

B. Add → away: Br2 is a reactant, so the system shifts away from it, to the right. The extra Br2 means more forward collisions, so for a while the forward reaction runs faster, using up some Br2 and NO and making more NOBr. The temperature did not change, so K stays the same; the concentrations rearrange until their ratio equals the same K again.

  • A shifts toward the added substance. Adding pushes the system away from it.
  • C has the right direction, but only a temperature change changes K.
  • D is wrong because adding Br2 disturbs the equilibrium. Le Chatelier's principle is about exactly that: how an equilibrium responds to a stress.

  6. Add catalyst: no shift (equilibrium is reached sooner); H2O no change; K no.

Only stress 4 changes K. A plant that wants more H2 runs hot (stress 4), at modest pressure (the opposite of stress 5), with excess steam, and removes H2; the catalyst only gets it there faster.

Problem 13.46

Limewater is a saturated solution of calcium hydroxide with some undissolved solid at the bottom of the bottle:

Ca(OH)2(s) ⇄ Ca2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq)

A student adds another spoonful of solid Ca(OH)2 and stirs, keeping the temperature constant. What happens to the concentration of OH− in the solution?

A. It increases, because adding a reactant shifts the equilibrium right.
B. It stays the same, because adding a pure solid causes no shift.
C. It decreases, because the system shifts toward the added solid.
D. It doubles, because twice as much solid is now present.

Hint

Does a pure solid appear in the K expression? If adding the solid changed [OH−], what would that do to the value of the expression?

Answer

B. A pure solid does not appear in K = [Ca2+][OH−]2, so adding more of it does not change the ratio, and nothing shifts. The solution was already saturated; the extra solid simply sits on the bottom.

  • A applies "add → away" to a solid. The rule applies only to species that appear in K, gases and aqueous species.
  • C moves toward the added substance, which is backward even for species that do count.
  • D assumes the dissolved amount depends on how much solid is present. A saturated solution holds a fixed amount at a given temperature, however much extra solid is underneath.

Problem 13.47

In a sealed can of soda, carbon dioxide gas in the space at the top is in equilibrium with CO2 dissolved in the drink. Dissolving CO2 releases heat:

CO2(g) ⇄ CO2(aq) + heat

(a) When the can is opened, the CO2 gas above the drink escapes. Use Le Chatelier's principle to explain the bubbles. (b) Why does a warm soda fizz more and go flat faster than a cold one? (c) Why does an opened soda eventually go completely flat instead of reaching a new equilibrium, as it would if you quickly put the cap back on?

Hint

(a) Opening the can removes CO2(g): Take → Toward. (b) Heat is a product; warming adds heat. (c) Is an open can a closed system?

Answer

(a) Opening the can removes CO2(g), a reactant. Take → toward: the equilibrium shifts left, and dissolved CO2 comes out of solution as gas, forming the bubbles.

(b) Heat is a product. A warmer soda has more heat added, so the equilibrium shifts left, toward CO2(g), and K for dissolving is smaller: less CO2 can stay dissolved. The warmer molecules also move faster, so the gas escapes faster (a rate effect, Section 13.1).

(c) An open can is not a closed system. The CO2 gas keeps drifting away, so the reverse process (gas dissolving back into the drink) can never catch up, and the drink keeps losing CO2 until almost none is left. With the cap back on, gas collects above the drink, the rates become equal again, and the system settles at a new equilibrium (Section 13.2).

Problem 13.48

A sealed flask that starts with 0.0400 M N2O4 and no NO2 is held at a constant temperature:

N2O4(g) ⇄ 2 NO2(g)    ΔH = +57.2 kJ

After 10 s, [N2O4] has fallen to 0.0300 M; after 20 s, to 0.0250 M; and after 30 s it reaches 0.0230 M, where it stays. (a) What is [NO2] at 10 s, 20 s, and 30 s? (b) At 10 s, which rate is larger, forward or reverse? At 40 s? (c) Calculate K at this temperature. (d) At 100 °C, K for this reaction is about 0.36. Was this experiment run above or below 100 °C? Explain using ΔH.

Hint

(a) Each mole of N2O4 lost makes 2 mol NO2. (b) Are the concentrations still changing? (c) Square [NO2] in K. (d) ΔH is positive: does heating make K larger or smaller?

Answer

(a) Each mole of N2O4 that reacts makes 2 mol of NO2, so [NO2] rises by twice the drop in [N2O4]: at 10 s, 2 × (0.0400 − 0.0300) = 0.0200 M; at 20 s, 2 × (0.0400 − 0.0250) = 0.0300 M; at 30 s, 2 × (0.0400 − 0.0230) = 0.0340 M.

(b) At 10 s the concentrations are still changing (N2O4 falling, NO2 rising), so the forward rate is larger. At 40 s the concentrations are constant, so the system is at equilibrium and the two rates are equal. Notice that the equilibrium concentrations, 0.0230 M and 0.0340 M, are not equal.

(c) \( K = \dfrac{[\text{NO}_2]^2}{[\text{N}_2\text{O}_4]} = \dfrac{(0.0340)^2}{0.0230} = \dfrac{0.001156}{0.0230} = 0.0503 \)

(c) K = 0.0503 (three significant figures). Without the exponent, 0.0340 ÷ 0.0230 = 1.48, which is wrong.

(d) Below 100 °C. ΔH is positive, so heat is a reactant, and raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium right and makes K larger. This experiment's K (0.0503) is smaller than K at 100 °C (0.36), so it must have been run at a lower temperature.

Problem 13.49

Return to Mateo Herrera, the irrigation technician with asthma from the chapter opener. In the first hour of his attack, his arterial blood gas (ABG) shows a PaCO2 of 31 mmHg and a pH of 7.49. Suppose, instead of improving, the attack had dragged on until his breathing muscles tired, and a later ABG showed a PaCO2 of 50 mmHg and a pH of 7.30. Use the blood equilibrium:

CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq) ⇄ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)

(a) Compare each PaCO2 and pH with the normal ranges (35 to 45 mmHg; 7.35 to 7.45). (b) Explain the first ABG with Le Chatelier's principle. (c) Explain the later ABG. (d) Carbonic anhydrase is working the whole time. Does it change which way the equilibrium shifts? (e) Write K for the first step, CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H2CO3(aq). Does a change in Mateo's breathing rate change this K?

Hint

(a) Compare with the normal ranges. (b) Fast breathing removes CO2: Take → Toward. (c) CO2 building up is adding: Add → Away. (d) Catalysts speed both directions. (e) Leave out water; only temperature changes K.

Answer

(a) First ABG: PaCO2 31 mmHg is below normal, and pH 7.49 is above normal. Later ABG: PaCO2 50 mmHg is above normal, and pH 7.30 is below normal.

(b) Breathing fast removes CO2 faster than his cells make it. Take → toward: the system shifts left, H+ combines with HCO3−, [H+] falls, and the pH rises: a respiratory alkalosis.

(c) With tired muscles and narrowed airways, CO2 cannot get out and builds up. Add → away: the system shifts right, more H+ forms, and the pH falls: a respiratory acidosis. This is why a PaCO2 that climbs back to normal or above during an asthma attack worries clinicians.

(d) No. The enzyme speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so it only lets the equilibrium keep up quickly with whatever direction his breathing pushes it.

(e) \( K = \dfrac{[\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3]}{[\text{CO}_2]} \)

(e) Water, a pure liquid, is left out. His breathing changes the concentration of CO2, not the temperature, so K does not change; the system shifts until the ratio equals the same K again. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice. In the book's case, Mateo responds to his bronchodilator and his blood gas returns to normal.)

Problem 13.50

Much of the nitrogen fertilizer used on Valley farms starts as ammonia from the Haber process:

N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇄ 2 NH3(g)    ΔH = −92.2 kJ

(a) At one high temperature, an equilibrium mixture contains [N2] = 0.400 M, [H2] = 1.20 M, and [NH3] = 0.150 M. Calculate K. (b) Is K larger or smaller at a lower temperature? (c) A student suggests running the plant at 600 °C instead of about 425 °C "to make more ammonia faster." Which half of the suggestion is right, and which is wrong? (d) The plant adds an iron catalyst. What does it change about the reaction, and what does it not change?

Hint

(a) Square [NH3] and cube [H2]. (b) ΔH is negative, so heat is a product. (c) Separate rate (Section 13.1) from equilibrium. (d) Does a catalyst speed one direction or both?

Answer

(a) \( K = \dfrac{[\text{NH}_3]^2}{[\text{N}_2][\text{H}_2]^3} = \dfrac{(0.150)^2}{(0.400)(1.20)^3} = \dfrac{0.0225}{(0.400)(1.728)} = \dfrac{0.0225}{0.6912} = 0.0326 \)

(a) K = 0.0326 (three significant figures). Forgetting the exponents would give 0.150 ÷ (0.400 × 1.20) = 0.313, and turning the expression upside down would give 30.7; both are wrong.

(b) Larger. ΔH is negative, so heat is a product. Lowering the temperature removes heat; take → toward shifts the equilibrium right, toward NH3, and K increases.

(c) "Faster" is right: at 600 °C molecules collide more often and more collisions have at least Ea, so equilibrium is reached sooner. "More ammonia" is wrong: adding heat to an exothermic reaction shifts it left and makes K smaller, so the equilibrium mixture holds less NH3. That conflict is why plants compromise at about 400–450 °C, use high pressure (4 mol of gas on the left, 2 on the right, so squeezing shifts right), and remove NH3 as it forms.

(d) The catalyst lowers the activation energy for both directions, so equilibrium is reached faster. It does not change the equilibrium amounts, K, or ΔH.

Problem 13.51

Some humidity indicator cards, packed with electronics and medicines, are coated with cobalt(II) chloride. The dry salt is blue, and the hydrated salt is pink. Hydration releases heat:

CoCl2(s, blue) + 6 H2O(g) ⇄ CoCl2·6H2O(s, pink) + heat

(a) Write K. (b) The card is moved from a dry room into a humid one. Which way does the equilibrium shift, and what color does the card turn? (c) A pink card is warmed in a low oven. Which way does it shift, what color does it turn, and what happens to K? (d) A student places a pink card in a sealed jar with extra solid CoCl2·6H2O. Does the extra solid shift the equilibrium?

Hint

(a) Cross out the solids; the water here is a gas. (b) More water vapor is adding a reactant. (c) Heat is a product; warming adds it. (d) Do solids appear in K?

Answer

(a) \( K = \dfrac{1}{[\text{H}_2\text{O}]^6} \)

(a) Both cobalt compounds are solids and are left out. Water is a gas here, so it stays in, on the bottom (a reactant) with exponent 6, and only a 1 is left on top.

(b) More water vapor means a reactant is added. Add → away: shift right, and the card turns pink.

(c) Heat is a product. Warming adds heat, so the system shifts left: water is driven off and the card turns blue. Because the temperature changed, K decreases.

(d) No. The hydrate is a pure solid, so it is not in K, and adding more of it causes no shift.

Problem 13.52

A car's catalytic converter turns carbon monoxide in the exhaust into carbon dioxide:

2 CO(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 CO2(g)    ΔH = −566 kJ

(a) Write K. (b) At 25 °C, K is about 1090. What does this say about the equilibrium mixture? (c) If K is so large, why does an engine's exhaust contain CO at all before it reaches the converter? (d) What does the platinum-group metal catalyst in the converter do, in terms of activation energy? Does it change K? (e) The converter runs at about 400 °C or hotter. Is K larger or smaller there than at 25 °C, and why does the converter still work well?

Hint

(a) Products over reactants, with exponents. (b) Compare K with 1. (c) K tells how far, not how fast. (d) What does a catalyst do to Ea? (e) ΔH is negative, so heat is a product.

Answer

(a) \( K = \dfrac{[\text{CO}_2]^2}{[\text{CO}]^2[\text{O}_2]} \)

(b) K ≫ 1, so at equilibrium the mixture would be almost entirely CO2, with essentially no CO left: products are strongly favored.

(c) K tells how far the reaction goes, not how fast. The exhaust spends only a fraction of a second in the engine and tailpipe, and without a catalyst the activation energy is high enough that much of the CO does not react in that time. The mixture never gets close to equilibrium.

(d) The catalyst provides a path with a lower activation energy, so many more collisions are effective and the reaction gets close to equilibrium in the short time the gas is in the converter. It does not change K or ΔH, and it is not used up.

(e) Smaller. The reaction is exothermic, so heat is a product, and raising the temperature shifts it left and lowers K. But K starts out so enormous that even at converter temperatures it remains very large, so the equilibrium still lies far toward CO2, and the high temperature makes the catalyzed reaction fast. Here, rate (helped by the catalyst and the heat) and equilibrium (a huge K) both work in the driver's favor.

Problem 13.53

In a two-tub water-transfer model of equilibrium, a 500 mL beaker moves water forward from the "reactant" tub and a 100 mL beaker moves water back from the "product" tub. After many rounds, the water levels stop changing. Which statement is true at that point?

A. The reactant tub is empty, because the forward beaker is five times larger.
B. Both beakers carry the same volume of water per trip, and the product tub holds more water.
C. Both tubs hold the same volume of water.
D. Water has stopped moving between the tubs.

Hint

The levels stop changing when each forward trip is matched by a reverse trip: equal rates. Do equal rates mean equal amounts?

Answer

B. The levels stop changing only when each forward trip is matched by an equal reverse trip: equal rates. The large forward beaker favors the product tub, so the product tub ends up with more water, but the reactant tub is not empty; once it is shallow, the big beaker cannot fill.

  • A forgets the reverse direction. As the product tub fills, the small beaker carries a full load back every trip, so the reactant tub never runs dry.
  • C confuses equal rates with equal amounts. At equilibrium the amounts are constant, not equal.
  • D describes a static stop. Equilibrium is dynamic: both beakers keep moving water, at the same rate.

Problem 13.54

For 2 NO(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 NO2(g), K is large at room temperature. (a) Write K for the reverse reaction, 2 NO2(g) ⇄ 2 NO(g) + O2(g). (b) If K for the forward reaction is 4.0 × 102 at some temperature, what is K for the reverse reaction? (c) Do the two values agree about which side is favored?

Hint

(a) Flip the expression: the old reactants go on top, and coefficients stay as exponents. (b) Kreverse = 1/K. (c) Compare each K with 1.

Answer

(a) \( K_{\text{reverse}} = \dfrac{[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]}{[\text{NO}_2]^2} \)

(a) The old reactants are now on top, and the coefficients are still the exponents.

(b) Kreverse = 1 / (4.0 × 102) = 2.5 × 10−3 (two significant figures).

(c) Yes. Forward, NO2 is a product and K > 1; reverse, NO2 is a reactant and K < 1. Both say NO2 is favored.

Problem 13.55

Iron(III) ions and thiocyanate ions are at equilibrium with the red complex ion:

Fe3+(aq) + SCN−(aq) ⇄ FeSCN2+(aq, red)

A student stirs a few crystals of potassium nitrate, KNO3, into the red solution. What happens?

A. The solution turns deeper red, because a salt was added.
B. The color fades, because adding a substance shifts the system left.
C. Nothing changes, because neither K+ nor NO3− is part of the equilibrium.
D. The solution turns deeper red, because NO3− is a common ion.

Hint

Write the dissociation of KNO3 first. Does either ion appear in the equilibrium?

Answer

C. Write the dissociation first: KNO3(s) → K+(aq) + NO3−(aq). Neither ion appears in the equilibrium, so both are spectators, and there is no shift and no color change.

  • A treats every added salt as a stress. Only an ion that appears in the equation (a common ion) causes a shift.
  • B applies "add → away" to a substance that is not in the equation at all.
  • D misnames NO3−. A common ion is one already in the equilibrium; nitrate is not. (Adding iron(III) nitrate would deepen the red, but because of its Fe3+, not its nitrate.)

Problem 13.56

Steam and hot coal react to make "water gas," a fuel mixture of CO and H2:

C(s) + H2O(g) + heat ⇄ CO(g) + H2(g)

(a) Is the reaction endothermic or exothermic, and is ΔH positive or negative? (b) Predict the shift for each change: H2 is removed; more C(s) is added; the volume is increased; the temperature is lowered. (c) Which of the changes in (b) changes K?

Hint

(a) Which side is heat written on? (b) Add → Away, Take → Toward; solids cause no shift; count only gas moles for volume; cooling removes heat. (c) Only temperature changes K.

Answer

(a) Heat is written on the left, as a reactant, so the reaction is endothermic and ΔH is positive.

(b) Remove H2: take → toward, shift right. Add C(s): a pure solid, no shift. Increase the volume: lower pressure, toward more moles of gas; left has 1 mol gas (C is a solid and is not counted), right has 2, so shift right. Lower the temperature: removes heat, a reactant, so shift toward heat, left.

(c) Only lowering the temperature changes K (it makes K smaller for this endothermic reaction).

Problem 13.57

Hydrogen sulfide, the rotten-egg gas, burns in oxygen:

2 H2S(g) + 3 O2(g) ⇄ 2 SO2(g) + 2 H2O(g)

The volume of the container is doubled at constant temperature. Which way does the equilibrium shift?

A. Right, because a larger volume always favors products.
B. Left, toward the side with more moles of gas.
C. Right, toward the side with fewer moles of gas.
D. No shift, because volume is not a stress.

Hint

Doubling the volume lowers the pressure. Count moles of gas on each side: does lower pressure favor the side with more or fewer moles of gas?

Answer

B. Doubling the volume lowers the pressure. Count moles of gas: left, 2 + 3 = 5; right, 2 + 2 = 4. Lower pressure shifts toward more moles of gas, which is the left side here.

  • A has no basis: a larger volume favors whichever side has more moles of gas, which can be either side.
  • C has the rule backward. Fewer moles of gas is the answer for a smaller volume (higher pressure).
  • D is wrong for gas reactions with different numbers of moles of gas on each side; volume changes the pressure, and the system responds.

Problem 13.58

For each reaction, predict the shift when the volume is increased: (a) PCl5(g) ⇄ PCl3(g) + Cl2(g); (b) 2 SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 SO3(g); (c) H2(g) + Br2(g) ⇄ 2 HBr(g); (d) NH4Cl(s) ⇄ NH3(g) + HCl(g).

Hint

A larger volume shifts toward more moles of gas. Count only the gases on each side; a solid counts as zero.

Answer

A larger volume means a lower pressure, which shifts toward more moles of gas.

(a) Left 1, right 2: shift right. (b) Left 3, right 2: shift left. (c) 2 and 2: no shift. (d) Left 0 (solid only), right 2: shift right.

Problem 13.59

A student's sealed test tube at 37 °C holds the same CO2/bicarbonate equilibrium that is found in blood:

CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)

The student adds solid sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO3 (baking soda), to the tube. (a) Write its dissociation. (b) Which ion is the common ion, and which way does the equilibrium shift? (c) What happens to [H+] and to the pH?

Hint

(a) Write the ions NaHCO3 splits into. (b) Which ion is already in the equilibrium? Add → Away. (c) Follow the shift to H+, then to the pH.

Answer

(a) NaHCO3(s) → Na+(aq) + HCO3−(aq).

(b) HCO3− is the common ion, a product. Add → away: the system shifts left. Na+ is a spectator.

(c) H+ is used up as the system shifts left, so [H+] decreases and the pH rises. This is the same idea as the bicarbonate buffer in Chapter 12. (Context only; this is a chemistry exercise, not treatment guidance.)
