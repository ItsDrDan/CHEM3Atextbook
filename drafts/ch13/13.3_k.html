Learning Objectives

  • Write the equilibrium constant expression for a balanced reaction, with coefficients as exponents.
  • Leave pure solids and pure liquids out of an equilibrium expression.
  • Calculate K from equilibrium concentrations.
  • Use the size of K to tell whether reactants or products are favored at equilibrium.
  • Write the expression and K for a reaction written in reverse.

Section 13.2 showed that at equilibrium the concentrations stop changing but are usually not equal. Is there anything about an equilibrium mixture that is always the same? Yes: one particular ratio of product and reactant concentrations, called the equilibrium constant. Its size tells you at a glance whether a reaction makes mostly products or barely gets started.

The Equilibrium Constant Expression

For a general balanced reaction

\( a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightleftharpoons c\text{C} + d\text{D} \)

the equilibrium constant expression is

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{C}]^c\,[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a\,[\text{B}]^b} \)

Square brackets mean "the molar concentration (mol/L) at equilibrium." Three rules build every expression:

  1. Balance the equation first.
  2. Products over reactants: product concentrations multiplied together on top, reactant concentrations multiplied together on the bottom.
  3. Coefficients become exponents: each concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation.

You may see the constant written Keq or Kc (c for concentration); in this course they mean the same thing. K is written without units.

Chemists hang other subscripts on K to name the kind of reaction: Kw for water (Chapter 12), and Ka, Kb, and Ksp for weak acids, weak bases, and slightly soluble salts (previewed in the Going Further page). Don't let the labels worry you. Every one of them is built with the same three rules; only the name changes.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Writing K for the Haber process

Problem. Write the equilibrium constant expression for the synthesis of ammonia:

N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇄ 2 NH3(g)

Solution. The equation is balanced. Product on top with exponent 2; reactants on the bottom with exponents 1 and 3:

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{NH}_3]^2}{[\text{N}_2][\text{H}_2]^3} \)

Check. Every species appears once, in the right place, with its coefficient as the exponent. An exponent of 1 is not written.

Leave Out Pure Solids and Liquids

An equilibrium in which everything is in the same phase, such as all gases or all aqueous, is homogeneous. One that involves more than one phase is heterogeneous. The concentration of a pure solid or a pure liquid (including water used as the solvent) does not change during the reaction, so it is left out of the expression. Only gases (g) and dissolved species (aq) appear in K.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Expressions with solids and liquids

Problem. Write K for each reaction.

(a) CaCO3(s) ⇄ CaO(s) + CO2(g)

(b) NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇄ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Solution. (a) Both calcium compounds are solids, so only the gas remains: \( K = [\text{CO}_2] \). (b) Water is a pure liquid (the solvent), so it is left out:

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{NH}_4^{\,+}][\text{OH}^-]}{[\text{NH}_3]} \)

Check. Look at the phase labels before you write anything: (s) and (l) stay out; (g) and (aq) go in.

Professor Tip

Before you write a single bracket, circle the phase labels. Cross out every (s) and (l). Then write products over reactants with the coefficients as exponents. Most lost points on K expressions come from two slips: forgetting an exponent, or putting water or a solid in the expression.

Calculating K from Equilibrium Concentrations

If you are given the concentrations of every species at equilibrium, you can find K by plugging them into the expression. For a given reaction at a given temperature, K has the same value no matter what amounts you started with: different starting mixtures reach different equilibrium concentrations, but the same K. K changes only when the temperature changes.

The table below shows this for H2(g) + I2(g) ⇄ 2 HI(g) in three sealed flasks at the same temperature (about 430 °C). Experiments 1 and 2 started with different amounts of H2 and I2; experiment 3 started with HI only.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Three starting mixtures, one K (equilibrium concentrations at the same temperature)
Experiment[H2] (M)[I2] (M)[HI] (M)K = [HI]2 / ([H2][I2])
10.01000.02000.10454.1
20.03000.01500.15654.1
3 (started with HI only)0.01360.01360.10054.1

Three different mixtures, one K. (Example 3 below uses the same reaction at a different temperature, so its K is different.)

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): K for hydrogen iodide

Problem. At a certain temperature, an equilibrium mixture contains [H2] = 0.0120 M, [I2] = 0.0150 M, and [HI] = 0.0900 M. Calculate K.

H2(g) + I2(g) ⇄ 2 HI(g)

Solution.

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{HI}]^2}{[\text{H}_2][\text{I}_2]} = \dfrac{(0.0900)^2}{(0.0120)(0.0150)} = \dfrac{0.00810}{0.000180} = 45.0 \)

Check. Each concentration has three significant figures, so K has three: 45.0. K is greater than 1, so at equilibrium the mixture contains more product than reactants, as the numbers show.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Don't forget the exponents

Problem. An equilibrium mixture for N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇄ 2 NH3(g) contains [N2] = 0.500 M, [H2] = 0.200 M, and [NH3] = 0.0300 M. Calculate K.

Solution.

\( K = \dfrac{[\text{NH}_3]^2}{[\text{N}_2][\text{H}_2]^3} = \dfrac{(0.0300)^2}{(0.500)(0.200)^3} = \dfrac{0.000900}{0.00400} = 0.225 \)

Check. Leaving out the exponents would give 0.0300 ÷ (0.500 × 0.200) = 0.300, a different and wrong answer. On the TI-36X Pro, put each bracketed term in parentheses and use the x2 key and the ^ key (for the cube) before dividing.

What the Size of K Tells You

Because K is products over reactants, its size shows which side the equilibrium mixture favors.

The table below summarizes how to read K.

Interpreting the size of K
Value of KWhat the equilibrium mixture containsWe say
K > 1 (very large, such as 105 or more: K ≫ 1)More products than reactants; with a very large K, almost no reactant is leftProducts are favored. A very large K means the reaction goes nearly to completion.
K close to 1 (roughly 0.1 to 10)Sizable amounts of both reactants and productsNeither side is strongly favored.
K < 1 (very small, such as 10−5 or less: K ≪ 1)More reactants than products; with a very small K, almost no product formsReactants are favored. A very small K means the reaction barely happens.

For example, the reaction of nitrogen and oxygen in air, N2(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 NO(g), has a K of roughly 10−30 at 25 °C. That is why the air you breathe is almost entirely N2 and O2, not toxic NO. (At the very high temperatures inside a car engine, K is much larger, and NO does form; Section 13.4 shows why temperature changes K.)

One more fact: the K for a reaction written in reverse is the reciprocal of the original, 1/K. Flipping the reaction flips the expression, because the old reactants are now the products on top:

\( \text{H}_2 + \text{I}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\,\text{HI}: \; K = \dfrac{[\text{HI}]^2}{[\text{H}_2][\text{I}_2]} = 45.0 \qquad 2\,\text{HI} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2 + \text{I}_2: \; K_{\text{reverse}} = \dfrac{[\text{H}_2][\text{I}_2]}{[\text{HI}]^2} = \dfrac{1}{45.0} = 0.0222 \)

Both say the same thing about the same flask: HI is favored. In the forward direction HI is a product and K is large; in the reverse direction HI is a reactant and K is small. Whichever way you write it, the favored species does not change.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Reading K

Problem. Three reactions have these K values at 25 °C: reaction A, K = 2.5 × 1012; reaction B, K = 0.84; reaction C, K = 3.1 × 10−9. For each, does the equilibrium mixture contain mostly reactants, mostly products, or a fair amount of both?

Solution. A: K ≫ 1, mostly products. B: K is close to 1, a fair amount of both. C: K ≪ 1, mostly reactants.

Check. Compare each K to 1: large exponent positive, products; large exponent negative, reactants.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The size of K explains why some medications stay in the blood and others leave it quickly. Many drugs travel partly bound to plasma proteins, mainly albumin, the most abundant protein in plasma (the liquid part of blood):

drug + albumin ⇄ drug–albumin complex

Only the unbound ("free") drug can leave the blood to reach its target or be removed by the kidneys. A drug whose binding equilibrium has a large K is mostly bound, sometimes more than 90 percent, so a small change in binding can make a large change in the free drug. When a patient's albumin is low, as in liver disease or malnutrition, or when a second drug competes for the same binding sites, the free fraction of a highly bound drug can rise. That is one reason pharmacists and providers check albumin levels and drug interactions. (Context only; always follow the order and facility policy.)

Common Mistakes

  • Reactants over products: K is always products over reactants.
  • Forgetting exponents, or multiplying by the coefficient instead of raising to its power.
  • Taking the exponent from a subscript: the exponent is the coefficient in front of the formula. In Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2 KCl(aq), [Pb(NO3)2] gets an exponent of 1 and [KCl] an exponent of 2.
  • Including solids, pure liquids, or solvent water in the expression.
  • Adding concentrations instead of multiplying them.
  • Giving K units: in this course K is written without units.
  • Thinking a large K means a fast reaction: K tells how far a reaction goes, not how fast (that is the rate, Section 13.1).

Summary

  • K = products over reactants, each concentration raised to its coefficient; balance first.
  • Pure solids and liquids (including solvent water) are left out; gases and aqueous species go in.
  • K is found by plugging in equilibrium concentrations; it is constant at a given temperature, whatever the starting mixture, and has no units here.
  • K > 1 favors products and K < 1 favors reactants; the farther K is from 1, the more one side wins. K close to 1 gives sizable amounts of both. The reverse reaction has K equal to 1/K.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write K for 2 SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇄ 2 SO3(g).

Answer

K = [SO3]2 / ([SO2]2[O2]).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write K for C(s) + H2O(g) ⇄ CO(g) + H2(g).

Answer

K = [CO][H2] / [H2O]. Carbon is a solid and is left out; water is a gas here, so it stays in.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

For the reaction in Exercise 1, an equilibrium mixture contains [SO2] = 0.100 M, [O2] = 0.0500 M, and [SO3] = 0.400 M. Calculate K and say which side is favored.

Answer

K = (0.400)2 / [(0.100)2(0.0500)] = 0.160 / 0.000500 = 3.20 × 102. K > 1, so products are favored.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

For PCl5(g) ⇄ PCl3(g) + Cl2(g), an equilibrium mixture contains [PCl5] = 0.800 M, [PCl3] = 0.0200 M, and [Cl2] = 0.0200 M. Calculate K. Are reactants or products favored?

Answer

K = (0.0200)(0.0200) / 0.800 = 5.00 × 10−4. K < 1, so the reactant, PCl5, is favored.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

For 2 NO2(g) ⇄ N2O4(g), an equilibrium mixture contains [NO2] = 0.0500 M and [N2O4] = 0.0150 M. Calculate K.

Answer

K = [N2O4] / [NO2]2 = 0.0150 / (0.0500)2 = 0.0150 / 0.00250 = 6.00.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

Using your answer to Exercise 5, write the expression and calculate K for the reverse reaction, N2O4(g) ⇄ 2 NO2(g). Do the two K values agree about which gas is favored?

Answer

Kreverse = [NO2]2 / [N2O4] = 1/6.00 = 0.167. Yes: in the forward direction N2O4 is a product and K > 1; in the reverse it is a reactant and K < 1. Both favor N2O4.
