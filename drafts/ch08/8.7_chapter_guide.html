Learning Objectives

  • Use mole ratios from balanced equations for mole-to-mole and mass-to-mass conversions.
  • Use molarity and molar volume at STP as conversion factors.
  • Find the limiting reactant, the maximum product, and the leftover excess reactant.
  • Calculate theoretical and percent yield.
  • Use ΔH to find the heat released or absorbed by a given amount of reaction.

Key Ideas

1. Coefficients are mole ratios

A balanced equation is a recipe in moles. mol A × (mol B / mol A) = mol B. Coefficients are not mass ratios. (Section 8.1)

2. Our goal is the mole

grams A → mol A → mol B → grams B, using molar mass A, the mole ratio, and molar mass B. Never skip moles; never multiply a molar mass by a coefficient. (Section 8.2)

3. More on-ramps to moles

Solutions: mol = L × M (convert mL to L). Gases at STP (0 °C, 1 atm): 1 mol = 22.4 L. (Section 8.3)

4. One reactant runs out first

Calculate the product each reactant could make; the smaller amount is the maximum, and that reactant is limiting. Leftover = amount present − amount used. (Section 8.4)

5. Real reactions fall short

Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100%, with the theoretical yield from the limiting reactant. Over 100% means a wet or impure product. (Section 8.5)

6. Heat is part of the recipe

ΔH < 0 exothermic (heat released); ΔH > 0 endothermic (heat absorbed). ΔH goes with the moles in the equation as written; double the equation, double ΔH; reverse it, change the sign. (Section 8.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Gas from an antacid

Problem. A 1.26 g sodium bicarbonate tablet reacts completely with stomach acid: NaHCO3(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). What volume of CO2 would this make at STP? (NaHCO3 84.01 g/mol)

Solution. 1.26 g ÷ 84.01 g/mol = 0.01500 mol NaHCO3 → 0.01500 mol CO2 (1 : 1) × 22.4 L/mol = 0.336 L (336 mL).

Check. In the stomach (37 °C) the volume would be a little larger than at STP, which is part of why antacids cause belching. (Context only.)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Limiting reactant, leftover, and yield

Problem. 4.00 g of NaOH (40.00 g/mol) reacts with 6.00 g of H2SO4 (98.07 g/mol): 2 NaOH + H2SO4 → Na2SO4 + 2 H2O. Find (a) the limiting reactant, (b) the theoretical yield of Na2SO4 (142.04 g/mol), (c) the excess left over, and (d) the percent yield if 6.52 g is collected.

Solution. NaOH: 0.1000 mol → 0.05000 mol Na2SO4. H2SO4: 0.06118 mol → 0.06118 mol Na2SO4. (a) NaOH is limiting. (b) 0.05000 × 142.04 = 7.10 g. (c) H2SO4 used: 0.05000 mol; left 0.01118 mol × 98.07 = 1.10 g. (d) 6.52 ÷ 7.102 × 100% = 91.8%.

Check. The heavier reactant (H2SO4) is the excess one here; mass alone never decides.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A propane heater

Problem. A propane tank holds 2.50 kg of propane (44.097 g/mol). How much heat can it release? C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O(l), ΔH = −2.220 × 103 kJ.

Solution. 2.50 × 103 g ÷ 44.097 g/mol = 56.69 mol; × 2.220 × 103 kJ/mol = 1.26 × 105 kJ released.

Check. Grams were converted to moles before using ΔH, and kg to g first.

Common Mistakes

  • Balance first; mole ratios from unbalanced equations are wrong.
  • Always pass through moles; never mole-ratio grams.
  • Find the limiting reactant by product, not by mass.
  • Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical.
  • Negative ΔH means heat released; scale ΔH by the moles in the equation.
  • Use 22.4 L/mol only at STP; molarity is per liter.
