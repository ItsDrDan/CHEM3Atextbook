Learning Objectives

  • Use mole ratios from balanced equations for mole-to-mole and mass-to-mass conversions.
  • Use molarity and molar volume at STP as conversion factors.
  • Find the limiting reactant, the maximum product, and the leftover excess reactant.
  • Calculate theoretical and percent yield, and actual yield from a given percent yield.
  • Use ΔH to find the heat released or absorbed by a given amount of reaction, and work backward from heat to mass.

Key Ideas

1. Coefficients are mole ratios

A balanced equation is a recipe in moles: 2 wheels : 1 bike = 2 dozen : 1 dozen = 2 mol : 1 mol. Stoichiometry is dimensional analysis. mol A × (mol B / mol A) = mol B, forward or backward; pick only the ratio you need. If the problem gives names, write and balance the equation first. Coefficients are not mass ratios. (Section 8.1)

2. Our goal is the mole

There is no "mole-o-meter," so problems start and end in measurable units. grams A → mol A → mol B → grams B, using molar mass A, the mole ratio, and molar mass B; particles join on either side through Avogadro's number. Never skip moles; never multiply a molar mass by a coefficient; never write "mol" for a mass. Round to the measured given, not to the molar mass. (Section 8.2)

3. More on-ramps to moles

Solutions: mol = L × M (convert mL to L); start with molarity only when a substance has both a volume and a concentration (the green light). Solution to solution: mL → L → mol → mole ratio → L → mL. Gases at STP (0 °C, 1 atm): 1 mol = 22.4 L. (Section 8.3)

4. One reactant runs out first

Amounts given for two or more reactants signal a limiting-reactant problem (the cake: milk runs out first). Calculate the product each reactant could make; the smaller amount is the maximum, and that reactant is limiting. Leftover = initial − used. A liquid given in mL needs its density first. (Section 8.4)

5. Real reactions fall short

Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100%, with the theoretical yield from the limiting reactant; both are amounts of the same product, never reactant ÷ product. Actual yield = (percent ÷ 100%) × theoretical when the percent is given. Product is lost in transfers, incomplete reactions, side reactions, and impure reactants. Over 100% means a wet or impure product. (Section 8.5)

6. Heat is part of the recipe

ΔH < 0 exothermic (heat released); ΔH > 0 endothermic (heat absorbed). ΔH goes with the moles in the equation as written, so one ΔH gives a conversion factor for every coefficient; double the equation, double ΔH; reverse it, change the sign. Heat written as a product means exothermic; as a reactant, endothermic. Start the setup with the given that is not a fraction. (Section 8.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Gas from an antacid

Problem. A 1.26 g sodium bicarbonate tablet reacts completely with stomach acid: NaHCO3(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). What volume of CO2 would this make at STP? (NaHCO3 84.01 g/mol)

Solution. 1.26 g ÷ 84.01 g/mol = 0.01500 mol NaHCO3 → 0.01500 mol CO2 (1 : 1) × 22.4 L/mol = 0.336 L (336 mL).

Check. In the stomach (37 °C) the volume would be a little larger than at STP, which is part of why antacids cause belching. (Context only.)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Limiting reactant, leftover, and yield

Problem. 4.00 g of NaOH (40.00 g/mol) reacts with 6.00 g of H2SO4 (98.07 g/mol): 2 NaOH + H2SO4 → Na2SO4 + 2 H2O. Find (a) the limiting reactant, (b) the theoretical yield of Na2SO4 (142.04 g/mol), (c) the excess left over, and (d) the percent yield if 6.52 g is collected.

Solution. NaOH: 0.1000 mol → 0.05000 mol Na2SO4. H2SO4: 0.06118 mol → 0.06118 mol Na2SO4. (a) NaOH is limiting. (b) 0.05000 × 142.04 = 7.10 g. (c) H2SO4 used: 0.05000 mol; left 0.01118 mol × 98.07 = 1.10 g. (d) 6.52 ÷ 7.102 × 100% = 91.8%.

Check. The heavier reactant (H2SO4) is the excess one here; mass alone never decides.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A propane heater

Problem. A propane tank holds 2.50 kg of propane (44.097 g/mol). How much heat can it release? C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O(l), ΔH = −2.220 × 103 kJ.

Solution. 2.50 × 103 g ÷ 44.097 g/mol = 56.69 mol; × 2.220 × 103 kJ/mol = 1.26 × 105 kJ released.

Check. Grams were converted to moles before using ΔH, and kg to g first.

Common Mistakes

  • Balance first; mole ratios from unbalanced equations are wrong.
  • Always pass through moles; never mole-ratio grams.
  • Find the limiting reactant by product, not by mass.
  • Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical, both for the same product; never divide the reactant mass by the product mass.
  • Round to the measured given; molar masses and coefficients do not limit significant figures.
  • Convert a liquid volume to grams with its density before a limiting-reactant comparison.
  • Negative ΔH means heat released; scale ΔH by the moles in the equation.
  • Use 22.4 L/mol only at STP; molarity is per liter.
