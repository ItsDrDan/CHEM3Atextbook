Learning Objectives

  • Use molarity (mol/L) to convert between solution volume and moles in a reaction.
  • Use the molar volume of a gas at STP (22.4 L/mol) to convert between gas volume and moles.
  • Combine these with mole ratios in stoichiometry problems.

Many reactions happen in solution, measured with pipets and burets, or make gases, measured by volume. The road map simply gains two new on-ramps to moles: one for solutions and one for gases. This section is a preview; Chapter 10 covers gases and Chapter 11 covers solutions in depth.

Molarity as a Conversion Factor

The molarity (M) of a solution is the number of moles of solute per liter of solution: 0.100 M HCl means 0.100 mol HCl in every liter. Molarity is a conversion factor between liters of solution and moles of solute:

\( \text{moles} = \text{liters} \times \text{molarity} \qquad \text{liters} = \dfrac{\text{moles}}{\text{molarity}} \)

Remember to convert mL to L first (divide by 1000).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Neutralizing an antacid

Problem. What volume of 0.100 M HCl reacts completely with 0.250 g of magnesium hydroxide (58.32 g/mol)? Mg(OH)2(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + 2 H2O(l)

Solution.

\( 0.250\ \text{g Mg(OH)}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{58.32\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol HCl}}{1\ \text{mol Mg(OH)}_2} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{0.100\ \text{mol HCl}} = 0.0857\ \text{L} = 85.7\ \text{mL} \)

Check. Dividing by molarity turns moles into liters, and 85.7 mL is a reasonable lab volume.

Gases at STP

At standard temperature and pressure (STP), 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm, one mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L, its molar volume. That makes 22.4 L/mol a conversion factor between gas volume at STP and moles, no matter which gas it is. (Chapter 10 shows how to handle gases at other conditions.)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Hydrogen from zinc

Problem. What volume of H2 gas at STP forms when 1.50 g of Zn reacts with excess HCl? Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g) (Zn 65.39 g/mol)

Solution.

\( 1.50\ \text{g Zn} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Zn}}{65.39\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol H}_2}{1\ \text{mol Zn}} \times \dfrac{22.4\ \text{L}}{1\ \text{mol H}_2} = 0.514\ \text{L H}_2 \)

Check. 1.50 g is a small fraction of a mole of zinc, so a small fraction of 22.4 L of gas makes sense.

Professor Tip

Before you start, ask what the givens and the wanted quantity involve. Only grams? Use molar masses. A solution volume and concentration? Use molarity. A gas volume at STP? Use 22.4 L/mol. Whatever the on-ramp and off-ramp, the middle of the problem is always the same: moles of A → mole ratio → moles of B.

In the Lab: Experiment 8a, Determination of the Gas Constant R

In this lab, a weighed piece of magnesium ribbon reacts with hydrochloric acid, Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g), and you collect the hydrogen in a gas-measuring tube over water. The equation says 1 mol of Mg makes 1 mol of H2, so the mass of magnesium tells you the moles of gas, and the measured volume, temperature, and pressure let you check the gas laws you will study in Chapter 10.

Common Mistakes

  • Using mL with molarity. Molarity is per liter.
  • Using 22.4 L/mol away from STP. It applies only at 0 °C and 1 atm.
  • Forgetting the mole ratio between the two substances.

Summary

  • moles = liters × molarity; liters = moles ÷ molarity.
  • At STP, 1 mol of gas = 22.4 L.
  • These join the road map at the moles boxes; the mole ratio still connects A and B.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many moles of NaOH are in 25.0 mL of 0.200 M NaOH?

Answer

0.0250 L × 0.200 mol/L = 0.00500 mol NaOH.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

What volume of CO2 at STP forms when 0.250 mol of CaCO3 decomposes? CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

Answer

0.250 mol CO2 × 22.4 L/mol = 5.60 L.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

How many mL of 0.500 M H2SO4 neutralize 0.0300 mol of KOH? H2SO4 + 2 KOH → K2SO4 + 2 H2O

Answer

0.0300 mol KOH × 1/2 = 0.0150 mol H2SO4; ÷ 0.500 M = 0.0300 L = 30.0 mL.
