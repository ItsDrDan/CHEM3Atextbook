Learning Objectives

  • Define limiting reactant and excess reactant.
  • Identify the limiting reactant from moles or masses of the reactants.
  • Calculate the maximum amount of product and the amount of excess reactant left over.
  • Recognize a limiting-reactant problem, and handle a reactant given as a liquid volume with a density.

With 4 frames and 6 wheels you can build only 3 bicycles, because you run out of wheels first (Section 8.1). Real reactions work the same way: when you have specific amounts of two reactants, one runs out first and stops the reaction. That reactant is the limiting reactant; the other is the excess reactant, and some of it is left over.

Professor Tip: Let's Bake a Cake

A cake recipe calls for 1 cup of flour, 3 eggs, and 2 cups of milk per cake. Your fridge holds 4 cups of flour, 10 eggs, and 4 cups of milk. Build cakes one at a time: after 2 cakes the milk is gone, even though flour and eggs remain. Milk is the limiting reactant: it is used up first, and when it runs out, the "reaction" stops. Flour and eggs are in excess. What is left over? Excess = initial − used: flour 4 − 2 = 2 cups, eggs 10 − 6 = 4 eggs. Notice that milk did not run out because you had less of it (you had just as much flour); it ran out because the recipe needs 2 cups per cake.

How to Recognize a Limiting-Reactant Problem

A stoichiometry problem is a limiting-reactant problem when amounts are given for two or more reactants: grams of both, moles of both, or a mass of one and a volume of the other. If the problem gives one amount and says the other reactant is "in excess" or "plentiful," it is an ordinary one-reactant problem (Section 8.2). On an exam, limiting-reactant problems take a few minutes each, so it is a good strategy to answer the quick questions first and come back to them.

Finding the Limiting Reactant

The most reliable method, used in CHEM 3A lectures, is to calculate how much product each reactant could make:

  1. Convert each reactant to moles.
  2. For each reactant, use the mole ratio to find the moles (or grams) of the same product it could make.
  3. The reactant that makes the least product is the limiting reactant, and that smaller amount is the most product you can get.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Limiting reactant from moles

Problem. 5.0 mol of H2 and 3.0 mol of O2 react: 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O. Which is limiting, how much water forms, and how much of the excess reactant is left?

Solution. From H2: 5.0 × (2/2) = 5.0 mol H2O. From O2: 3.0 × (2/1) = 6.0 mol H2O. H2 makes less, so H2 is limiting and 5.0 mol H2O forms. O2 used: 5.0 mol H2 × (1/2) = 2.5 mol, so 0.5 mol O2 is left over.

Check. There were more moles of H2 than O2, yet H2 limits, because the recipe needs twice as much H2.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Limiting reactant from grams

Problem. 10.0 g of Al reacts with 25.0 g of Cl2: 2 Al(s) + 3 Cl2(g) → 2 AlCl3(s). Find the limiting reactant, the mass of AlCl3 that can form, and the mass of excess reactant left. (Al 26.98, Cl2 70.90, AlCl3 133.33 g/mol)

Solution. Moles: Al, 10.0/26.98 = 0.3706 mol; Cl2, 25.0/70.90 = 0.3526 mol.

AlCl3 from Al: 0.3706 × (2/2) = 0.3706 mol. AlCl3 from Cl2: 0.3526 × (2/3) = 0.2351 mol. Cl2 is limiting.

Mass of AlCl3: 0.2351 mol × 133.33 g/mol = 31.3 g.

Al used: 0.3526 mol Cl2 × (2/3) × 26.98 g/mol = 6.34 g, so Al left over = 10.0 − 6.34 = 3.7 g.

Check. Mass is conserved: 25.0 g Cl2 + 6.34 g Al used = 31.3 g AlCl3.

A reactant given by volume

Sometimes a liquid reactant is measured by volume. Use its density as one extra conversion factor (mL → g, Chapter 1) at the start, and then continue exactly as before.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Limiting reactant with an extra step

Problem. 3.00 g of aluminum foil reacts with 6.00 mL of liquid bromine (density 3.10 g/mL): 2 Al(s) + 3 Br2(l) → 2 AlBr3(s). Which reactant is limiting, what mass of AlBr3 can form, and what mass of the excess reactant is left? (Al 26.98, Br2 159.80, AlBr3 266.68 g/mol)

Solution. Extra step: mass of bromine = 6.00 mL × 3.10 g/mL = 18.6 g Br2.

Product from each reactant:

\( {3.00\;\text{g Al}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol Al}}{26.98\;\text{g Al}} \times \dfrac{2\;\text{mol AlBr}_3}{2\;\text{mol Al}} = {0.1112\;\text{mol AlBr}}_3 \)

\( {18.6\;\text{g Br}}_2 \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol Br}_2}{159.80\;\text{g Br}_2} \times \dfrac{2\;\text{mol AlBr}_3}{3\;\text{mol Br}_2} = {0.07760\;\text{mol AlBr}}_3 \)

Bromine makes less, so Br2 is limiting. Maximum product: 0.07760 mol × 266.68 g/mol = 20.7 g AlBr3.

Aluminum used by the limiting reactant: 0.07760 mol AlBr3 × (2 mol Al / 2 mol AlBr3) × 26.98 g/mol = 2.09 g Al. Left over: 3.00 g − 2.09 g = 0.91 g Al.

Check. Mass is conserved: 18.6 g Br2 + 2.09 g Al = 20.7 g AlBr3. Excess = initial − used, both in grams.

Professor Tip

Never decide the limiting reactant by comparing grams, or even by comparing moles directly, unless the coefficients are equal. Always run each reactant through the mole ratio to the same product. The smaller answer wins, and that is your final product amount; do not add the two answers or average them.

Clinical Connection: Nursing and Pharmacy

Compounding a medication or preparing a feeding formula has a limiting ingredient, just like a reaction. If a recipe for an oral rehydration solution calls for set amounts of glucose, sodium chloride, and potassium chloride per liter, the ingredient in shortest supply sets how many liters can be made, and the rest is left over. Hospital pharmacies plan batches this way, and the same logic explains why the body can only make as much of a protein as its limiting amino acid allows. (Context only; follow the order and the pharmacy's procedures.)

Common Mistakes

  • Choosing the reactant with the smaller mass as limiting.
  • Ignoring the mole ratio when comparing.
  • Using the excess reactant to calculate the product.
  • Subtracting moles from grams when finding leftovers. Keep units consistent.
  • Missing the hint. Two given amounts means a limiting-reactant problem; one amount plus "excess" does not.
  • Using a liquid's volume as if it were a mass. Convert mL to grams with the density first.

Summary

  • The limiting reactant is used up first and sets the maximum product.
  • Find it by calculating the product each reactant could make; the smaller amount wins.
  • Leftover excess = amount present − amount used by the limiting reactant.
  • Given amounts of two or more reactants signal a limiting-reactant problem. A liquid measured in mL needs its density first.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

For N2 + 3 H2 → 2 NH3, which is limiting if 2.0 mol N2 and 4.5 mol H2 react? How many moles of NH3 form?

Hint

Find the moles of NH3 each reactant could make. The reactant that makes less is limiting, and that smaller amount is the product.

Answer

From N2: 4.0 mol NH3; from H2: 4.5 × 2/3 = 3.0 mol. H2 is limiting; 3.0 mol NH3.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

In Exercise 1, how many moles of N2 are left over?

Hint

Use the limiting reactant's moles to find how much N2 was used (1 N2 : 3 H2). Excess = initial − used.

Answer

N2 used: 4.5 mol H2 × 1/3 = 1.5 mol. Left: 2.0 − 1.5 = 0.5 mol N2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

5.00 g of Mg reacts with 5.00 g of O2: 2 Mg + O2 → 2 MgO. Which is limiting, and what mass of MgO forms? (Mg 24.31, O2 31.998, MgO 40.31 g/mol)

Hint

Convert each mass to moles, then find the moles of MgO each reactant could make. Do not compare grams; the smaller product amount wins.

Answer

Mg: 0.2057 mol → 0.2057 mol MgO. O2: 0.1563 mol → 0.3125 mol MgO. Mg is limiting: 0.2057 × 40.31 = 8.29 g MgO.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A sandwich shop's recipe is 2 slices of bread + 3 slices of turkey + 1 slice of cheese → 1 sandwich. The shop has 20 slices of bread, 24 slices of turkey, and 15 slices of cheese. How many sandwiches can it make, what is limiting, and what is left over?

Hint

Divide each supply by what one sandwich needs. The fewest sandwiches marks the limiting ingredient; leftover = start − used.

Answer

Bread allows 20/2 = 10; turkey allows 24/3 = 8; cheese allows 15/1 = 15. Turkey makes the fewest, so turkey is limiting and 8 sandwiches can be made. Used: 16 bread, 24 turkey, 8 cheese. Left over: 20 − 16 = 4 bread and 15 − 8 = 7 cheese.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Ethanol burns: C2H5OH(l) + 3 O2(g) → 2 CO2(g) + 3 H2O(g). A burner holds 5.00 mL of ethanol (density 0.789 g/mL) and has 8.00 g of O2 available. Which is limiting, and what mass of CO2 can form? (C2H5OH 46.069, O2 31.998, CO2 44.009 g/mol)

Hint

Extra step first: mL ethanol × density gives grams. Then find the moles of CO2 each reactant could make. The two results are close, so carry extra digits.

Answer

Ethanol: 5.00 mL × 0.789 g/mL = 3.945 g; ÷ 46.069 = 0.08563 mol; × 2 = 0.1713 mol CO2. O2: 8.00 ÷ 31.998 = 0.2500 mol; × 2/3 = 0.1667 mol CO2. O2 is limiting (just barely): 0.1667 mol × 44.009 g/mol = 7.34 g CO2.
