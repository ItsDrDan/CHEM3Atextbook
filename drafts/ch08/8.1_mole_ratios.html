Learning Objectives

  • Interpret the coefficients of a balanced equation as mole ratios.
  • Write the mole ratios between any two substances in a reaction.
  • Convert moles of one substance into moles of another, forward (reactant to product) and backward (product to reactant).
  • Write and balance the equation from words before using its mole ratios.

A recipe that says "1 frame + 2 wheels makes 1 bicycle" lets you plan: 10 bicycles need 10 frames and 20 wheels. A balanced chemical equation works exactly the same way, except the "parts" are molecules, and the ratios hold whether you count single molecules, dozens, or moles.

From Bicycles to Dozens to Moles

Suppose you have 13 wheels and plenty of frames. How many bicycles can you build? In your head: 13 ÷ 2 = 6 complete bicycles, with one wheel left over. Written out as dimensional analysis, the recipe supplies the conversion factor:

\( 13\ \text{wheels} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{bicycle}}{2\ \text{wheels}} = 6.5\ \text{bicycles} \)

(only 6 can actually roll out of the shop). The recipe ratio does not care what counting unit you use. 2 wheels per bicycle is the same as 2 dozen wheels per dozen bicycles, and the same as 2 moles of wheels per mole of bicycles. A mole is just a much bigger counting unit, like the dozen. That is the whole idea of stoichiometry: it is dimensional analysis, the same unit-cancelling you have used since Chapter 1, with the balanced equation supplying a new kind of conversion factor.

Coefficients Are Mole Ratios

In the balanced equation for making ammonia,

N2(g) + 3 H2(g) → 2 NH3(g)

the coefficients say that 1 molecule of N2 reacts with 3 molecules of H2 to make 2 molecules of NH3. Multiply every number by Avogadro's number and the same statement holds for moles: 1 mol N2 : 3 mol H2 : 2 mol NH3. Any pair gives a mole ratio, a conversion factor that can be written either way up:

\( \dfrac{3\ \text{mol H}_2}{1\ \text{mol N}_2} \qquad \dfrac{2\ \text{mol NH}_3}{3\ \text{mol H}_2} \qquad \dfrac{1\ \text{mol N}_2}{2\ \text{mol NH}_3} \)

The coefficients tell a story. Read it before you calculate anything. In Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g), the story is: 1 mol Zn needs 2 mol HCl; 1 mol Zn makes 1 mol ZnCl2 and 1 mol H2; and 2 mol HCl make 1 mol H2. Every one of those sentences is a conversion factor.

Mole ratios come only from a balanced equation, and they are exact, so they never limit significant figures. Coefficients do not give mass ratios: 1 g of N2 does not react with 3 g of H2, because the molecules have different masses.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Mole-to-mole

Problem. How many moles of NH3 can be made from 4.20 mol of H2 (with plenty of N2)?

Solution.

\( 4.20\ \text{mol H}_2 \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol NH}_3}{3\ \text{mol H}_2} = 2.80\ \text{mol NH}_3 \)

Check. The equation makes fewer moles of NH3 than it uses of H2 (2 for every 3), so the answer should be smaller than 4.20.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Cellular respiration

Problem. Using C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O, how many moles of O2 are needed, and how many moles of CO2 are made, when 0.150 mol of glucose is used?

Solution. 0.150 mol glucose × (6 mol O2 / 1 mol glucose) = 0.900 mol O2. The ratio of CO2 to glucose is also 6 : 1, so 0.900 mol CO2 forms.

Check. For glucose, moles of CO2 made equal moles of O2 used (6 and 6).

Working Backward from a Product

Mole ratios work in either direction. If you know how much product was made, you can find how much of each reactant was used. You do not need to write every possible ratio: pick only the ratio that connects what you have to what you want.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): From ammonia back to the reactants

Problem. A fertilizer plant makes 12.0 mol of NH3 by N2(g) + 3 H2(g) → 2 NH3(g). How many moles of N2 and of H2 were used?

Given: 12.0 mol NH3. Desire: ??? mol N2; ??? mol H2. Ratios needed: 1 mol N2 = 2 mol NH3; 3 mol H2 = 2 mol NH3.

Solution.

\( 12.0\ \text{mol NH}_3 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol N}_2}{2\ \text{mol NH}_3} = 6.00\ \text{mol N}_2 \qquad 12.0\ \text{mol NH}_3 \times \dfrac{3\ \text{mol H}_2}{2\ \text{mol NH}_3} = 18.0\ \text{mol H}_2 \)

Check. Put the numbers back into the recipe: 6.00 : 18.0 : 12.0 is 1 : 3 : 2. The answers keep 3 significant figures, like 12.0; the coefficients are exact.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Write the equation first

Problem. Aluminum bromide is made by combining aluminum metal with liquid bromine. How many moles of aluminum bromide can be made from 2.40 mol of bromine, with plenty of aluminum?

Solution. No equation is given, so build it (Section 7.3): two elements combining is a combination reaction. Al3+ and Br− swap-drop to AlBr3; bromine is diatomic, Br2. Balance by evening the odds on Br (2 and 3 make 6): 3 Br2 and 2 AlBr3, then 2 Al:

2 Al(s) + 3 Br2(l) → 2 AlBr3(s)

\( 2.40\ \text{mol Br}_2 \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol AlBr}_3}{3\ \text{mol Br}_2} = 1.60\ \text{mol AlBr}_3 \)

Check. Fewer moles of product than of Br2 (2 for every 3), so 1.60 < 2.40 makes sense. A wrong formula (such as AlBr) or an unbalanced equation would have given a wrong ratio, so steps 1 and 2 matter as much as the math.

Professor Tip

Write the mole ratio with the unit you want on top and the unit you have on the bottom, and include the formulas, not just "mol." "2 mol NH3 / 3 mol H2" cancels mol H2 and leaves mol NH3. If you ever write a mole ratio from an unbalanced equation, every answer after it will be wrong, so balance first, every time.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

The ratio of CO2 produced to O2 consumed is called the respiratory quotient (RQ). For burning pure glucose, the equation gives 6 mol CO2 / 6 mol O2 = 1.0. Fats contain much less oxygen and need more O2 for each CO2 made, so their RQ is about 0.7. A typical mixed diet gives an RQ near 0.8: an adult at rest uses roughly 250 mL of O2 per minute and produces roughly 200 mL of CO2. Respiratory therapists and dietitians use indirect calorimetry, measuring the O2 in and CO2 out of a patient's breath, to estimate how many calories a critically ill patient is burning. It is stoichiometry at the bedside.

Common Mistakes

  • Using an unbalanced equation. Mole ratios are only correct for a balanced equation.
  • Flipping the ratio. Put the substance you want on top.
  • Treating coefficients as grams. They are ratios of particles and moles, not masses.
  • Skipping the equation in a word problem. If the problem gives names, write formulas and balance before you look for a ratio.

Summary

  • Coefficients in a balanced equation give mole ratios between any two substances.
  • moles of A × (mol B / mol A) = moles of B.
  • Mole ratios are exact and come only from balanced equations.
  • Stoichiometry is dimensional analysis: 2 wheels : 1 bike = 2 dozen : 1 dozen = 2 mol : 1 mol.
  • Ratios work forward and backward; choose only the ratio that links what you have to what you want.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

For 4 Fe(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Fe2O3(s), how many moles of O2 react with 2.0 mol Fe?

Hint

Use the coefficients of Fe and O2 as the mole ratio, with mol O2 on top so that mol Fe cancels.

Answer

2.0 mol Fe × 3 mol O2 / 4 mol Fe = 1.5 mol O2.

The balanced equation says 3 mol O2 react for every 4 mol Fe, so multiply the moles of Fe by 3/4.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

For C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O, how many moles of water form from 0.75 mol propane?

Hint

Find the coefficients of propane and water. Set up the mole ratio with mol H2O on top and mol C3H8 on the bottom.

Answer

0.75 × 4/1 = 3.0 mol H2O.

One mole of propane makes 4 mol of water (coefficients 1 and 4), so multiply by 4/1; the ratio is exact, so the answer keeps two significant figures like 0.75.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Write all six mole ratios for 2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2.

Hint

Three substances make three pairs. Each pair's coefficients give one ratio, and each ratio can be written either way up.

Answer

2 mol H2O2/2 mol H2O and its inverse; 2 mol H2O2/1 mol O2 and its inverse; 2 mol H2O/1 mol O2 and its inverse.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

For 2 Al(s) + 3 CuCl2(aq) → 2 AlCl3(aq) + 3 Cu(s), how many moles of Al are needed to react completely with 0.45 mol CuCl2?

Hint

Put mol Al on top and mol CuCl2 on the bottom, using their coefficients from the balanced equation.

Answer

0.45 mol CuCl2 × 2 mol Al / 3 mol CuCl2 = 0.30 mol Al.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Sodium carbonate reacts with hydrochloric acid: Na2CO3(aq) + 2 HCl(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). How many moles of CO2 form along with 0.84 mol NaCl? (Work from one product to another.)

Hint

You can go from one product to another: the coefficients of NaCl and CO2 make the mole ratio. Put mol CO2 on top.

Answer

0.84 mol NaCl × 1 mol CO2 / 2 mol NaCl = 0.42 mol CO2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

Write the balanced equation for the combination of sodium metal with chlorine gas, then find the moles of Cl2 used to make 5.0 mol NaCl.

Hint

Sodium is Na; chlorine is diatomic, Cl2; Na+ and Cl− swap-drop to NaCl. Balance first, then use the Cl2 : NaCl ratio.

Answer

2 Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2 NaCl(s). 5.0 mol NaCl × 1 mol Cl2 / 2 mol NaCl = 2.5 mol Cl2.
