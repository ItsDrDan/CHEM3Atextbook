Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish theoretical yield from actual yield.
  • Calculate percent yield and judge whether it is reasonable.
  • Distinguish percent yield from percent error.
  • Find the actual yield from a given percent yield, and combine a limiting reactant with percent yield.
  • Avoid the most common percent-yield mistake: comparing masses of two different substances.

Everything you have calculated so far assumes the reaction runs perfectly: every bit of the limiting reactant turns into product, and you collect all of it. Conservation of mass is a true law, but in a real lab some product always goes missing. Where does it go?

  • It is left behind during transfers: on the stirring rod, the spatula, the walls of the beaker, the filter paper.
  • The reaction did not go to completion. The procedure said 15 minutes, but your lab partner wanted to leave early, so it ran for 5.
  • A side reaction made a different product.
  • The reactants were not pure to begin with.
  • Plain human error: a spill, a misread scale, too much rinse water.

Comparing what you got with what was possible tells you how well the experiment went, and where to improve.

Theoretical, Actual, and Percent Yield

  • The theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product predicted by stoichiometry from the limiting reactant (Sections 8.2 and 8.4).
  • The actual yield is the amount you actually collect and weigh in the lab.
  • The percent yield compares them:

\( \text{percent yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\% \)

A percent yield is normally less than 100%. A yield above 100% means something is wrong: usually the product is still wet, or it contains an impurity.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Aluminum chloride

Problem. In Section 8.4 (Example 2) the theoretical yield of AlCl3 was 31.3 g. A chemist collects 27.8 g. What is the percent yield?

Solution.

\( \dfrac{27.8\ \text{g}}{31.34\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 88.7\% \)

Check. The actual yield is a little less than the theoretical, so the percent is a little less than 100.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Making aspirin

Problem. Aspirin is made from salicylic acid: C7H6O3 + C4H6O3 → C9H8O4 + C2H4O2. Starting with 2.00 g of salicylic acid (138.122 g/mol) and excess acetic anhydride, a student collects 2.18 g of aspirin (180.159 g/mol). Find the theoretical yield and percent yield.

Solution. Theoretical yield:

\( 2.00\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_3}{138.122\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol C}_9\text{H}_8\text{O}_4}{1\ \text{mol C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_3} \times \dfrac{180.159\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 2.61\ \text{g} \)

Percent yield: 2.18 g ÷ 2.609 g × 100% = 83.6%.

Check. Salicylic acid is the limiting reactant (the other is in excess), so it sets the theoretical yield.

Is 84% good? For an introductory lab, yes. A yield around 75% is a good result for many reactions, and in multi-step organic synthesis a chemist can be happy with 5 to 10% for a difficult step.

The Most Common Percent-Yield Mistake

The actual yield and the theoretical yield must be masses of the same substance, the product. A very common mistake is to divide the mass of the reactant you started with by the mass of product you collected. Those are different substances with different molar masses, so the ratio means nothing. First convert the reactant to the theoretical yield of product, then compare.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Lithium hydroxide scrubbing CO2

Problem. Lithium hydroxide removes carbon dioxide from the air in spacecraft and closed breathing systems: 2 LiOH(s) + CO2(g) → Li2CO3(s) + H2O(l). In a test, 20.0 g of LiOH with excess CO2 gives 24.0 g of Li2CO3. What is the percent yield? (LiOH 23.95 g/mol; Li2CO3 73.89 g/mol)

Solution. Not 20.0 g ÷ 24.0 g × 100% = 83.3%: that compares grams of LiOH with grams of Li2CO3. Find the theoretical yield of Li2CO3 first (the balanced equation gives 2 mol LiOH : 1 mol Li2CO3):

\( 20.0\ \text{g LiOH} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol LiOH}}{23.95\ \text{g LiOH}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Li}_2\text{CO}_3}{2\ \text{mol LiOH}} \times \dfrac{73.89\ \text{g Li}_2\text{CO}_3}{1\ \text{mol Li}_2\text{CO}_3} = 30.85\ \text{g Li}_2\text{CO}_3 \)

\( \text{percent yield} = \dfrac{24.0\ \text{g}}{30.85\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 77.8\% \)

Check. Both numbers in the percent-yield fraction are grams of Li2CO3. The theoretical yield (30.9 g, unrounded 30.85 g kept for the next step) is larger than the actual yield, as it should be. The wrong-way 83.3% looks believable, which is what makes this mistake easy to miss.

Working Backward: Actual Yield from Percent Yield

The actual yield is always measured in the lab or given to you; you cannot calculate it from the equation alone. The one exception is when the problem gives you the percent yield. Then rearrange the formula:

\( \text{actual yield} = \dfrac{\text{percent yield}}{100\%} \times \text{theoretical yield} \)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): How much will we really get?

Problem. Magnesium burns in oxygen: 2 Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2 MgO(s). A student burns 12.0 g of Mg in excess O2, and the procedure typically gives an 85.0% yield. What mass of MgO should the student expect to collect? (Mg 24.31 g/mol; MgO 40.31 g/mol)

Solution. Theoretical yield first:

\( 12.0\ \text{g Mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Mg}}{24.31\ \text{g Mg}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol MgO}}{2\ \text{mol Mg}} \times \dfrac{40.31\ \text{g MgO}}{1\ \text{mol MgO}} = 19.90\ \text{g MgO} \)

Then the actual yield:

\( 0.850 \times 19.90\ \text{g} = 16.9\ \text{g MgO} \)

Check. 85% of about 20 g is about 17 g, and the actual yield is less than the theoretical, as it must be.

Limiting reactant and percent yield together

When amounts of two reactants are given, find the limiting reactant first (Section 8.4); its product amount is the theoretical yield. For example, a reaction box holds 9 H2 molecules and 4 N2 molecules (N2 + 3 H2 → 2 NH3). H2 could make 9 × 2/3 = 6 NH3; N2 could make 4 × 2 = 8. H2 limits, so the theoretical yield is 6 NH3 molecules. If only 4 NH3 molecules form, the percent yield is 4 ÷ 6 × 100% = 66.7%.

Professor Tip

Do not confuse percent yield with percent error, which equals |measured − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100%. Percent yield asks, "How much of the possible product did I collect?" Percent error asks, "How close is my measurement to the accepted value?" Also, always find the theoretical yield from the limiting reactant; using the excess reactant makes your percent yield look too small.

In the Lab: Experiment 11a, Synthesis of Alum

In this experiment you turn scrap aluminum into crystals of alum, potassium aluminum sulfate dodecahydrate, KAl(SO4)2·12H2O, and collect them by vacuum filtration. Each Al atom ends up in one formula unit of alum, so the moles of aluminum set the theoretical yield. The hydrate's molar mass is large, 474.37 g/mol with yellow-sheet values, because of its 12 waters: 0.500 g of Al (0.01853 mol) could make at most 8.79 g of alum. If you collect 7.25 g, your percent yield is 82.5%. Part of the lab is deciding whether a yield is reasonable: crystals lost in the filtrate lower it; crystals that are still wet raise it, sometimes above 100%.

Major Connection: Chemistry and Pharmaceutical Science

Making a modern drug can take 10 or more reaction steps, and the overall yield is the product of the yields of every step. Ten steps at 90% each give 0.9010 ≈ 35% overall; at 70% each, only about 3%. That is why process chemists work hard to raise the yield of every step: a few percent per step can decide whether a medicine is affordable to produce.

Common Mistakes

  • Dividing theoretical by actual (the ratio is actual ÷ theoretical).
  • Using the excess reactant to find the theoretical yield.
  • Accepting a yield over 100% without questioning it.
  • Dividing the reactant's mass by the product's mass. Convert to the theoretical yield of the product first; both numbers must be the same substance.
  • Calculating an actual yield from the equation. The actual yield is measured or given, or found from a given percent yield.

Summary

  • Theoretical yield: stoichiometric maximum from the limiting reactant. Actual yield: what you collect.
  • Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100%, normally below 100%. Both yields are amounts of the same product.
  • Actual yield = (percent yield ÷ 100%) × theoretical yield, when the percent yield is given.
  • Product is lost in transfers, incomplete reactions, side reactions, impure reactants, and human error.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A reaction has a theoretical yield of 45.0 g and an actual yield of 38.2 g. What is the percent yield?

Hint

Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100%. Which number is the actual yield?

Answer

38.2 ÷ 45.0 × 100% = 84.9%.

The actual yield (38.2 g) goes on top and the theoretical yield (45.0 g) on the bottom, so the result is a little below 100%, as expected.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Heating 10.0 g of CaCO3 (100.09 g/mol) gives 5.25 g of CaO (56.08 g/mol). What is the percent yield? CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

Hint

Find the theoretical yield of CaO first (grams CaCO3 → mol → mol CaO → grams CaO). Then compare grams of CaO with grams of CaO.

Answer

Theoretical: 10.0/100.09 × 56.08 = 5.603 g. Percent: 5.25/5.603 × 100% = 93.7%.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A student reports a 112% yield of a solid that was dried for only five minutes. What is the most likely explanation?

Hint

A yield above 100% means the weighed product holds something extra. What could still be in a solid that was dried for only five minutes?

Answer

The product still contains water (it is not dry), so its mass is too high. Drying it longer would lower the measured mass and the percent yield.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Iron is made by Fe2O3 + 3 CO → 2 Fe + 3 CO2. If 500. g of Fe2O3 has a theoretical yield of 350. g of Fe (Section 8.2, Exercise 3) and the process runs at 92.0% yield, what mass of iron is actually produced?

Hint

Rearrange: actual yield = (percent yield ÷ 100%) × theoretical yield. Use the unrounded theoretical yield and round once at the end.

Answer

Actual = 0.920 × 349.7 g = 322 g Fe. (Use the unrounded theoretical yield, 349.7 g, and round once at the end.)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

In Section 8.4, Example 3, the theoretical yield of AlBr3 was 20.7 g (Br2 limiting). If 18.2 g of AlBr3 is collected, what is the percent yield? A classmate instead divides 18.2 g by the 3.00 g of Al used. What is wrong with that?

Hint

Both numbers in a percent yield must be grams of the same product, AlBr3. Is 3.00 g of Al a mass of product?

Answer

18.2 g ÷ 20.69 g × 100% = 88.0%. Dividing by 3.00 g of Al compares the product with a reactant (and with the excess reactant, at that); the percent yield must compare actual and theoretical amounts of the same product, from the limiting reactant.
