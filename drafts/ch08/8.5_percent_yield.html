Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish theoretical yield from actual yield.
  • Calculate percent yield and judge whether it is reasonable.
  • Distinguish percent yield from percent error.

Everything you have calculated so far assumes the reaction runs perfectly: every bit of the limiting reactant turns into product, and you collect all of it. Real reactions fall short. Some product sticks to the glassware or the filter paper, side reactions make other products, and some reactant never reacts.

Theoretical, Actual, and Percent Yield

  • The theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product predicted by stoichiometry from the limiting reactant (Sections 8.2 and 8.4).
  • The actual yield is the amount you actually collect and weigh in the lab.
  • The percent yield compares them:

\( \text{percent yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\% \)

A percent yield is normally less than 100%. A yield above 100% means something is wrong: usually the product is still wet, or it contains an impurity.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Aluminum chloride

Problem. In Section 8.4 (Example 2) the theoretical yield of AlCl3 was 31.3 g. A chemist collects 27.8 g. What is the percent yield?

Solution.

\( \dfrac{27.8\ \text{g}}{31.34\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 88.7\% \)

Check. The actual yield is a little less than the theoretical, so the percent is a little less than 100.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Making aspirin

Problem. Aspirin is made from salicylic acid: C7H6O3 + C4H6O3 → C9H8O4 + C2H4O2. Starting with 2.00 g of salicylic acid (138.122 g/mol) and excess acetic anhydride, a student collects 2.18 g of aspirin (180.159 g/mol). Find the theoretical yield and percent yield.

Solution. Theoretical yield:

\( 2.00\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_3}{138.122\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol C}_9\text{H}_8\text{O}_4}{1\ \text{mol C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_3} \times \dfrac{180.159\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 2.61\ \text{g} \)

Percent yield: 2.18 g ÷ 2.609 g × 100% = 83.6%.

Check. Salicylic acid is the limiting reactant (the other is in excess), so it sets the theoretical yield.

Professor Tip

Do not confuse percent yield with percent error (Chapter 1). Percent yield asks, "How much of the possible product did I collect?" Percent error asks, "How close is my measurement to the accepted value?" Also, always find the theoretical yield from the limiting reactant; using the excess reactant makes your percent yield look too small.

In the Lab: Experiment 11a, Synthesis of Alum

In this experiment you turn scrap aluminum into crystals of alum, potassium aluminum sulfate dodecahydrate, KAl(SO4)2·12H2O, and collect them by vacuum filtration. Each Al atom ends up in one formula unit of alum, so the moles of aluminum set the theoretical yield. The hydrate's molar mass is large, 474.37 g/mol with yellow-sheet values, because of its 12 waters: 0.500 g of Al (0.01853 mol) could make at most 8.79 g of alum. If you collect 7.25 g, your percent yield is 82.5%. Part of the lab is deciding whether a yield is reasonable: crystals lost in the filtrate lower it; crystals that are still wet raise it, sometimes above 100%.

Major Connection: Chemistry and Pharmaceutical Science

Making a modern drug can take 10 or more reaction steps, and the overall yield is the product of the yields of every step. Ten steps at 90% each give 0.9010 ≈ 35% overall; at 70% each, only about 3%. That is why process chemists work hard to raise the yield of every step: a few percent per step can decide whether a medicine is affordable to produce.

Common Mistakes

  • Dividing theoretical by actual (the ratio is actual ÷ theoretical).
  • Using the excess reactant to find the theoretical yield.
  • Accepting a yield over 100% without questioning it.

Summary

  • Theoretical yield: stoichiometric maximum from the limiting reactant. Actual yield: what you collect.
  • Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100%, normally below 100%.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A reaction has a theoretical yield of 45.0 g and an actual yield of 38.2 g. What is the percent yield?

Answer

38.2 ÷ 45.0 × 100% = 84.9%.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Heating 10.0 g of CaCO3 (100.09 g/mol) gives 5.25 g of CaO (56.08 g/mol). What is the percent yield? CaCO3 → CaO + CO2

Answer

Theoretical: 10.0/100.09 × 56.08 = 5.603 g. Percent: 5.25/5.603 × 100% = 93.7%.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A student reports a 112% yield of a solid that was dried for only five minutes. What is the most likely explanation?

Answer

The product still contains water (it is not dry), so its mass is too high. Drying it longer would lower the measured mass and the percent yield.
