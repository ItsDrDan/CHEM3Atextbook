Biology Connection: The Recipe in Every Cell

Right now, trillions of your cells are running the same reaction: they take glucose from your blood and oxygen from your lungs and turn them into carbon dioxide and water, capturing the energy released to make ATP.

C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O

The balanced equation is a recipe with exact proportions. For every molecule of glucose your cells use, they need six molecules of O2 and they make six molecules of CO2, which your blood carries back to the lungs to breathe out. Because those proportions are fixed, a respiratory therapist can relate how much oxygen a person uses to how much carbon dioxide they exhale, and a dietitian can relate grams of food to the energy it provides.

Stoichiometry (stoy-kee-AH-meh-tree, from Greek words for "element" and "measure") is the art of using these recipes to answer "how much?": how much product a reaction can make, how much of each reactant it needs, which reactant runs out first, and how much heat it releases. In this chapter you will learn to answer all of those questions with the mole road map you built in Chapter 5.

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Write mole ratios from a balanced equation and do mole-to-mole conversions (Section 8.1).
  • Convert between masses of reactants and products (Section 8.2).
  • Use molarity and molar volume at STP as conversion factors in stoichiometry (Section 8.3).
  • Identify the limiting reactant and find the amount of product and leftover reactant (Section 8.4).
  • Calculate theoretical yield and percent yield (Section 8.5).
  • Use the enthalpy of reaction to find the heat released or absorbed by a given amount of reaction (Section 8.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Pharmacists, lab technicians, nurses preparing solutions, engineers designing fuel systems, and farmers planning fertilizer all depend on stoichiometry. It connects the balanced equations of Chapter 7 to real amounts you can weigh and measure, and it is the calculation behind gas volumes (Chapter 10), solution reactions (Chapter 11), and acid–base titrations (Chapter 12).
