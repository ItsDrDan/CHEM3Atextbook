Learning Objectives

  • Use the stoichiometry road map to convert grams of one substance into grams of another.
  • Combine molar masses and mole ratios in one setup.
  • Extend the road map to particles with Avogadro's number, and start or finish a problem in moles, grams, or particles.
  • Round a stoichiometry answer to the measured given, and state what the answer means in a sentence.

In the lab and the pharmacy, nobody measures moles directly; there is no "mole-o-meter" on the bench. People weigh grams on a balance. A mass-to-mass problem asks, "If I start with this many grams of A, how many grams of B can I make (or do I need)?" The answer always goes through moles, because only moles have a ratio in the balanced equation. As the lectures put it: our goal is the mole.

The Road Map

  1. Balance the equation.
  2. grams of A → moles of A (divide by the molar mass of A).
  3. moles of A → moles of B (mole ratio from the coefficients).
  4. moles of B → grams of B (multiply by the molar mass of B).

Lay out every problem the same way: Given (the measured number, with its unit and formula), Desired (the unit and formula you want, with "???"), and the conversion factors in between. Not every problem goes all the way from grams to grams. Start on the map wherever the given is, stop wherever the desired is, and count the arrows.

Particles on the map

The mole is the hub on both sides of the mole ratio, so Avogadro's number (6.0221 × 1023 particles per mole, Section 5.1) gives one more on-ramp and off-ramp on each side:

grams A ↔ mol A ↔ particles A   |   mol A → (mole ratio) → mol B   |   mol B ↔ grams B or particles B

Divide grams by molar mass, or divide particles by 6.0221 × 1023, to get moles; multiply to go back. The bridge between A and B is still the mole ratio.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): CO2 from glucose

Problem. How many grams of CO2 are produced when your cells completely oxidize 25.0 g of glucose? (C6H12O6 180.156 g/mol; CO2 44.009 g/mol)

Solution. C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O.

\( {25.0\;\text{g C}}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6}{180.156\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{6\;\text{mol CO}_2}{1\;\text{mol C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6} \times \dfrac{44.009\;\text{g CO}_2}{1\;\text{mol CO}_2} = {36.6\;\text{g CO}}_2 \)

Check. The CO2 outweighs the glucose because oxygen from the air is added. By conservation of mass, 25.0 g glucose + 26.6 g O2 = 51.6 g = 36.6 g CO2 + 15.0 g H2O.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Ammonia for fertilizer

Problem. How many grams of NH3 can be made from 100. g of N2 and plenty of H2? (N2 28.014 g/mol; NH3 17.031 g/mol)

Solution. N2 + 3 H2 → 2 NH3.

\( {100.\;\text{g N}}_2 \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol N}_2}{28.014\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{2\;\text{mol NH}_3}{1\;\text{mol N}_2} \times \dfrac{17.031\;\text{g NH}_3}{1\;\text{mol NH}_3} = {122\;\text{g NH}}_3 \)

Check. All of the nitrogen ends up in ammonia, plus the added hydrogen, so a little more than 100 g of product makes sense. In a sentence: reacting 100. g of N2 completely, with plenty of H2, should make 122 g of NH3. The calculated answer is how much we should make; Section 8.5 compares it with how much a real reaction gives.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Grams of fuel to molecules of CO2

Problem. A camp stove burns 5.00 g of propane: C3H8(g) + 5 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 4 H2O(g). How many molecules of CO2 are produced? (C3H8 44.097 g/mol)

Given: 5.00 g C3H8. Desired: ??? molecules CO2. Map: grams C3H8 → mol C3H8 → mol CO2 → molecules CO2.

Solution.

\( {5.00\;\text{g C}}_3\text{H}_8 \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8}{44.097\;\text{g C}_3\text{H}_8} \times \dfrac{3\;\text{mol CO}_2}{1\;\text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\;\text{molecules CO}_2}{1\;\text{mol CO}_2} = {2.05 \times 10^{23}\;\text{molecules CO}}_2 \)

Check. 5.00 g is about 0.113 mol of propane, which makes 3 times as many moles of CO2 (0.340 mol), about a third of Avogadro's number of molecules.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Butane, from balancing to grams

Problem. A butane lighter burns 15.0 g of butane, C4H10. What mass of CO2 is produced? (C4H10 58.124 g/mol; CO2 44.009 g/mol)

Solution. Step 1, balance. With one butane, C gives 4 CO2 and H gives 5 H2O, so the oxygen on the right is 8 + 5 = 13, which is odd. Double the hydrocarbon (Section 7.2): 2 C4H10(g) + 13 O2(g) → 8 CO2(g) + 10 H2O(g). Steps 2–4:

\( {15.0\;\text{g C}}_4\text{H}_{10} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol C}_4\text{H}_{10}}{58.124\;\text{g C}_4\text{H}_{10}} \times \dfrac{8\;\text{mol CO}_2}{2\;\text{mol C}_4\text{H}_{10}} \times \dfrac{44.009\;\text{g CO}_2}{1\;\text{mol CO}_2} = {45.4\;\text{g CO}}_2 \)

Check. Each butane carbon ends up in CO2, which is heavier than the fuel, so a mass of CO2 about three times the fuel's is reasonable. The answer has 3 significant figures because the measured mass, 15.0 g, has 3. The molar masses (5 significant figures) and the exact coefficients are not what limits you; the measured number you started with is.

Professor Tip

Two pitfalls from the lectures: never put a coefficient into a molar mass (the molar mass of K is 39.10 g/mol even when the equation says 2 K), and never "add mol" to a given number that was in grams. "How many grams of water form from 12 g of hydrogen?" gives 12 g, not 12 mol; the gram-to-mole step needs the molar mass of H2, 2.016 g/mol (answer: 1.1 × 102 g H2O). Label every unit with its formula (write "mol Li," not just "mol") so you can see which factors cancel. Set up the whole chain of factors in one line, check that every unit cancels except the one you want, and press enter once on the TI-36X Pro.

Biology Connection: Microbiology

Yeast running out of oxygen switch to fermentation: C6H12O6 → 2 C2H5OH + 2 CO2. Bakers rely on the CO2 to raise bread; brewers and winemakers in California rely on the ethanol. The same stoichiometry applies: 50.0 g of glucose can produce at most 25.6 g of ethanol and 24.4 g of CO2. Many bacteria ferment too, and microbiology labs identify species partly by which acids and gases they produce from a sugar, for example by watching for gas bubbles trapped in a small inverted tube in the growth broth.

Common Mistakes

  • Skipping moles: grams of A × mole ratio is meaningless.
  • Using the wrong molar mass at each end: A's molar mass first, B's last.
  • Multiplying a molar mass by a coefficient.
  • Rounding to the molar mass. Significant figures follow the measured given, not the molar mass or the coefficients.
  • Writing "mol" for a mass. 12 g H2 is not 12 mol H2.

Summary

  • grams A → mol A → mol B → grams B, using molar mass, mole ratio, molar mass.
  • Always balance first; always pass through moles.
  • Particles join the map through Avogadro's number on either side of the mole ratio.
  • Round to the measured given, and read the answer as "how much we should make (or need)."

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many grams of O2 are needed to burn 10.0 g of methane? CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O (CH4 16.043, O2 31.998 g/mol)

Hint

Follow the road map: grams CH4 → mol CH4 → mol O2 (mole ratio) → grams O2. The goal is the mole.

Answer

10.0/16.043 = 0.6233 mol CH4; × 2 = 1.247 mol O2; × 31.998 = 39.9 g O2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

How many grams of H2O form when 36.6 g of CO2 form from glucose (Example 1)?

Hint

Grams of CO2 to moles first. Compare the coefficients of CO2 and H2O in the glucose equation, then use the molar mass of water.

Answer

The ratio is 6 CO2 : 6 H2O, so equal moles: 36.6/44.009 = 0.8316 mol; × 18.015 = 15.0 g H2O.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Iron is made from iron ore: Fe2O3 + 3 CO → 2 Fe + 3 CO2. How many grams of Fe come from 500. g of Fe2O3 (159.70 g/mol)?

Hint

Road map: grams Fe2O3 → mol Fe2O3 → mol Fe (2 Fe per Fe2O3) → grams Fe, using the molar mass of Fe from the yellow sheet.

Answer

500./159.70 = 3.131 mol; × 2 = 6.262 mol Fe; × 55.85 = 350. g Fe.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

How many moles of AgCl precipitate when 3.5 g of KCl in solution reacts with excess silver nitrate? KCl(aq) + AgNO3(aq) → AgCl(s) + KNO3(aq) (KCl 74.55 g/mol)

Hint

The desired unit is moles, so stop there: grams KCl → mol KCl → mol AgCl. Count the significant figures in the given mass.

Answer

3.5 g KCl × (1 mol KCl / 74.55 g) × (1 mol AgCl / 1 mol KCl) = 0.047 mol AgCl (2 significant figures, like 3.5 g). The problem stops at moles, so there is no last molar-mass step.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

What mass of N2 is needed to make 1.50 × 1024 molecules of NH3? N2 + 3 H2 → 2 NH3 (N2 28.014 g/mol)

Hint

Start on the particle on-ramp: divide molecules by Avogadro's number to get mol NH3. Then use the mole ratio and the molar mass of N2.

Answer

1.50 × 1024 ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 = 2.491 mol NH3; × (1 mol N2 / 2 mol NH3) = 1.245 mol N2; × 28.014 g/mol = 34.9 g N2.
