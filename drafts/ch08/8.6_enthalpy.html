Learning Objectives

  • Classify reactions and processes as exothermic or endothermic from the sign of ΔH.
  • Read a thermochemical equation.
  • Use ΔH as a conversion factor to find the heat released or absorbed by a given amount of reactant or product.
  • Read heat written as a reactant or a product, and write the conversion factors one ΔH gives for every substance in the equation.
  • Turn a word problem into a balanced thermochemical equation and work backward from heat to mass.

Burning natural gas heats a home, a cold pack chills a sprained ankle, and the food you eat powers your muscles. Every chemical reaction either releases or absorbs heat, and the amount is proportional to how much reacts. That makes heat one more quantity on the stoichiometry road map.

Exothermic and Endothermic

The enthalpy of reaction, ΔH (delta H), is the heat absorbed or released by a reaction at constant pressure, the conditions of most reactions in open containers and in your body. It is usually given in kilojoules (kJ).

  • Exothermic: heat is released to the surroundings, which warm up. ΔH is negative. Combustion, cellular respiration, and neutralization are exothermic.
  • Endothermic: heat is absorbed from the surroundings, which cool down. ΔH is positive. Photosynthesis, decomposing CaCO3 by heating, and dissolving ammonium nitrate in an instant cold pack are endothermic.

Thermochemical Equations

A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation with its ΔH:

CH4(g) + 2 O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2 H2O(l)    ΔH = −890.3 kJ

The ΔH belongs to the amounts in the equation as written: burning 1 mol of CH4 with 2 mol of O2 releases 890.3 kJ. So ΔH gives new conversion factors, such as 890.3 kJ released / 1 mol CH4 or 890.3 kJ released / 2 mol O2. If you double the equation, you double ΔH; if you reverse it, you change the sign.

Heat written into the equation

You will also see the heat written right in the equation, as if it were a reactant or a product. An exothermic reaction makes heat, so heat appears as a product (A → B + heat); an endothermic reaction uses heat, so heat appears as a reactant (A + heat → B). Each pair of lines below says the same thing:

C3H8(g) + 5 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 4 H2O(g)    ΔH = −2044 kJ
C3H8(g) + 5 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 4 H2O(g) + 2044 kJ

CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)    ΔH = +178 kJ
CaCO3(s) + 178 kJ → CaO(s) + CO2(g)

When heat is written in the equation, it carries no sign: its side of the arrow tells you whether it is released or absorbed.

One ΔH, a conversion factor for every substance

Because ΔH goes with the whole balanced equation, it pairs with the coefficient of every substance. For propane, −2044 kJ goes with each of these:

\( \dfrac{-2044\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8} \qquad \dfrac{-2044\ \text{kJ}}{5\ \text{mol O}_2} \qquad \dfrac{-2044\ \text{kJ}}{3\ \text{mol CO}_2} \qquad \dfrac{-2044\ \text{kJ}}{4\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}} \)

Pick the one that matches the substance you are given. For example, when 3.20 mol of O2 reacts with propane: 3.20 mol O2 × (2044 kJ / 5 mol O2) = 1.31 × 103 kJ released. (Some problems give propane ΔH = −2.220 × 103 kJ instead. That value is for water formed as a liquid, H2O(l); −2044 kJ is for water formed as a gas, H2O(g). The value depends on whether water forms as a gas or a liquid, so use the ΔH given with the equation.) A useful cue: if a problem gives or asks for an amount of energy (kJ, J, or Calories) along with a reaction, it is almost always an enthalpy-stoichiometry problem.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Heat from burning methane

Problem. How much heat is released when 10.0 g of methane (16.043 g/mol) burns?

Solution.

\( 10.0\ \text{g CH}_4 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol CH}_4}{16.043\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{890.3\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \text{mol CH}_4} = 555\ \text{kJ released} \)

Check. 10.0 g is a bit more than half a mole, so a bit more than half of 890 kJ. Report it as 555 kJ released, or q = −555 kJ for the reaction.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Working backward

Problem. What mass of methane must burn to release 1.00 × 103 kJ?

Solution.

\( 1.00 \times 10^{3}\ \text{kJ} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol CH}_4}{890.3\ \text{kJ}} \times \dfrac{16.043\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol CH}_4} = 18.0\ \text{g CH}_4 \)

Check. A little more than 1 mol of reaction's worth of heat needs a little more than 1 mol (16 g) of methane.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): From a word problem to grams

Problem. Aluminum oxide is the solid formed when aluminum powder is ignited in oxygen gas. For the balanced reaction, ΔH = −3351 kJ. What mass of aluminum oxide forms when 1.25 × 103 kJ of heat is released? (Al2O3 101.96 g/mol)

Solution. Write the equation from the words. Aluminum oxide is a product: Al3+ and O2− swap-drop to Al2O3. The reactants are Al(s) and the diatomic gas O2(g). Balance. Even the odds on oxygen (2 and 3 make 6): 3 O2 and 2 Al2O3; then the lonely Al: 4 Al.

4 Al(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Al2O3(s)    ΔH = −3351 kJ

Set up. Start with the given that is not a fraction, 1.25 × 103 kJ; a ratio such as 3351 kJ per 2 mol could be written either way up, but a lone given cannot, so starting with it keeps the setup from flipping. The heat pairs with the coefficient of Al2O3, 2 mol:

\( 1.25 \times 10^{3}\ \text{kJ} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol Al}_2\text{O}_3}{3351\ \text{kJ}} \times \dfrac{101.96\ \text{g Al}_2\text{O}_3}{1\ \text{mol Al}_2\text{O}_3} = 76.1\ \text{g Al}_2\text{O}_3 \)

Check. 1250 kJ is a bit more than a third of 3351 kJ, so a bit more than a third of 2 mol (0.746 mol) of Al2O3 forms. Both quantities are "released," so the signs agree and can be dropped. In a sentence: forming 76.1 g of aluminum oxide releases 1.25 × 103 kJ of heat.

Professor Tip

Treat "kJ" like the name of another product in the equation. The coefficient of the substance you are given and the ΔH of the equation form the ratio. Watch the wording: "released" or "given off" goes with negative ΔH, "absorbed" or "required" with positive ΔH. In your final answer, state the amount of heat and whether it is released or absorbed.

Biology Connection: Food Calories

The Calorie on a food label (capital C) is a kilocalorie: 1 Cal = 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ. Your body releases the same total energy from glucose as burning it in a flame: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O, ΔH = −2803 kJ. Per gram, that is 2803 kJ ÷ 180.156 g = 15.6 kJ, or about 3.7 Cal, close to the familiar "4 Calories per gram" used for carbohydrates on nutrition labels. Your cells release this energy in small steps rather than all at once, capturing part of it as ATP and releasing the rest as the body heat that keeps you at about 37 °C.

Valley Connection: Dairy Biogas

The San Joaquin Valley is home to most of California's dairy cows, and the manure they produce releases methane, a powerful greenhouse gas, as bacteria break it down. Many Valley dairies now capture that methane in covered lagoons called digesters and use the gas as fuel, much of it cleaned up and piped as renewable natural gas or used to run vehicles. California's Dairy Digester Research and Development Program, run by the state Department of Food and Agriculture, has helped fund more than 100 digesters, many of them in Valley counties such as Tulare, Kings, Merced, and Kern. Burning the captured methane follows the thermochemical equation above: every 16 g of CH4 releases about 890 kJ of heat, and the carbon leaves as CO2, a much weaker greenhouse gas than the methane would have been.

Sources: California Department of Food and Agriculture, Dairy Digester Research and Development Program (cdfa.ca.gov); California Air Resources Board, dairy and livestock methane programs (ww2.arb.ca.gov).

Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up the sign: negative ΔH means heat released (exothermic).
  • Ignoring the coefficient: ΔH goes with the moles in the balanced equation, which may not be 1.
  • Using grams directly with ΔH: convert to moles first.
  • Pairing ΔH with the wrong coefficient. −2044 kJ goes with 5 mol O2 or 4 mol H2O, not 1 mol of each.
  • Adding a sign to heat written in the equation. "+ 2044 kJ" on the product side means 2044 kJ released (ΔH = −2044 kJ).

Summary

  • Exothermic: ΔH < 0, heat released. Endothermic: ΔH > 0, heat absorbed.
  • ΔH applies to the moles in the balanced equation; it is a conversion factor between moles and kJ.
  • grams → moles → kJ, using molar mass and ΔH per moles in the equation.
  • Exothermic: heat written as a product. Endothermic: heat written as a reactant.
  • One ΔH gives a conversion factor for every substance's coefficient. Start a setup with the given that is not a fraction.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Is each exothermic or endothermic: (a) ΔH = +178 kJ; (b) a hand warmer heating up; (c) water evaporating from your skin?

Hint

A positive ΔH means heat is absorbed. For (b) and (c), ask whether the surroundings (your hand, your skin) get warmer or colder.

Answer

(a) endothermic. (b) exothermic. (c) endothermic (it absorbs heat from your skin, which is why sweating cools you).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

For C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O, ΔH = −2803 kJ, how much heat is released by 9.00 g of glucose?

Hint

Grams glucose → mol glucose (molar mass from the yellow sheet), then use 2803 kJ per 1 mol glucose, its coefficient, as the conversion factor.

Answer

9.00/180.156 = 0.04996 mol; × 2803 kJ/mol = 140. kJ released.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l), ΔH = −571.6 kJ. How much heat is released when 4.00 mol of H2 reacts?

Hint

ΔH goes with the equation as written, 2 mol H2. Use 571.6 kJ / 2 mol H2 as the conversion factor.

Answer

4.00 mol H2 × 571.6 kJ / 2 mol H2 = 1.14 × 103 kJ released.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Rewrite 2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l), ΔH = −571.6 kJ, with the heat written in the equation. Then write the conversion factor that links the heat to H2O.

Hint

A negative ΔH means exothermic, so heat is a product: write it on the right side, with no sign. Then pair the heat with the coefficient of H2O.

Answer

2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l) + 571.6 kJ (exothermic, so heat is a product). Conversion factor: 571.6 kJ released / 2 mol H2O (or its inverse).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Neutralization releases heat: NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l), ΔH = −55.8 kJ. How much heat is released when 4.00 g of NaOH (40.00 g/mol) is neutralized by excess HCl?

Hint

Start with the given, 4.00 g NaOH: grams → mol, then 55.8 kJ released per 1 mol NaOH.

Answer

Start with the given, 4.00 g NaOH: 4.00 g × (1 mol / 40.00 g) × (55.8 kJ / 1 mol NaOH) = 5.58 kJ released.
