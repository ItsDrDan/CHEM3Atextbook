10.1 Kinetic Molecular Theory

Problem 10.1

Which statement is not part of kinetic molecular theory for an ideal gas?

A. The particles are in constant, random, straight-line motion.
B. The particles attract one another, which holds the gas together.
C. Collisions between particles and with the walls are elastic.
D. The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the Kelvin temperature.

Hint

Compare each choice with the KMT list in Section 10.1. A gas spreads out to fill any container: what does that say about forces between its particles?

Answer

B. Kinetic molecular theory assumes the particles neither attract nor repel each other. Nothing holds a gas together, which is why it spreads out to fill any container. (Attractions do matter for real gases near condensation, which is why the ideal model fails there.)

  • A is part of the theory: constant random motion explains diffusion and why a gas fills its container.
  • C is part of the theory: in elastic collisions no kinetic energy is lost overall, so a gas left alone at constant temperature never "runs down" and its pressure stays steady.
  • D is part of the theory, and it is the reason every gas law needs temperatures in kelvins.

Problem 10.2

What causes the pressure of the oxygen inside a sealed cylinder?

A. The O2 molecules are attracted to the steel walls and pull on them.
B. The O2 molecules swell when they are warm and press against the walls.
C. The O2 molecules constantly collide with the walls, and each collision pushes on them.
D. The weight of the gas presses down on the bottom of the cylinder.

Hint

Pressure is force per area. In KMT, what are gas particles constantly doing to the walls? Heating changes their speed, not their size.

Answer

C. Pressure is force per area. Each molecule that hits a wall gives it a tiny push, and billions upon billions of hits every second add up to a steady pressure on every wall, not just the bottom.

  • A is wrong: kinetic molecular theory assumes no attractions, and a pull would not explain why the gas pushes outward on the walls.
  • B is a common misconception. Heating makes molecules move faster, not grow larger; faster molecules hit the walls harder and more often.
  • D is wrong: a gas pushes equally on the top, sides, and bottom of its container. The weight of a gas this small is negligible compared with its collisions.

Problem 10.3

A basketball is pumped up in a warm gym and then left in a car overnight on a cold January night in Fresno. The next morning it feels soft. (a) Explain why, using kinetic molecular theory. (b) After the ball sits in the warm gym for an hour, it feels firm again, even though no air was added. Explain.

Hint

Answer with particles: how does temperature change how fast the air molecules move, and how hard and how often they hit the inside of the ball? Their number stays the same.

Answer

(a) As the air inside cools, its molecules slow down (their average kinetic energy is proportional to the Kelvin temperature). Slower molecules hit the inside of the ball less often and less hard, so the pressure inside drops, and the ball feels soft.

(b) Warming speeds the molecules back up. They hit the walls harder and more often again, so the pressure rises back to where it was. The number of molecules never changed; only their speed did.

Problem 10.4

A sample of helium is at 310. K (about body temperature). To what temperature must it be heated to double the average kinetic energy of its atoms?

A. 155 K
B. 347 K
C. 310. K
D. 620. K

Hint

Average kinetic energy is proportional to the Kelvin temperature. What must happen to the Kelvin temperature to double the kinetic energy? Do not switch to °C.

Answer

D. Average kinetic energy is directly proportional to the Kelvin temperature, so doubling the kinetic energy means doubling the Kelvin temperature: 2 × 310. K = 620. K (the factor 2 is exact, so the answer keeps three significant figures).

  • A halves the temperature, which would halve the kinetic energy.
  • B doubles the Celsius temperature: 310. K is 36.85 °C, twice that is 73.7 °C, or 347 K. Kinetic energy is proportional to the Kelvin temperature, not the Celsius temperature.
  • C assumes that heating changes only the speed, not the kinetic energy. Faster atoms have more kinetic energy; that is what temperature measures.

Problem 10.5

Several gases are used in anesthesia and respiratory care: helium (He, 4.003 g/mol), oxygen (O2, 31.998 g/mol), nitrous oxide (N2O, 44.013 g/mol), and xenon (Xe, 131.29 g/mol), an anesthetic gas. (a) At the same temperature, which has the greatest average kinetic energy? (b) Rank them from slowest to fastest average speed. (c) Which would leak fastest through a tiny gap in a tubing connection?

Hint

(a) What sets the average kinetic energy? (b) With equal kinetic energy, lighter particles move faster: rank by molar mass. (c) Faster particles escape through small gaps sooner.

Answer

(a) None: at the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy.

(b) With equal kinetic energy, lighter particles must move faster. Slowest to fastest: Xe < N2O < O2 < He.

(c) Helium, the lightest and fastest. Its small, fast atoms escape through tiny openings more quickly than any of the others, which is why helium is also used to test equipment for leaks.

Problem 10.6

Under which conditions does a real gas behave most like an ideal gas?

A. High pressure and low temperature
B. High pressure and high temperature
C. Low pressure and high temperature
D. Low pressure and low temperature

Hint

An ideal gas has particles that take up no space and do not attract each other. Which pressure keeps particles far apart, and which temperature makes attractions unimportant?

Answer

C. At low pressure the particles are far apart, so their own volume is a tiny fraction of the container. At high temperature they move so fast that attractions between them hardly matter. Both match the assumptions of kinetic molecular theory.

  • A is the worst case: at high pressure the particles are crowded, and at low temperature attractions become important; this is how gases are liquefied, as with the liquid oxygen stored at hospitals.
  • B fixes the temperature but not the crowding: at high pressure the particles' own volume still matters.
  • D fixes the crowding but not the attractions: slow particles near the boiling point stick together.

10.2 Pressure and Its Units

Problem 10.7

A barometer at a ranger station in Yosemite Valley, about 4,000 ft above sea level, reads 656 mmHg. What is this pressure in atmospheres?

A. 1.16 atm
B. 0.863 atm
C. 4.99 × 105 atm
D. 6.47 atm

Hint

Use 1 atm = 760 mmHg with mmHg on the bottom. Check: at 4,000 ft there is less air above you, so should the answer be above or below 1 atm?

Answer

B.

\( 656\ \text{mmHg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{760\ \text{mmHg}} = 0.863\ \text{atm} \)

The answer makes sense: at 4,000 ft there is less air above you, so the pressure is below 1 atm.

  • A flips the conversion factor (760/656). A pressure above 1 atm at a high elevation should be a warning sign.
  • C multiplies by 760 instead of dividing. The mmHg units do not cancel, and no air pressure on Earth is near 500,000 atm.
  • D divides by 101.325, the number of kilopascals in 1 atm. That factor converts kPa, not mmHg.

Problem 10.8

Going further (beyond CHEM 3A; not on exams). A tire gauge on a car tire reads 35.0 psi. Like most gauges, it reads the pressure above the outside air pressure, which is 1.00 atm. (a) Convert the gauge reading to atm. (b) What is the actual (absolute) pressure of the air inside the tire, in atm? (c) Express the absolute pressure in kPa.

Hint

(a) Divide by 14.7 psi per atm. (b) The gauge reads above the outside pressure, so add the 1.00 atm of air. (c) Use 101.325 kPa per atm.

Answer

(a) \( 35.0\ \text{psi} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{14.7\ \text{psi}} = 2.38\ \text{atm} \)

(b) Absolute pressure = gauge pressure + outside air pressure = 2.381 + 1.00 = 3.38 atm (rounded to the hundredths place, set by 1.00).

(c) \( 3.381\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{101.325\ \text{kPa}}{1\ \text{atm}} = 343\ \text{kPa} \)

(a) 2.38 atm; (b) 3.38 atm; (c) 343 kPa. A "flat" tire still holds air at 1 atm; the gauge reads 0 because the pressure inside equals the pressure outside.

Problem 10.9

Which is the highest pressure?

A. 795 mmHg
B. 106 kPa
C. 15.3 psi
D. 1.06 atm

Hint

Convert every choice to the same unit (atm is easiest), then compare. A big number in a small unit can still be a smaller pressure.

Answer

D. Convert each to atm and compare.

  • A: 795 ÷ 760 = 1.046 atm. It has the biggest number, but a millimeter of mercury is a small unit.
  • B: 106 ÷ 101.325 = 1.046 atm, the same as A.
  • C: 15.3 ÷ 14.7 = 1.041 atm, the lowest of the four.

So 1.06 atm is the highest. Never compare pressures in different units by their numbers alone.

Problem 10.10

An arterial blood gas (ABG) measures the partial pressures of oxygen and carbon dioxide in arterial blood. A patient's report shows PaO2 = 92 mmHg and PaCO2 = 41 mmHg. (a) Express each value in torr. (b) Some labs, especially outside the United States, report blood gases in kPa. Convert each value to kPa.

Hint

(a) How do torr and mmHg compare? (b) Go through the base unit: 760 mmHg = 1 atm = 101.325 kPa. Keep two significant figures, like the readings.

Answer

(a) 1 torr = 1 mmHg, so the numbers do not change: PaO2 = 92 torr and PaCO2 = 41 torr.

(b) \( 92\ \text{mmHg} \times \dfrac{101.325\ \text{kPa}}{760\ \text{mmHg}} = 12\ \text{kPa} \qquad 41\ \text{mmHg} \times \dfrac{101.325\ \text{kPa}}{760\ \text{mmHg}} = 5.5\ \text{kPa} \)

(b) PaO2 = 12 kPa and PaCO2 = 5.5 kPa, two significant figures each, like the readings. A handy check: 1 kPa is about 7.5 mmHg. (Context only; interpretation of blood gases belongs to the care team.)

Problem 10.11

In seawater, the pressure increases by about 1 atm for every 10.1 m (33 ft) of depth, on top of the 1.00 atm of air pressing on the surface. What is the total pressure on a scuba diver at a depth of 18.0 m?

A. 1.78 atm
B. 1.56 atm
C. 2.78 atm
D. 183 atm

Hint

Find the pressure from the water (depth × 1 atm per 10.1 m), then add the air pressing on the surface of the water.

Answer

C. The water adds 18.0 m × (1 atm / 10.1 m) = 1.782 atm, and the air above the water adds 1.00 atm:

\( P_\text{total} = 1.00\ \text{atm} + 18.0\ \text{m} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{10.1\ \text{m}} = 1.00 + 1.782 = 2.78\ \text{atm} \)

  • A counts only the water and forgets the atmosphere above it.
  • B flips the conversion factor (10.1/18.0 + 1). Going deeper than 10.1 m must add more than 1 atm.
  • D multiplies the depth by 10.1 m instead of dividing. The meters do not cancel, and 183 atm would crush a diver.

Problem 10.12

On the summit of Mount Whitney (14,505 ft), the highest point in California, the air pressure is about 438 mmHg (an estimate from the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, NOAA/NASA/U.S. Air Force, 1976). (a) Express this pressure in atm and in kPa. (b) What percent of sea-level pressure (760 mmHg) is it? (c) At the same temperature, about how many oxygen molecules does each breath hold on the summit, compared with a breath at sea level?

Hint

(a) Divide by 760 mmHg per atm, then use 101.325 kPa per atm. (b) Summit pressure ÷ sea-level pressure × 100%. (c) At the same T and V, the number of molecules is proportional to the pressure.

Answer

(a) \( 438\ \text{mmHg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{760\ \text{mmHg}} = 0.576\ \text{atm} \qquad 0.5763\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{101.325\ \text{kPa}}{1\ \text{atm}} = 58.4\ \text{kPa} \)

(b) \( \dfrac{438\ \text{mmHg}}{760\ \text{mmHg}} \times 100\% = 57.6\% \)

(a) 0.576 atm and 58.4 kPa; (b) 57.6%.

(c) The air is still about 21% oxygen, but at the same temperature and lung volume the number of molecules is proportional to the pressure. Each breath holds only about 58% as many O2 molecules as at sea level, which is why hikers acclimatize slowly and breathe harder near the top.

10.3 The Simple Gas Laws and the Combined Gas Law

Problem 10.13

A student traps air in a sealed syringe attached to a pressure sensor, pushes the plunger in to several positions at constant temperature, and records the data in the table below.

Pressure and volume of trapped air at constant temperature
TrialPressure (atm)Volume (mL)
11.0024.0
21.5016.0
32.0012.0
43.008.00

(a) Is the relationship between P and V direct or inverse? Which gas law is this? (b) Show that one combination of P and V stays constant. (c) Predict the volume at 4.00 atm.

Hint

(a) As P goes up, what does V do? (b) Try both P × V and V/P for each trial; which stays the same? (c) Use P1V1 = P2V2.

Answer

(a) As the pressure goes up, the volume goes down: doubling P from 1.00 to 2.00 atm halves V from 24.0 to 12.0 mL. This is an inverse relationship, Boyle's law.

(b) The product PV is constant: (1.00)(24.0) = (1.50)(16.0) = (2.00)(12.0) = (3.00)(8.00) = 24.0 atm·mL in every trial. (The ratio V/P is not constant: 24.0, 10.7, 6.00, 2.67.)

(c) \( V_2 = \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{(1.00\ \text{atm})(24.0\ \text{mL})}{4.00\ \text{atm}} = 6.00\ \text{mL} \)

(c) 6.00 mL, which continues the pattern: four times the pressure of trial 1, one-fourth the volume.

Problem 10.14

A scuba diver at a depth where the pressure is 2.80 atm fills her lungs to 4.20 L from her regulator. If she held her breath while rising to the surface (1.00 atm), what volume would that air try to occupy, at constant temperature?

A. 11.8 L
B. 1.50 L
C. 7.56 L
D. 4.20 L

Hint

Constant temperature and moles: Boyle's law, P1V1 = P2V2. Predict first: as the pressure drops on the way up, what happens to the volume?

Answer

A. Boyle's law:

\( V_2 = \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{(2.80\ \text{atm})(4.20\ \text{L})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 11.8\ \text{L} \)

Lungs cannot stretch to nearly three times their volume; the expanding air could rupture them. This is why divers are trained to breathe out continuously as they ascend.

  • B sets up Boyle's law as a ratio (or flips the pressures): 4.20 × 1.00/2.80. The pressure goes down, so the volume must go up.
  • C multiplies the volume by the difference in pressures, 4.20 × (2.80 − 1.00). Boyle's law uses the ratio of the pressures, not their difference.
  • D assumes the lungs are rigid. Air in a flexible container changes volume whenever the pressure on it changes.

Problem 10.15

A 2.75 L party balloon is filled inside a warm house at 24.0 °C and carried outside on a frosty Valley morning at −2.0 °C. Assuming the pressure stays constant, what is its new volume? Show why using °C gives an impossible answer.

Hint

Charles's law, V1/T1 = V2/T2, with both temperatures in kelvins (add 273.15). Then redo it with °C and look at the sign of the result.

Answer

T1 = 24.0 + 273.15 = 297.2 K; T2 = −2.0 + 273.15 = 271.2 K.

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 2.75\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{271.15\ \text{K}}{297.15\ \text{K}} = 2.51\ \text{L} \)

The balloon shrinks to 2.51 L, a modest change, because 26 °C is less than a tenth of the Kelvin temperature. Using °C, 2.75 × (−2.0/24.0) = −0.229 L, a negative volume, which is impossible. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, so it is the only scale that makes V proportional to T.

Problem 10.16

A rigid steel cylinder of helium for filling balloons is at 155 atm at 21.0 °C. It is left in the bed of a pickup truck, where it warms to 48.0 °C. What is the new pressure?

A. 155 atm
B. 354 atm
C. 142 atm
D. 169 atm

Hint

Rigid means constant volume: Gay-Lussac's law, P1/T1 = P2/T2. Convert both temperatures to kelvins; a 27 °C rise should raise the pressure only a little.

Answer

D. The volume and moles are constant, so use Gay-Lussac's law with Kelvin temperatures (294.15 K and 321.15 K):

\( P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 155\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{321.15\ \text{K}}{294.15\ \text{K}} = 169\ \text{atm} \)

Hotter gas in a rigid container means higher pressure, which is why gas cylinders are stored out of direct sun.

  • A assumes nothing changes because the cylinder is rigid. Its volume is fixed, but the faster-moving atoms hit the walls harder, so the pressure rises.
  • B uses Celsius temperatures (155 × 48.0/21.0). The pressure does not really more than double for a 27 °C rise.
  • C flips the temperature ratio, so the pressure drops on heating, the wrong direction for a direct relationship.

Problem 10.17

During testing, a partly inflated airbag holds 0.850 mol of N2 in 21.0 L. How many moles of N2 must it hold to reach its full volume of 60.0 L at the same temperature and pressure?

Hint

Constant T and P: Avogadro's law, V1/n1 = V2/n2. The volume grows, so the moles must grow by the same factor.

Answer

At constant T and P, Avogadro's law applies: V1/n1 = V2/n2.

\( n_2 = n_1 \times \dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = 0.850\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{60.0\ \text{L}}{21.0\ \text{L}} = 2.43\ \text{mol} \)

The bag needs 2.43 mol of N2. Check: the volume grows by a factor of about 2.9, so the moles must too (0.850 × 2.86 = 2.43).

Problem 10.18

Name the gas law that best explains each observation, and say whether the relationship is direct or inverse. (a) The bubbles a diver breathes out get bigger as they rise toward the surface. (b) The gauge on a rigid oxygen cylinder reads lower after the cylinder is moved from a warm hallway into a cold storage room. (c) A plastic bottle capped in a hot car crumples a little after an hour in an air-conditioned clinic. (d) A ventilator pushes in more air, and the patient's lungs expand.

Hint

For each one, ask which two variables change and which stay constant (rigid means constant V; a flexible container stays near constant P). Then decide direct or inverse.

Answer
  • (a) Boyle's law (P and V, inverse): the water pressure on each bubble drops as it rises, so its volume increases.
  • (b) Gay-Lussac's law (P and T, direct): the volume is fixed, so lowering the temperature lowers the pressure.
  • (c) Charles's law (V and T, direct): the air inside cools at roughly constant pressure, so its volume shrinks and the soft bottle dents.
  • (d) Avogadro's law (n and V, direct): more moles of gas at about the same temperature and pressure take up more volume.

Problem 10.19

A 455 mL sample of gas is at 1.12 atm and 18.0 °C. What is its volume at 685 mmHg and 40.0 °C?

A. 526 mL
B. 608 mL
C. 0.800 mL
D. 1.26 × 103 mL

Hint

Put both pressures in the same unit and both temperatures in kelvins. Predict: lower pressure and higher temperature both make the volume bigger.

Answer

B. Put both pressures in the same unit first: 1.12 atm × 760 mmHg/atm = 851.2 mmHg. T1 = 291.15 K; T2 = 313.15 K.

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 455\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{851.2\ \text{mmHg}}{685\ \text{mmHg}} \times \dfrac{313.15\ \text{K}}{291.15\ \text{K}} = 608\ \text{mL} \)

Predict first: the pressure drops (volume up) and the temperature rises (volume up), so the answer must be larger than 455 mL.

  • A flips the temperature ratio. Warming a gas at lower pressure cannot shrink it below the pressure-only answer (565 mL).
  • C divides 1.12 atm by 685 mmHg without converting. The two pressures must be in the same unit.
  • D uses Celsius temperatures (40.0/18.0), which more than doubles the temperature effect.

Problem 10.20

The Kelvin temperature of a gas sample is doubled while its pressure is tripled, with the amount of gas constant. What happens to its volume?

A. It is multiplied by 6.
B. It is multiplied by 2/3.
C. It is multiplied by 3/2.
D. It is multiplied by 1/6.

Hint

Use V2 = V1 × (P1/P2) × (T2/T1) with easy numbers: P1 = 1, P2 = 3, T1 = 1, T2 = 2.

Answer

B. From the combined gas law, V2 = V1 × (P1/P2) × (T2/T1) = V1 × (1/3) × (2/1) = (2/3)V1. Doubling T doubles V (direct), and tripling P cuts V to a third (inverse).

  • A treats both changes as direct. Pressure and volume are inversely related.
  • C has both relationships upside down: it treats temperature as inverse and pressure as direct.
  • D treats both changes as inverse. Temperature and volume are directly related.

Problem 10.21

An "empty" aerosol can still holds propellant gas at 1.20 atm at 22.0 °C. If the can is thrown into a fire and heated to 650. °C, what is the pressure inside (assuming the can does not burst first)? Why do the labels say "Do not incinerate"?

Hint

The can's volume is fixed: Gay-Lussac's law. Convert both temperatures to kelvins (650. °C is known to the ones place), then compare with what °C would predict.

Answer

T1 = 22.0 + 273.15 = 295.2 K; T2 = 650. + 273.15 = 923 K (ones place, like 650.).

\( P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 1.20\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{923.15\ \text{K}}{295.15\ \text{K}} = 3.75\ \text{atm} \)

The pressure triples to 3.75 atm. A thin aluminum or steel can that is not built for that pressure can burst violently, so even an "empty" can is dangerous in a fire. (Using °C, 1.20 × 650./22.0 = 35.5 atm, overstates the rise almost tenfold.)

Problem 10.22

A hiker squeezes the air out of a collapsible water bottle except for 0.250 L, then caps it in Fresno at 1.00 atm and 30.0 °C. She drives up to Mammoth Lakes (about 7,900 ft), where the pressure is 0.740 atm and the temperature is 10.0 °C. What volume does the trapped air expand to?

Hint

Pressure and temperature both change, so use the combined gas law, V2 = V1 × (P1/P2) × (T2/T1), with kelvins.

Answer

T1 = 303.2 K; T2 = 283.2 K. All three variables change, so use the combined gas law:

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 0.250\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1.00\ \text{atm}}{0.740\ \text{atm}} \times \dfrac{283.15\ \text{K}}{303.15\ \text{K}} = 0.316\ \text{L} \)

The air expands to 0.316 L. The lower pressure alone would give 0.338 L; the cooler mountain air shrinks it a little.

10.4 The Ideal Gas Law

Problem 10.23

How many moles of O2 are in a 2.50 L flask at 1.85 atm and 24.0 °C?

A. 0.112 mol
B. 2.35 mol
C. 0.190 mol
D. 5.27 mol

Hint

One set of conditions: n = PV/RT. The gas is not at STP, so 22.4 L/mol does not apply, and T must be in kelvins.

Answer

C. T = 24.0 + 273.15 = 297.2 K.

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(1.85\ \text{atm})(2.50\ \text{L})}{(0.082058\ \tfrac{\text{L}\cdot\text{atm}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}})(297.15\ \text{K})} = 0.190\ \text{mol} \)

  • A divides the volume by 22.4 L/mol. That molar volume applies only at STP, and this gas is at 1.85 atm and 24.0 °C.
  • B uses 24.0 °C for T. R is in kelvins, so T must be too.
  • D flips the equation (RT/PV). Check the units: the answer must come out in mol, not mol−1.

Problem 10.24

A small cartridge for inflating a bike tire holds 16.0 g of CO2 (44.009 g/mol). What volume would this gas occupy at 1.00 atm and 20.0 °C?

Hint

Our goal is the mole: change grams of CO2 to moles first. Then V = nRT/P with T in kelvins.

Answer

Grams to moles first: n = 16.0 g ÷ 44.009 g/mol = 0.3636 mol. T = 293.15 K.

\( V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{(0.3636\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(293.15\ \text{K})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 8.75\ \text{L} \)

The gas would fill 8.75 L at 1.00 atm. A road-bike tire holds only about 0.7 L, but at several atmospheres, so one cartridge fills one tire.

Problem 10.25

A 1.75 L balloon holds 0.0850 mol of helium at 27.0 °C. What is the pressure inside, in mmHg?

A. 909 mmHg
B. 1.20 mmHg
C. 81.8 mmHg
D. 1.57 × 10−3 mmHg

Hint

Find P = nRT/V with T in kelvins; with R = 0.082058 the pressure comes out in atm. Then convert atm to mmHg; there are more mmHg than atm.

Answer

A. With R = 0.082058, the pressure comes out in atm; convert to mmHg at the end. T = 300.15 K.

\( P = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{(0.0850)(0.082058)(300.15)}{1.75} = 1.196\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{760\ \text{mmHg}}{1\ \text{atm}} = 909\ \text{mmHg} \)

\( R_2 = \dfrac{(0.9713\ \text{atm})(0.0435\ \text{L})}{(0.001719\ \text{mol})(298.15\ \text{K})} = 0.0824\ \dfrac{\text{L}\cdot\text{atm}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \)

(b) Trial 1: (0.0817 − 0.082058)/0.082058 × 100% = −0.4%. Trial 2: (0.0824 − 0.082058)/0.082058 × 100% = +0.4%. Average R = (0.08168 + 0.08242)/2 = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K), very close to the accepted value.

(c) Too high. Using the full 762 mmHg (1.003 atm) for the hydrogen in trial 1 gives R = (1.003)(0.0364)/[(0.001489)(293.15)] = 0.0836, about 2% high. The barometric pressure includes the water vapor, so it is larger than the hydrogen's own pressure.

Problem 10.48

A 10.0 L tank at 25.0 °C holds a mixture of oxygen and helium at a total pressure of 5.00 atm. The partial pressure of O2 is 1.20 atm. How many moles of O2 are in the tank?

A. 2.04 mol
B. 1.55 mol
C. 5.85 mol
D. 0.490 mol

Hint

To find the moles of one gas, use that gas's own partial pressure in PV = nRT, with T in kelvins.

Answer

D. To find the moles of one gas, use that gas's partial pressure in PV = nRT:

\( n_{\text{O}_2} = \dfrac{P_{\text{O}_2} V}{RT} = \dfrac{(1.20\ \text{atm})(10.0\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})} = 0.490\ \text{mol} \)

  • A uses the total pressure, which gives the total moles of gas (O2 plus He), not the moles of O2.
  • B uses the partial pressure of helium (5.00 − 1.20 = 3.80 atm), so it is the moles of He.
  • C uses 25.0 °C instead of 298.15 K.

Problem 10.49

Mateo Herrera, 34, is an irrigation technician on a Fresno County almond orchard. He has asthma and was admitted overnight on oxygen at 2.0 L/min by nasal cannula. The barometric pressure at the hospital is 757 mmHg. (a) Room air is 21.0% O2. What is the partial pressure of O2 in dry room air? (b) His nose and airways warm the air to 37 °C and saturate it with water vapor (47.1 mmHg at 37 °C). What is the partial pressure of O2 in this humidified air, still with 21.0% of the dry gas as O2? (c) With the cannula, the oxygen fraction of the dry gas he inhales rises to about 0.280. Repeat (b). (d) The flowmeter measures the oxygen at room temperature, 21.0 °C. At constant pressure, what volume per minute does it fill once it warms to 37.0 °C in his airways?

Hint

(a) Mole fraction × pressure. (b) Subtract the water vapor first, then take the O2 fraction of what is left. (c) Repeat with the new fraction. (d) Charles's law, in kelvins.

Answer

(a) PO2 = 0.210 × 757 mmHg = 159 mmHg.

(b) The water vapor takes 47.1 mmHg of the total, leaving 757 − 47.1 = 709.9 mmHg for the dry gases, of which O2 is 21.0%:

\( P_{\text{O}_2} = 0.210 \times (757 - 47.1)\ \text{mmHg} = 0.210 \times 709.9\ \text{mmHg} = 149\ \text{mmHg} \)

(c) 0.280 × 709.9 mmHg = 199 mmHg.

(d) \( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 2.0\ \tfrac{\text{L}}{\text{min}} \times \dfrac{310.15\ \text{K}}{294.15\ \text{K}} = 2.1\ \tfrac{\text{L}}{\text{min}} \)

(a) 159 mmHg; (b) 149 mmHg; (c) 199 mmHg; (d) 2.1 L/min, two significant figures like 2.0 L/min. Raising the inspired PO2 by about 50 mmHg pushes more oxygen across the alveolar walls by diffusion. (The case is for practicing chemistry only; oxygen is a medication and is given as ordered, and this is not treatment advice.)

Problem 10.50

Recreational divers sometimes breathe "nitrox," air enriched with oxygen. One common blend is 32.0% O2 and 68.0% N2 by moles. (a) What is the partial pressure of O2 at a depth where the total pressure is 4.00 atm? (b) To limit the risk of oxygen toxicity, divers keep PO2 at or below 1.40 atm. What is the highest total pressure at which this blend may be breathed? (c) Using 1.00 atm at the surface plus 1 atm for every 10.1 m of seawater, about how deep is that?

Hint

(a) Partial pressure of O2 = mole fraction × Ptotal. (b) Solve the same equation for Ptotal. (c) Subtract the 1.00 atm of air first, then convert atm to meters with 10.1 m per atm.

Answer

(a) PO2 = XO2 × Ptotal = 0.320 × 4.00 atm = 1.28 atm.

(b) \( P_\text{total} = \dfrac{P_{\text{O}_2}}{X_{\text{O}_2}} = \dfrac{1.40\ \text{atm}}{0.320} = 4.38\ \text{atm} \)

(c) \( \text{depth} = (4.375 - 1.00)\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{10.1\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{atm}} = 34.1\ \text{m} \)

(a) 1.28 atm; (b) 4.38 atm; (c) about 34.1 m (about 112 ft). Remember to subtract the 1.00 atm of air above the water before converting to depth. (Context only; dive limits come from training agencies and dive computers.)

Problem 10.51

Helium is pumped into a rigid tank that already holds oxygen at 0.800 atm, while the temperature stays constant. What happens to the partial pressure of the O2?

A. It increases, because the total pressure increases.
B. It decreases, because the helium pushes the oxygen molecules aside.
C. It becomes equal to the partial pressure of the helium.
D. It stays at 0.800 atm.

Hint

Each gas in a mixture acts as if it were alone. Did the moles of O2, the volume, or the temperature change?

Answer

D. In a mixture, each gas behaves as if it were alone in the container. The moles of O2, the volume, and the temperature have not changed, so the O2 molecules hit the walls just as often as before. Only the total pressure rises, by the helium's partial pressure. (The mole fraction of O2 does drop, but XO2 × Ptotal is still 0.800 atm.)

  • A confuses the total pressure with the oxygen's share of it.
  • B treats the gas particles as crowding each other. In an ideal gas, the particles take up almost none of the space and do not interact.
  • C has no basis: partial pressures depend on the moles of each gas, and nothing forces them to be equal.

Problem 10.52

Mateo Herrera, the irrigation technician with asthma whose inspired oxygen you worked out in Section 10.6 above, walks the hospital hallway with a small portable oxygen cylinder. Its internal volume is 1.05 L, and it is filled to 1.85 × 103 psi at 21.0 °C. (a) Convert the fill pressure to atm. (b) What volume would this oxygen occupy at 1.00 atm and the same temperature? (c) At 2.0 L/min, about how many minutes could it last? (d) How many moles and how many grams of O2 (31.998 g/mol) does the cylinder hold? (Treat the fill pressure as the pressure of the gas.)

Hint

(a) Divide by 14.7 psi per atm. (b) Same temperature before and after: Boyle's law. (c) Volume ÷ flow rate. (d) n = PV/RT, then moles × molar mass.

Answer

(a) \( 1.85 \times 10^{3}\ \text{psi} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atm}}{14.7\ \text{psi}} = 126\ \text{atm} \)

(b) \( V_2 = \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{(125.85\ \text{atm})(1.05\ \text{L})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 132\ \text{L} \)

(c) 132.1 L ÷ 2.0 L/min = 66 min (two significant figures, set by 2.0 L/min).

(d) \( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(125.85\ \text{atm})(1.05\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(294.15\ \text{K})} = 5.47\ \text{mol} \qquad 5.475\ \text{mol} \times 31.998\ \tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{mol}} = 175\ \text{g} \)

(a) 126 atm; (b) 132 L; (c) about 66 min; (d) 5.47 mol, or 175 g. In practice the cylinder is changed well before it is empty, so the usable time is shorter. (Context only; not a clinical calculation.)

Problem 10.53

Vaccines and lab samples are shipped packed in dry ice, solid CO2 (44.009 g/mol), which sublimes into gas. Shipping rules require the boxes to vent, and here is why. (a) If 45.0 g of dry ice sublimed completely inside a sealed 2.00 L plastic bottle at 22.0 °C, what would the pressure be, in atm and in psi? (b) What volume would the same CO2 occupy at 1.00 atm and 22.0 °C?

Hint

Our goal is the mole: grams of CO2 to moles. Then P = nRT/V (convert atm to psi with 14.7 psi per atm) and V = nRT/P.

Answer

n = 45.0 g ÷ 44.009 g/mol = 1.023 mol CO2; T = 295.15 K.

(a) \( P = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{(1.023\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(295.15\ \text{K})}{2.00\ \text{L}} = 12.4\ \text{atm} \qquad 12.38\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{14.7\ \text{psi}}{1\ \text{atm}} = 182\ \text{psi} \)

(b) \( V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{(1.023\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(295.15\ \text{K})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 24.8\ \text{L} \)

(a) 12.4 atm, or 182 psi, far above what a soda bottle can hold; it would burst violently. (b) 24.8 L, more than twelve times the bottle's volume. Sublimation is a physical change, so the moles of CO2 stay the same; only the state changes.

Problem 10.54

A 2.50 L anaerobic jar is sealed with air inside at 1.00 atm and 25.0 °C. The air is 21.0% O2 by moles. The hydrogen released into the jar removes the oxygen by the reaction

2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l)

(a) What is the partial pressure of O2 in the jar before the reaction? (b) How many moles of O2 does the jar hold? (c) How many moles of H2 are needed to remove it all? (d) What volume would that H2 occupy at 1.00 atm and 25.0 °C? Check your answer with a volume ratio.

Hint

(a) X × Ptotal. (b) n = PV/RT using the O2 partial pressure. (c) Mole ratio from the equation. (d) V = nRT/P; check with a volume ratio.

Answer

(a) PO2 = 0.210 × 1.00 atm = 0.210 atm.

(b) \( n_{\text{O}_2} = \dfrac{P_{\text{O}_2} V}{RT} = \dfrac{(0.210\ \text{atm})(2.50\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})} = 0.0215\ \text{mol} \)

(c) 0.02146 mol O2 × (2 mol H2 / 1 mol O2) = 0.0429 mol H2.

(d) \( V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{(0.04292\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 1.05\ \text{L} \)

(a) 0.210 atm; (b) 0.0215 mol; (c) 0.0429 mol; (d) 1.05 L. Check: at 1.00 atm the O2 alone would fill 21.0% of 2.50 L, or 0.525 L, and the 2 : 1 ratio gives 2 × 0.525 = 1.05 L of H2 at the same T and P. Using the total pressure in (b) would overstate the oxygen nearly fivefold.

Problem 10.55

A student reacts a piece of zinc with excess hydrochloric acid and collects the hydrogen over water:

Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)

She collects 28.4 mL of gas at 20.0 °C when the barometric pressure is 749 mmHg (water vapor pressure at 20 °C = 17.5 mmHg). (a) What is the pressure of the dry H2, in atm? (b) How many moles of H2 were collected? (c) What mass of zinc (65.39 g/mol) reacted? (d) What mass would she calculate if she forgot to subtract the water vapor, and by what percent would it be off?

Hint

(a) Subtract the water vapor, then convert to atm. (b) n = PV/RT; change mL to L. (c) 1 : 1 ratio, then molar mass. (d) Repeat with the full barometric pressure.

Answer

(a) PH2 = 749 − 17.5 = 731.5 mmHg × (1 atm / 760 mmHg) = 0.9625 atm (to the ones place in mmHg, 732 mmHg; carry the unrounded value).

(b) \( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(0.9625\ \text{atm})(0.0284\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(293.15\ \text{K})} = 1.14 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol H}_2 \)

(c) \( 1.136 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol H}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Zn}}{1\ \text{mol H}_2} \times \dfrac{65.39\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol Zn}} = 0.0743\ \text{g Zn} \)

(d) Using 749 mmHg (0.9855 atm) gives n = 1.163 × 10−3 mol and 0.0761 g of zinc, about 2.4% too high. The answers: (a) 0.9625 atm; (b) 1.14 × 10−3 mol; (c) 0.0743 g. Remember to convert 28.4 mL to 0.0284 L for R.

Problem 10.56

Valley packing houses ripen fruit such as tomatoes and bananas with a gas. A 0.268 g sample of the gas occupies 0.250 L at 0.950 atm and 30.0 °C. (a) Find its molar mass. (b) Is it methane (CH4, 16.043 g/mol), ethylene (C2H4, 28.054 g/mol), ethane (C2H6, 30.070 g/mol), or propane (C3H8, 44.097 g/mol)? (c) The gas burns by the reaction below. What volumes of O2 are used and of CO2 made when the 0.250 L sample burns, with all gases at the same temperature and pressure?

C2H4(g) + 3 O2(g) → 2 CO2(g) + 2 H2O(g)

Hint

(a) n = PV/RT, then M = grams ÷ moles. (b) Match the molar mass to the list. (c) Same T and P, so the coefficients are volume ratios.

Answer

(a) \( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(0.950\ \text{atm})(0.250\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(303.15\ \text{K})} = 0.009547\ \text{mol} \qquad M = \dfrac{0.268\ \text{g}}{0.009547\ \text{mol}} = 28.1\ \text{g/mol} \)

(a) 28.1 g/mol. (b) Ethylene, C2H4 (28.054 g/mol); ethane (30.070 g/mol) is about 7% too heavy. Fruits make ethylene themselves as a ripening hormone, so a little extra speeds up and evens out ripening.

(c) Same T and P, so the coefficients are volume ratios: 0.250 L C2H4 × 3 = 0.750 L O2 used, and 0.250 L × 2 = 0.500 L CO2 made.

Problem 10.57

An open jar holds 0.120 mol of air at 15.0 °C. The jar is warmed to 60.0 °C; its volume does not change, and because it is open, the pressure stays equal to the room's. How many moles of air remain in the jar?

A. 0.104 mol
B. 0.139 mol
C. 0.0300 mol
D. 0.120 mol

Hint

P and V are constant: cross them out of the combined gas law to get n1T1 = n2T2. Use kelvins; warming pushes air out of an open jar.

Answer

A. P and V are constant, so cross them out of P1V1/(n1T1) = P2V2/(n2T2), leaving n1T1 = n2T2. With T1 = 288.15 K and T2 = 333.15 K:

\( n_2 = \dfrac{n_1 T_1}{T_2} = \dfrac{(0.120\ \text{mol})(288.15\ \text{K})}{333.15\ \text{K}} = 0.104\ \text{mol} \)

Warming pushes some air out of the open jar, so fewer moles remain.

  • B treats n and T as directly related (0.120 × 333.15/288.15). More moles in a warmer, open jar of fixed size is backward.
  • C uses Celsius temperatures (0.120 × 15.0/60.0). Gas laws need kelvins.
  • D assumes nothing changes; that would be true only for a sealed jar, whose pressure would rise instead.

Problem 10.58

A balloon holds 2.25 L of air at 21.0 °C. At constant pressure, to what temperature must it be heated for its volume to reach 2.60 L? Give the answer in kelvins and in °C.

Hint

Charles's law with the unknown on the bottom: flip both sides, T1/V1 = T2/V2. Convert 21.0 °C to kelvins first, and back to °C at the end.

Answer

Charles's law, V1/T1 = V2/T2, has the unknown in a denominator, so flip both sides: T1/V1 = T2/V2. T1 = 294.15 K.

\( T_2 = \dfrac{T_1 V_2}{V_1} = \dfrac{(294.15\ \text{K})(2.60\ \text{L})}{2.25\ \text{L}} = 340.\ \text{K} \qquad 340. - 273.15 = 67\ {}^\circ\text{C} \)

The answer is 340. K (three significant figures, shown by the decimal point), which is 67 °C (the kelvin value is known to the ones place, so the Celsius value is too). The volume grew, so the temperature had to rise: a direct relationship.

Problem 10.59

"A 2.50 L flask holds 0.110 mol of argon at 28.0 °C. What is the pressure in the flask?" Which equation should you use?

A. PV = nRT
B. P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2
C. P1V1 = P2V2
D. Ptotal = P1 + P2

Hint

Look for subscripts. Is the gas changing, with before and after values, or is there just one set of conditions? Is there a mixture of gases?

Answer

A. There is one volume, one amount, and one temperature, and nothing changes: no subscripts, so this is the current state of the gas. P = nRT/V = (0.110)(0.082058)(301.15) ÷ 2.50 = 1.09 atm.

  • B and C compare a gas before and after a change; they need two values (subscripts 1 and 2) of at least one variable.
  • D is Dalton's law for mixtures; the flask holds only argon.

Problem 10.60

A 0.562 g sample of a diatomic element occupies 0.520 L at 0.950 atm and 27.0 °C. Identify the element.

Hint

n = PV/RT (kelvins; parentheses around R × T), then M = grams ÷ moles. The element is diatomic, so halve the molar mass before checking the periodic table.

Answer

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(0.950)(0.520)}{(0.082058)(300.15)} = 0.02006\ \text{mol} \qquad M = \dfrac{0.562\ \text{g}}{0.02006\ \text{mol}} = 28.0\ \text{g/mol} \)

Half of 28.0 is 14.0, the atomic mass of nitrogen. The gas is N2 (28.014 g/mol). On the calculator, put (0.082058 × 300.15) in parentheses.

Problem 10.61

A gas used in welding torches is 92.26% carbon and 7.74% hydrogen by mass. A 0.325 g sample occupies 0.280 L at STP. Find its empirical formula, molar mass, and molecular formula. (C 12.011, H 1.008 g/mol)

Hint

Molar mass: 22.4 L/mol at STP gives moles; grams ÷ moles. Empirical formula: assume 100 g, change to moles, divide by the smaller. Then molar mass ÷ empirical-formula mass.

Answer

Molar mass: 0.280 L ÷ 22.4 L/mol = 0.0125 mol; 0.325 g ÷ 0.0125 mol = 26.0 g/mol.

Empirical formula (100 g basis): 92.26 ÷ 12.011 = 7.681 mol C; 7.74 ÷ 1.008 = 7.679 mol H. The ratio is 1 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH (13.019 g/mol).

Multiplier: 26.0 ÷ 13.019 = 2.00. The molecular formula is C2H2 (acetylene, 26.038 g/mol). The percent data alone could never tell CH from C2H2 or C6H6; the gas data supply the molar mass that decides.

Problem 10.62

Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). A student collects 82.5 mL of H2 over water at 30.0 °C and a total pressure of 755 mmHg. What mass of magnesium (24.31 g/mol) reacted?

Hint

Subtract water's vapor pressure at 30.0 °C (Table 1 in Section 10.6). Then n = PV/RT for the H2, the mole ratio to Mg, and the molar mass.

Answer

PH2 = 755 − 31.8 = 723 mmHg = 0.9513 atm. V = 0.0825 L; T = 303.15 K.

\( n_{\text{H}_2} = \dfrac{(0.9513)(0.0825)}{(0.082058)(303.15)} = 3.155 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \qquad 3.155 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol H}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Mg}}{1\ \text{mol H}_2} \times 24.31\ \dfrac{\text{g}}{\text{mol}} = 0.0767\ \text{g Mg} \)

The answer is 0.0767 g Mg (76.7 mg). The water vapor was subtracted first, so the pressure belongs to the hydrogen alone, and the moles from PV = nRT are moles of H2, converted to Mg by the mole ratio.

Problem 10.63

A sealed flask at a total pressure of 2.00 atm contains 5 ▲ (He), 3 ● (Ne), and 2 ■ (Ar). What is the partial pressure of neon?

A. 0.600 atm
B. 0.667 atm
C. 0.300 atm
D. 1.00 atm

Hint

Count all the particles. Neon's share of the particles is its mole fraction; multiply it by the total pressure.

Answer

A. There are 10 particles, 3 of them neon: XNe = 3/10 = 0.300, and PNe = 0.300 × 2.00 atm = 0.600 atm.

  • B splits the pressure equally among the three gases (2.00 ÷ 3). Each gas's share depends on how many particles it has, not on how many kinds of gas there are.
  • C is the mole fraction, 0.300, which has no units; multiply it by the total pressure.
  • D is the partial pressure of helium (5/10 × 2.00 atm), the gas with the most particles.

Problem 10.64

A rigid cylinder holds 4.00 g of helium (4.003 g/mol) at 0.800 atm. Nitrogen (28.014 g/mol) is added at the same temperature until the pressure is 2.00 atm. What mass of N2 was added?

Hint

Grams of He to moles. At fixed V and T, pressure is proportional to total moles: scale to the new total, subtract the helium, then convert moles of N2 to grams.

Answer

nHe = 4.00 ÷ 4.003 = 0.9993 mol. At fixed V and T, pressure is proportional to total moles, whatever the gases are:

\( n_\text{total} = 0.9993 \times \dfrac{2.00\ \text{atm}}{0.800\ \text{atm}} = 2.498\ \text{mol} \qquad n_{\text{N}_2} = 2.498 - 0.9993 = 1.499\ \text{mol} \qquad 1.499 \times 28.014 = 42.0\ \text{g} \)

The answer is 42.0 g N2. Although each N2 molecule is 7 times as heavy as a He atom, it pushes no harder; the 1.20 atm rise comes from 1.50 times as many moles as the helium's 0.800 atm.
