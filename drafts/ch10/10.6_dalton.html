Learning Objectives

  • State Dalton's law of partial pressures and find total and partial pressures.
  • Use mole fractions to find the partial pressure of each gas in a mixture.
  • Find mole fractions and partial pressures from a particle picture or from grams.
  • Use the idea that total pressure depends only on total moles, not on which gases are present.
  • Correct for water vapor when a gas is collected over water, treating vapor pressure as a partial pressure.

The air you breathe is a mixture: mostly nitrogen, about a fifth oxygen, and small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide, and water vapor. For your body, what matters is not the total pressure of that mixture but the share that belongs to oxygen. That share is its partial pressure.

Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

According to kinetic molecular theory, gas particles do not attract or repel each other, so in a mixture each gas behaves as if the others were not there. The pressure each gas would exert alone in the same container at the same temperature is its partial pressure. Dalton's law says the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures:

\( P_\text{total} = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \cdots \)

Mole Fraction and Partial Pressure

Pressure depends only on the number of particles, so each gas's share of the pressure equals its share of the moles. The mole fraction X of a gas is its moles divided by the total moles:

\( X_i = \dfrac{n_i}{n_\text{total}} \qquad P_i = X_i \times P_\text{total} \)

Mole fractions have no units and add up to 1. A percentage by moles (or by volume, for gases) divided by 100 is a mole fraction.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Oxygen in air

Problem. Dry air is 20.9% O2 by moles. What is the partial pressure of O2 in dry air at 760. mmHg?

Solution. XO2 = 0.209.

\( P_{\text{O}_2} = (0.209)(760.\ \text{mmHg}) = 159\ \text{mmHg} \)

Check. Oxygen is about a fifth of the air, so its partial pressure is about a fifth of 760 mmHg.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Heliox

Problem. A heliox gas mixture contains 0.720 mol of He and 0.280 mol of O2. It is delivered at a total pressure of 1.00 atm. Find the mole fraction and partial pressure of each gas.

Solution. ntotal = 1.000 mol. XHe = 0.720 and XO2 = 0.280.

\( P_\text{He} = (0.720)(1.00\ \text{atm}) = 0.720\ \text{atm} \qquad P_{\text{O}_2} = (0.280)(1.00\ \text{atm}) = 0.280\ \text{atm} \)

Check. The partial pressures add up to the total, 1.00 atm. Because helium is much less dense than nitrogen, heliox flows more easily through narrowed airways, which is why it is sometimes used in severe airway obstruction. (Context only.)

Reading a particle picture

Because every particle pushes the same, a drawing of a gas mixture is all you need: count the particles. If one gas makes up 2 of every 5 particles, it supplies 2/5 of the total pressure.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Counting particles in a vial

Problem. The sealed vial below holds four gases at a total pressure of 1.50 atm. Each symbol is one molecule or atom: ● = N2, ■ = O2, ▲ = CO2, ◆ = Ar.

(a) Which gas has the highest partial pressure? (b) What is the mole fraction of O2? (c) What is the partial pressure of CO2?

Solution. Count: 6 N2, 4 O2, 3 CO2, 2 Ar, 15 particles in all. (a) N2, the gas with the most particles (size and mass do not matter). (b) XO2 = 4/15 = 0.267. (c) PCO2 = (3/15)(1.50 atm) = 0.300 atm.

Check. The four partial pressures, (6, 4, 3, 2)/15 × 1.50 atm = 0.600, 0.400, 0.300, and 0.200 atm, add up to 1.50 atm.

Pressure depends only on the total moles

Kinetic molecular theory says gas particles are tiny, shapeless, and do not interact, so two different gases mixed together cannot be told apart by the pressure they exert. At a fixed volume and temperature, the total pressure depends only on the total number of moles, not on which gases they are.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Adding argon to a rigid tank

Problem. A rigid tank holds 8.00 g of CH4 (16.043 g/mol) at 0.450 atm. Argon is added at the same temperature until the pressure reaches 1.20 atm. What mass of argon (39.95 g/mol) was added?

Solution. nCH4 = 8.00 ÷ 16.043 = 0.4987 mol. V and T are fixed, so pressure is proportional to total moles (P1/n1 = P2/n2):

\( n_\text{total} = 0.4987\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{1.20\ \text{atm}}{0.450\ \text{atm}} = 1.330\ \text{mol} \qquad n_\text{Ar} = 1.330 - 0.4987 = 0.8311\ \text{mol} \)

\( 0.8311\ \text{mol Ar} \times \dfrac{39.95\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 33.2\ \text{g Ar} \)

Check. The argon raised the pressure by 0.75 atm, more than the methane's 0.450 atm, so there must be more moles of argon than of methane, and there are (0.831 vs 0.499).

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): A balloon with two gases

Problem. A balloon at 1.00 atm holds 1.50 mol of He. Then 3.50 mol of N2 is added, and the balloon expands. What is the partial pressure of each gas afterward?

Solution. A balloon expands until the pressure inside matches the pressure outside, so the total pressure stays 1.00 atm (the volume grows with the moles: Avogadro's law). ntotal = 5.00 mol: XHe = 1.50/5.00 = 0.300 and XN2 = 0.700.

\( P_\text{He} = (0.300)(1.00\ \text{atm}) = 0.300\ \text{atm} \qquad P_{\text{N}_2} = (0.700)(1.00\ \text{atm}) = 0.700\ \text{atm} \)

Check. The helium's partial pressure dropped from 1.00 atm to 0.300 atm even though no helium left: the same helium now shares a bigger balloon with the nitrogen.

Starting from grams

If a mixture is described by mass, convert each gas to moles first. Mass percent is not mole percent, because different gases have different molar masses.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Equal masses, unequal pressures

Problem. A made-up mixture of 20.0 g of neon (20.180 g/mol) and 20.0 g of argon (39.95 g/mol) fills a 10.0 L tank at 25.0 °C. Find the total pressure and the partial pressure of each gas.

Solution. nNe = 20.0 ÷ 20.180 = 0.9911 mol; nAr = 20.0 ÷ 39.95 = 0.5006 mol; ntotal = 1.4917 mol. T = 298.15 K.

\( P_\text{total} = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{(1.4917)(0.082058)(298.15)}{10.0} = 3.65\ \text{atm} \)

\( P_\text{Ne} = \dfrac{0.9911}{1.4917}(3.650\ \text{atm}) = 2.42\ \text{atm} \qquad P_\text{Ar} = \dfrac{0.5006}{1.4917}(3.650\ \text{atm}) = 1.22\ \text{atm} \)

Check. The masses are equal, but neon atoms are about half as heavy, so there are about twice as many of them, and neon supplies about twice the pressure. (The rounded partial pressures add to 3.64 atm; the unrounded ones add to 3.65 atm.)

Collecting a Gas over Water

Gases made in the lab are often collected by bubbling them into an upside-down tube filled with water, which they push out. The collected gas is never pure: water evaporates into it, so the tube holds the gas plus water vapor. When the water level inside the tube matches the level outside, the total pressure inside equals the room's air pressure, and

\( P_\text{atm} = P_\text{gas} + P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \qquad \text{so} \qquad P_\text{gas} = P_\text{atm} - P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \)

Vapor pressure (Section 9.8) is a partial pressure: it is the push of the water molecules that have evaporated into the gas. It depends only on the temperature, so it is looked up in a table such as Table \(\PageIndex{1}\).

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): Oxygen from a generator

Problem. A chemical oxygen generator produces O2, which is collected over water at 25.0 °C. The bottle holds 0.250 L of gas at a total pressure of 762 mmHg. How many moles and how many grams of O2 (31.998 g/mol) were collected?

Solution. From Table 1, water's vapor pressure at 25 °C is 23.8 mmHg. PO2 = 762 − 23.8 = 738 mmHg = 0.9711 atm.

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(0.9711\ \text{atm})(0.250\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})} = 9.92 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol O}_2 \qquad 9.92 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \times 31.998\ \text{g/mol} = 0.318\ \text{g} \)

Check. This is exactly how you will treat your data in the lab: subtract the water vapor, then use PV = nRT for the dry gas. A quarter liter of gas near room conditions is about 0.01 mol, as found.

Example \(\PageIndex{8}\): Finding R in the lab

Problem. A student reacts 0.0405 g of Mg (24.31 g/mol) with excess HCl and collects 42.1 mL of H2 over water at 25.0 °C. The room pressure is 755 mmHg, and water's vapor pressure at 25.0 °C is 23.8 mmHg. Find the student's value of R and the percent error compared with 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K).

Solution. PH2 = 755 − 23.8 = 731 mmHg = 0.9618 atm. Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g), so nH2 = nMg = 0.0405 ÷ 24.31 = 0.001666 mol. V = 0.0421 L; T = 298.15 K.

\( R = \dfrac{PV}{nT} = \dfrac{(0.9618\ \text{atm})(0.0421\ \text{L})}{(0.001666\ \text{mol})(298.15\ \text{K})} = 0.0815\ \dfrac{\text{L}\cdot\text{atm}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}} \)

\( \text{percent error} = \dfrac{0.0815 - 0.082058}{0.082058} \times 100\% = -0.7\% \)

Check. Forgetting to subtract the water vapor would make P too large and R too high. Here the result is within 1% of the accepted value. (The hydrogen collected has a mass of only 0.001666 mol × 2.016 g/mol = 0.003359 g, about 3.36 mg, far too little to weigh, which is why the experiment measures its volume instead.)

In the Lab: Experiment 8a, Determination of the Gas Constant R

This experiment is Example 8 with your own data. You clean and weigh a short piece of magnesium ribbon, let it react with hydrochloric acid at the top of an inverted, water-filled gas collection tube, and read the volume of hydrogen when bubbles stop. You record the water temperature and the room pressure, look up water's vapor pressure, and use Dalton's law to find the pressure of the dry hydrogen. Comparing your R with 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K) gives your percent error, and thinking about its sign tells you which error (an unmatched water level, a trapped bubble, oxide on the ribbon) is most likely.

Professor Tip

Partial pressure problems come in two kinds. If you are given amounts (moles or percents), find mole fractions and multiply by the total pressure. If you are given pressures, add them or subtract them. For a gas collected over water, always subtract water's vapor pressure before using PV = nRT for the collected gas.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

What reaches Mateo's alveoli is not dry room air. As air passes through the nose and airways, it is warmed to 37 °C and saturated with water vapor, which at that temperature has a partial pressure of about 47 mmHg. That vapor takes up part of the total pressure, so the oxygen in humidified inspired air has a partial pressure of about 0.21 × (760 − 47) ≈ 150 mmHg at sea level. Supplemental oxygen raises the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2): at an FiO2 of about 0.28, the inspired PO2 rises to roughly 200 mmHg. A higher PO2 in the alveoli pushes more oxygen into the blood by diffusion, which is how 2 L/min through a cannula helps his saturation rise. (Context only; oxygen is a medication and is given as ordered.)

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Nitrous oxide–oxygen sedation is a gas mixture delivered through a nasal hood. The flowmeters set the fraction of each gas, and the partial pressure of N2O is what produces the calming effect. Dental units are built so they cannot deliver less than 30% oxygen, and patients breathe 100% oxygen for a few minutes at the end of the appointment so the nitrous oxide leaves the lungs and does not dilute the oxygen in the alveoli. Scavenging systems pull exhaled N2O away to protect the dental team. (Context only; follow the dentist's orders and office protocols.)

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the water vapor when a gas is collected over water.
  • Using a percent as a mole fraction without dividing by 100.
  • Using the total pressure for one gas in PV = nRT; use that gas's partial pressure to find its moles.
  • Treating mass percent as mole percent: convert grams to moles first.
  • Thinking a heavier gas pushes harder: each particle contributes equally, so only the number of moles matters.

Summary

  • Ptotal = P1 + P2 + …; each gas acts as if alone.
  • Xi = ni/ntotal; Pi = Xi × Ptotal.
  • Gas collected over water: Pgas = Patm − PH2O; the vapor pressure of water is itself a partial pressure.
  • Total pressure depends only on total moles; in a picture, count particles to get mole fractions.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A tank holds N2 at 2.15 atm, O2 at 0.580 atm, and Ar at 0.0270 atm. What is the total pressure?

Answer

2.15 + 0.580 + 0.0270 = 2.76 atm (rounded to hundredths, set by 2.15).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A mixture contains 1.50 mol N2 and 0.500 mol CO2 at a total pressure of 3.00 atm. What is the partial pressure of CO2?

Answer

XCO2 = 0.500 ÷ 2.00 = 0.250; P = 0.250 × 3.00 = 0.750 atm.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Oxygen is collected over water at 20.0 °C when the room pressure is 748 mmHg. What is the partial pressure of the oxygen? (Water vapor pressure at 20 °C = 17.5 mmHg)

Answer

748 − 17.5 = 730.5, reported to the ones place (set by 748) as 731 mmHg.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A drawing of a gas sample shows 9 particles of gas A and 3 particles of gas B at a total pressure of 800. mmHg. What is the partial pressure of B?

Answer

XB = 3/12 = 0.250; PB = 0.250 × 800. = 200. mmHg.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

A rigid container holds 0.200 mol of N2 at 0.600 atm. If 0.100 mol of CO2 is added at the same temperature, what is the new total pressure?

Answer

Pressure is proportional to total moles: 0.600 atm × (0.300 mol / 0.200 mol) = 0.900 atm. The identity of the added gas does not matter.
