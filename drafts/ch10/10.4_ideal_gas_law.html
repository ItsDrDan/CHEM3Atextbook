Learning Objectives

  • Use the ideal gas law, PV = nRT, to find pressure, volume, moles, or temperature.
  • Choose the value of R that matches the units, and use the molar volume at STP.
  • Find the molar mass and density of a gas from its mass, volume, pressure, and temperature.
  • Decide whether a problem needs the combined gas law or the ideal gas law from the subscripts.
  • Identify a gas from its molar mass, and combine molar volume with an empirical formula to find a molecular formula.

The simple gas laws compare a gas before and after a change. Often, though, there is no change: you know three of the four variables for one sample and need the fourth. How many moles of air fill your lungs? What pressure does a known mass of gas exert in a flask? For these, combine all four relationships into one equation.

The Ideal Gas Law

Boyle's, Charles's, and Avogadro's laws together say that V is proportional to nT/P. Writing the proportionality constant as R gives the ideal gas law:

\( PV = nRT \)

R is the ideal gas constant. Its value depends on the units, so the units of P, V, and T must match R exactly:

  • R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K): P in atm, V in liters, T in kelvins. This is the one you will use most.
  • R = 62.36 L·torr/(mol·K): P in torr (mmHg).
  • R = 8.3144 J/(mol·K): for energy calculations.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Air in the lungs

Problem. A person's lungs hold 5.85 L of air at 37.0 °C and 1.00 atm. How many moles of gas is that?

Solution. T = 37.0 + 273.15 = 310.15 K. Rearrange PV = nRT for n:

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(1.00\ \text{atm})(5.85\ \text{L})}{(0.082058\ \tfrac{\text{L}\cdot\text{atm}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}})(310.15\ \text{K})} = 0.230\ \text{mol} \)

Check. At body temperature a mole of gas fills about 25 L, so 5.85 L is roughly a quarter of a mole. All units cancel except mol.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Pressure from a mass of gas

Problem. 12.5 g of CO2 (44.009 g/mol) is placed in an empty 5.00 L flask at 25.0 °C. What is the pressure in the flask?

Solution. n = 12.5 ÷ 44.009 = 0.2840 mol; T = 298.15 K.

\( P = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{(0.2840\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})}{5.00\ \text{L}} = 1.39\ \text{atm} \)

Check. Grams were converted to moles first: our goal is the mole. About a quarter of a mole in 5 L gives a pressure a bit above 1 atm.

Professor Tip: Parentheses Around the Bottom

When you solve PV = nRT for n, P, or V, the denominator holds two numbers multiplied together. On the TI-36X Pro, wrap the whole bottom in parentheses, or use the fraction template so the top and bottom are separate. For Example 1, type 1.00 × 5.85 ÷ (0.082058 × 310.15). Without the parentheses, the calculator divides by R and then multiplies by T, and the answer is off by a factor of about 96,000.

STP and Molar Volume

Standard temperature and pressure (STP) is 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm. Solving PV = nRT for the volume of 1.00 mol at STP:

\( V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{(1.00\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(273\ \text{K})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 22.4\ \text{L} \)

This molar volume, 22.4 L/mol, is the same for every ideal gas at STP, because volume depends on the number of particles, not on what they are (Avogadro's law). Use it as a conversion factor only at STP; at any other conditions, use PV = nRT.

Molar Mass and Density of a Gas

If you know the mass of a gas sample and can find its moles from PV = nRT, then its molar mass is grams divided by moles, M = m/n. Its density is simply mass divided by volume, d = m/V, usually in g/L for gases. Gas densities depend strongly on temperature and pressure, so a density is always reported with its conditions.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Identifying a fuel gas

Problem. A 0.846 g sample of a fuel gas occupies 0.355 L at 755 mmHg and 22.0 °C. Find its molar mass and its density. Is it propane (C3H8, 44.097 g/mol) or butane (C4H10, 58.124 g/mol)?

Solution. P = 755 ÷ 760 = 0.9934 atm; T = 295.15 K.

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(0.9934)(0.355)}{(0.082058)(295.15)} = 0.01456\ \text{mol} \qquad M = \dfrac{0.846\ \text{g}}{0.01456\ \text{mol}} = 58.1\ \text{g/mol} \)

The gas is butane. Its density at these conditions is d = 0.846 g ÷ 0.355 L = 2.38 g/L.

Check. Butane's density is about twice that of air (about 1.2 g/L), which is why leaking butane or propane collects in low places, a fire hazard around camp stoves and lighters.

Molar mass works like a gas's ID number or fingerprint: you cannot tell gases apart by looking at them, but you can calculate their molar mass. The route is always the same: P, V, and T give n; then M = m/n; then match M to a clue in the problem.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): A mystery diatomic gas

Problem. A 3.48 g sample of a gas occupies 1.20 L at 1.00 atm and 25.0 °C. The gas is an element that exists as diatomic molecules. Which one is it?

Solution. One set of conditions, no subscripts: use PV = nRT. T = 298.15 K.

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(1.00\ \text{atm})(1.20\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})} = 0.04905\ \text{mol} \qquad M = \dfrac{3.48\ \text{g}}{0.04905\ \text{mol}} = 71.0\ \text{g/mol} \)

The seven diatomic elements are H2, N2, F2, O2, I2, Cl2, and Br2 ("Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Beer"). Each molecule has two atoms, so halve the molar mass: 71.0 ÷ 2 = 35.5 g/mol, the atomic mass of chlorine. The gas is Cl2 (70.90 g/mol).

Check. Chlorine is a gas at room temperature, as it must be here. Forgetting to halve would send you looking for an atom of mass 71, and no diatomic element has one.

Molar volume plus an empirical formula

The hardest problems combine this chapter with Chapter 6. Percent composition gives only the empirical formula (the simplest ratio). Gas data give the molar mass. Divide the molar mass by the empirical-formula mass to get the whole number that turns the empirical formula into the molecular formula.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): From gas data and percent composition to a molecular formula

Problem. A hydrocarbon gas used to make plastics is 85.63% carbon and 14.37% hydrogen by mass. A 1.26 g sample occupies 0.672 L at STP. What is its molecular formula?

Solution. Step 1, molar mass from the gas data. At STP, 1 mol = 22.4 L:

\( 0.672\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{22.4\ \text{L}} = 0.0300\ \text{mol} \qquad M = \dfrac{1.26\ \text{g}}{0.0300\ \text{mol}} = 42.0\ \text{g/mol} \)

Step 2, empirical formula. Assume 100 g: 85.63 g C ÷ 12.011 g/mol = 7.129 mol C; 14.37 g H ÷ 1.008 g/mol = 14.26 mol H. Divide by the smaller: C 1.000, H 2.000. The empirical formula is CH2 (14.027 g/mol).

Step 3, the multiplier. 42.0 ÷ 14.027 = 2.99, about 3. The molecular formula is 3 × CH2 = C3H6 (propene, 42.081 g/mol).

Check. The multiplier must come out close to a whole number; if it does not, recheck the molar mass. C3H6 has the same 1 : 2 ratio as CH2.

Combined or Ideal? The Secret Is in the Subscripts

You really need only two gas equations: the combined gas law and the ideal gas law. To choose, read the problem for subscripts. If it gives two pressures, two volumes, two temperatures, or two amounts (words like "increased," "compressed," "heated," "then"), the gas is changing, and the numbers carry subscripts 1 and 2: use the combined gas law. If it gives just one value of each, it describes the current state of the gas, with no subscripts: use PV = nRT.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Same balloon, two questions

Problem. (a) A 6.00 L balloon is filled at STP. The balloon is then taken to a hyperbaric chamber where the pressure is increased to 1.50 atm and the temperature to 35.0 °C. What is its new volume? (b) A 6.00 L balloon is at 1.50 atm and 35.0 °C. How many moles of gas does it hold?

Solution. (a) Two pressures and two temperatures: the gas changes, so use the combined gas law, with T1 = 273 K (STP) and T2 = 308.15 K:

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 6.00\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1.00\ \text{atm}}{1.50\ \text{atm}} \times \dfrac{308.15\ \text{K}}{273\ \text{K}} = 4.52\ \text{L} \)

(b) One pressure, one volume, one temperature: the current state, so use PV = nRT:

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(1.50\ \text{atm})(6.00\ \text{L})}{(0.082058)(308.15\ \text{K})} = 0.356\ \text{mol} \)

Check. In (a) the higher pressure squeezes the balloon more than the warming expands it, so it shrinks. Part (a) needed no R, and part (b) needed no "before" values.

Professor Tip

Ask first: is the gas changing? If a problem has a "before" and an "after," use the combined gas law and you never need R. If there is one set of conditions, use PV = nRT. Then line up your units with R: liters (not mL), atm (not mmHg), kelvins (not °C), and moles (not grams). Most wrong answers come from one unconverted unit.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Patients like Mateo often arrive in imaging on portable oxygen. In an MRI suite, a standard steel oxygen cylinder is a deadly hazard: the scanner's magnet is always on, and it can pull a steel cylinder across the room like a missile. MRI departments use non-magnetic cylinders and equipment labeled MR Safe or MR Conditional (often aluminum), keep ferromagnetic items outside a controlled zone, and screen everything that enters. The cylinder's high pressure (Section 10.2) adds a second danger: a damaged valve can turn it into a projectile anywhere. (Context only; follow the department's MRI safety policy.)

Common Mistakes

  • Using °C, mL, or mmHg with R = 0.082058: convert to K, L, and atm.
  • Putting grams in for n: convert to moles with the molar mass.
  • Using 22.4 L/mol away from STP.
  • Using PV = nRT for a before-and-after problem (it works, but the combined gas law is simpler and avoids R). Look for subscripts.
  • Forgetting to halve the molar mass of a diatomic element before matching it to the periodic table.
  • Dividing by R and then multiplying by T on the calculator: put (R × T) in parentheses.

Summary

  • PV = nRT, with R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K) and T in kelvins.
  • At STP (273 K, 1 atm), 1 mol of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L.
  • Molar mass M = m/n with n from PV = nRT; density d = m/V. Molar mass is a gas's ID number.
  • Subscripts 1 and 2 (change): combined gas law. One value of each (current state): PV = nRT.
  • Gas data give the molar mass; percent composition gives the empirical formula; their ratio gives the molecular formula.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What volume do 2.25 mol of N2 occupy at 1.50 atm and 30.0 °C?

Hint

One set of conditions, so use PV = nRT solved for V. Convert 30.0 °C to kelvins and use R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K).

Answer

V = (2.25)(0.082058)(303.15) ÷ 1.50 = 37.3 L.

With one set of conditions, V = nRT/P, using T = 303.15 K; R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K) with P in atm leaves the answer in liters.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

How many moles of O2 are in Mateo's 4.70 L portable cylinder at 136 atm and 21.0 °C?

Hint

Solve PV = nRT for n. Change 21.0 °C to kelvins, and put R × T in parentheses on the calculator.

Answer

n = (136)(4.70) ÷ [(0.082058)(294.15)] = 26.5 mol.

n = PV/(RT) with T = 294.15 K; P in atm and V in liters match R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K), so only moles are left.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What volume does 4.40 g of CO2 occupy at STP?

Hint

Our goal is the mole: change grams to moles with the molar mass of CO2, then use 22.4 L/mol, which works because the gas is at STP.

Answer

4.40 ÷ 44.009 = 0.1000 mol; × 22.4 L/mol = 2.24 L.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A 1.14 g sample of a diatomic element occupies 0.800 L at STP. Identify the gas.

Hint

At STP, 22.4 L/mol gives the moles; grams divided by moles gives the molar mass. The gas is diatomic, so halve it before looking at the periodic table.

Answer

n = 0.800 ÷ 22.4 = 0.03571 mol; M = 1.14 ÷ 0.03571 = 31.9 g/mol. Half is 16.0, oxygen: the gas is O2 (31.998 g/mol).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Combined or ideal? (a) A tire holds 12.0 L of air at 2.40 atm and 18.0 °C; how many moles of air does it hold? (b) The same tire warms to 42.0 °C on the road at constant volume; what is its new pressure?

Hint

Look for subscripts. (a) One set of conditions: PV = nRT. (b) Two temperatures, so the gas changes: cross V and n out of the combined gas law. Use kelvins.

Answer

(a) One set of conditions: n = (2.40)(12.0) ÷ [(0.082058)(291.15)] = 1.21 mol. (b) A change (two temperatures): P2 = 2.40 × 315.15 ÷ 291.15 = 2.60 atm.
