Learning Objectives

  • Use the ideal gas law, PV = nRT, to find pressure, volume, moles, or temperature.
  • Choose the value of R that matches the units, and use the molar volume at STP.
  • Find the molar mass and density of a gas from its mass, volume, pressure, and temperature.
  • Decide whether a problem needs the combined gas law or the ideal gas law.

The simple gas laws compare a gas before and after a change. Often, though, there is no change: you know three of the four variables for one sample and need the fourth. How many moles of air fill your lungs? What pressure does a known mass of gas exert in a flask? For these, combine all four relationships into one equation.

The Ideal Gas Law

Boyle's, Charles's, and Avogadro's laws together say that V is proportional to nT/P. Writing the proportionality constant as R gives the ideal gas law:

\( PV = nRT \)

R is the ideal gas constant. Its value depends on the units, so the units of P, V, and T must match R exactly:

  • R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K): P in atm, V in liters, T in kelvins. This is the one you will use most.
  • R = 62.36 L·torr/(mol·K): P in torr (mmHg).
  • R = 8.3144 J/(mol·K): for energy calculations.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Air in the lungs

Problem. A person's lungs hold 5.85 L of air at 37.0 °C and 1.00 atm. How many moles of gas is that?

Solution. T = 37.0 + 273.15 = 310.15 K. Rearrange PV = nRT for n:

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(1.00\ \text{atm})(5.85\ \text{L})}{(0.082058\ \tfrac{\text{L}\cdot\text{atm}}{\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}})(310.15\ \text{K})} = 0.230\ \text{mol} \)

Check. At body temperature a mole of gas fills about 25 L, so 5.85 L is roughly a quarter of a mole. All units cancel except mol.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Pressure from a mass of gas

Problem. 12.5 g of CO2 (44.009 g/mol) is placed in an empty 5.00 L flask at 25.0 °C. What is the pressure in the flask?

Solution. n = 12.5 ÷ 44.009 = 0.2840 mol; T = 298.15 K.

\( P = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{(0.2840\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})}{5.00\ \text{L}} = 1.39\ \text{atm} \)

Check. Grams were converted to moles first: our goal is the mole. About a quarter of a mole in 5 L gives a pressure a bit above 1 atm.

STP and Molar Volume

Standard temperature and pressure (STP) is 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm. Solving PV = nRT for the volume of 1.00 mol at STP:

\( V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{(1.00\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(273\ \text{K})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 22.4\ \text{L} \)

This molar volume, 22.4 L/mol, is the same for every ideal gas at STP, because volume depends on the number of particles, not on what they are (Avogadro's law). Use it as a conversion factor only at STP; at any other conditions, use PV = nRT.

Molar Mass and Density of a Gas

If you know the mass of a gas sample and can find its moles from PV = nRT, then its molar mass is grams divided by moles, M = m/n. Its density is simply mass divided by volume, d = m/V, usually in g/L for gases. Gas densities depend strongly on temperature and pressure, so a density is always reported with its conditions.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Identifying a fuel gas

Problem. A 0.846 g sample of a fuel gas occupies 0.355 L at 755 mmHg and 22.0 °C. Find its molar mass and its density. Is it propane (C3H8, 44.097 g/mol) or butane (C4H10, 58.124 g/mol)?

Solution. P = 755 ÷ 760 = 0.9934 atm; T = 295.15 K.

\( n = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{(0.9934)(0.355)}{(0.082058)(295.15)} = 0.01456\ \text{mol} \qquad M = \dfrac{0.846\ \text{g}}{0.01456\ \text{mol}} = 58.1\ \text{g/mol} \)

The gas is butane. Its density at these conditions is d = 0.846 g ÷ 0.355 L = 2.38 g/L.

Check. Butane's density is about twice that of air (about 1.2 g/L), which is why leaking butane or propane collects in low places, a fire hazard around camp stoves and lighters.

Professor Tip

Ask first: is the gas changing? If a problem has a "before" and an "after," use the combined gas law and you never need R. If there is one set of conditions, use PV = nRT. Then line up your units with R: liters (not mL), atm (not mmHg), kelvins (not °C), and moles (not grams). Most wrong answers come from one unconverted unit.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Patients like Mateo often arrive in imaging on portable oxygen. In an MRI suite, a standard steel oxygen cylinder is a deadly hazard: the scanner's magnet is always on, and it can pull a steel cylinder across the room like a missile. MRI departments use non-magnetic cylinders and equipment labeled MR Safe or MR Conditional (often aluminum), keep ferromagnetic items outside a controlled zone, and screen everything that enters. The cylinder's high pressure (Section 10.2) adds a second danger: a damaged valve can turn it into a projectile anywhere. (Context only; follow the department's MRI safety policy.)

Common Mistakes

  • Using °C, mL, or mmHg with R = 0.082058: convert to K, L, and atm.
  • Putting grams in for n: convert to moles with the molar mass.
  • Using 22.4 L/mol away from STP.
  • Using PV = nRT for a before-and-after problem (it works, but the combined gas law is simpler and avoids R).

Summary

  • PV = nRT, with R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K) and T in kelvins.
  • At STP (273 K, 1 atm), 1 mol of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L.
  • Molar mass M = m/n with n from PV = nRT; density d = m/V.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What volume do 2.25 mol of N2 occupy at 1.50 atm and 30.0 °C?

Answer

V = (2.25)(0.082058)(303.15) ÷ 1.50 = 37.3 L.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

How many moles of O2 are in Mateo's 4.70 L portable cylinder at 136 atm and 21.0 °C?

Answer

n = (136)(4.70) ÷ [(0.082058)(294.15)] = 26.5 mol.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What volume does 4.40 g of CO2 occupy at STP?

Answer

4.40 ÷ 44.009 = 0.1000 mol; × 22.4 L/mol = 2.24 L.
