Learning Objectives

  • Describe the properties of gases and how they differ from liquids and solids.
  • State the main ideas of kinetic molecular theory.
  • Use kinetic molecular theory to explain pressure, compressibility, diffusion, and the effect of temperature.
  • Explain why equal amounts of different gases take up the same volume.

A gas has no shape and no fixed volume: it spreads out to fill whatever container holds it, it can be squeezed into a small tank, and its smell drifts across a room. In Chapter 9, strong intermolecular forces held liquids and solids together. In a gas, the particles are so far apart and moving so fast that those forces hardly matter, and that is exactly why all gases behave in nearly the same way.

Properties of Gases

  • Gases expand to fill their container and take its shape.
  • Gases are compressible: their volume shrinks a great deal when pressure increases. Liquids and solids barely compress.
  • Gases have low densities, about a thousand times lower than liquids. A liter of air has a mass of only about 1.2 g.
  • Gases mix completely with each other, and they spread through one another by diffusion.

Kinetic Molecular Theory

Kinetic molecular theory (KMT) is the model chemists use to explain these properties. It describes an ideal gas:

  1. A gas is made of particles (atoms or molecules) that are tiny compared with the distances between them. Most of a gas is empty space.
  2. The particles move constantly and randomly in straight lines until they collide with each other or with the walls.
  3. Collisions are elastic: no kinetic energy is lost overall. Each collision with a wall pushes on it, and these countless pushes are the gas's pressure.
  4. The particles neither attract nor repel each other.
  5. The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the Kelvin temperature. At the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy.

A good picture is a table of billiard balls that never slow down: they travel in straight lines, bounce off each other and off the cushions without losing energy, and never stick together. Gases are the easiest state to study for exactly this reason: their particles are so far apart that the intermolecular forces of Chapter 9 (hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole, dispersion) hardly come into play.

A particle simulation makes the ideas visible. Put three particles in a box at room temperature and they bounce slowly around; heat the box and they speed up and hit the walls more often. Put 200 particles in the same box and the walls are struck constantly, a "free-for-all" that shows where pressure comes from: more particles, more hits. Cool the box with ice and the particles slow down, drifting like turtles, and they hit the walls less hard and less often.

Explaining Gas Behavior

  • Compressibility: there is lots of empty space between particles, so they can be pushed closer together.
  • Pressure: particles hitting the walls create pressure. More particles, or faster particles, mean more hits and higher pressure.
  • Temperature: heating a gas makes its particles move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often. If the container can expand, the gas takes up more volume; if it cannot, the pressure rises.
  • Diffusion: particles move randomly and constantly, so gases spread into each other. At the same temperature, lighter particles move faster on average than heavier ones, so lighter gases diffuse faster.

Real gases follow this ideal model closely at ordinary temperatures and pressures. They depart from it at very high pressures, when the particles are crowded and their own volume matters, and at low temperatures, when attractions between particles start to matter and the gas condenses.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Explaining with KMT

Problem. Use kinetic molecular theory to explain (a) why a sealed bag of chips puffs up when it is carried up a mountain; (b) why a car's tire pressure is higher after a long drive on a hot day.

Solution. (a) Outside air pressure is lower at high altitude, so fewer air molecules hit the outside of the bag than the gas molecules inside hit from within; the bag expands until the pushes balance. (b) The air in the tire gets hotter, its molecules move faster and hit the tire walls harder and more often, and the tire cannot expand much, so the pressure rises.

Check. Each explanation talks about particles: how many hit the walls, and how hard.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Which gas moves faster?

Problem. At the same temperature, which has the greater average kinetic energy, He or CO2? Which has the greater average speed?

Solution. They have the same average kinetic energy, because kinetic energy depends only on the Kelvin temperature. Helium atoms (4.003 g/mol) are much lighter than CO2 molecules (44.009 g/mol), so to have the same kinetic energy they must move faster.

Check. This is why a helium balloon deflates faster than an air-filled one: the small, fast helium atoms escape through the rubber more quickly.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Can you tell the gases apart?

Problem. Two identical sealed flasks sit side by side at the same temperature and pressure. One holds 1 mol of oxygen, O2, which you need to live; the other holds 1 mol of carbon monoxide, CO, a colorless, odorless gas that is deadly because it blocks oxygen transport in the blood. Can you tell which is which by the volume each gas takes up?

Solution. No. Gas particles are so tiny and so far apart that the identity of the particle has almost no effect on how much space the gas occupies; the volume is set by the number of particles, the temperature, and the pressure. One mole of any gas fills the same volume under the same conditions (22.4 L at 0 °C and 1 atm, Section 10.4). The labels on gas cylinders matter.

Check. This is very different from liquids and solids: a mole of liquid water (18 mL) and a mole of liquid mercury (15 mL) take up different volumes because their particles touch.

Professor Tip

Keep four variables in mind for every gas: pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T), and amount in moles (n). Every gas law in this chapter is a statement about how changing one of them affects another. When a question asks "why," answer in terms of particles: how many there are, how fast they move, and how often they hit the walls.

Biology Connection: Gas Exchange by Diffusion

Your lungs contain roughly 300 to 500 million tiny air sacs called alveoli, whose walls are thinner than a sheet of tissue paper and wrapped in capillaries. No pump pushes oxygen into the blood: O2 simply diffuses from the air in the alveoli, where it is more concentrated, into the blood, and CO2 diffuses the other way. The huge surface area and thin walls make this random molecular motion fast enough to supply the whole body. Diseases that thicken the alveolar walls or destroy alveoli, such as pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema, slow diffusion and lower blood oxygen.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking heavier gases have more kinetic energy: at the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy; heavier ones just move slower.
  • Using °C for the temperature in KMT: kinetic energy is proportional to the Kelvin temperature.
  • Forgetting that a gas is mostly empty space, which is why it compresses.
  • Thinking a heavier gas takes up more room: equal moles of any gases at the same temperature and pressure fill the same volume.

Summary

  • Gases fill their containers, compress easily, have low densities, and mix by diffusion.
  • KMT: tiny, widely spaced particles in constant random motion, with elastic collisions and no attractions; average kinetic energy is proportional to Kelvin temperature.
  • Pressure comes from collisions with the walls; lighter gases move faster at the same temperature.
  • Gas particles bounce like billiard balls and have almost no intermolecular forces; their identity does not change the volume a gas occupies.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Why can a large amount of oxygen fit into a small steel cylinder, while the same mass of liquid water cannot be squeezed into a much smaller volume?

Hint

Compare how far apart the particles are in a gas and in a liquid. What fills most of a gas that a liquid does not have?

Answer

A gas is mostly empty space, so its particles can be pushed much closer together. In a liquid the molecules already touch, so it hardly compresses.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A rigid, sealed container of gas is cooled. What happens to the speed of the particles and to the pressure?

Hint

Temperature sets the average kinetic energy. If the particles slow down, how do their hits on the walls change? The rigid volume cannot change.

Answer

The particles slow down, so they hit the walls less hard and less often; the pressure decreases.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

At the same temperature, rank N2, H2, and Cl2 from slowest to fastest average speed.

Hint

Same temperature means the same average kinetic energy. Compare the molar masses: lighter particles must move faster to carry the same energy.

Answer

Cl2 (heaviest) < N2 < H2 (lightest). All three have the same average kinetic energy.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A rigid box holds 10 gas particles. Using kinetic molecular theory, describe what happens to the pressure if (a) 10 more particles are added at the same temperature; (b) the original 10 particles are heated.

Hint

Pressure comes from particles hitting the walls. (a) More particles means more hits. (b) Heating makes each particle faster: think about how hard and how often they hit.

Answer

(a) The pressure increases (about doubles): twice as many particles hit the walls. (b) The pressure increases: the particles move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often.
