Learning Objectives

  • State Boyle's, Charles's, Gay-Lussac's, and Avogadro's laws and identify each relationship as direct or inverse.
  • Use the simple gas laws and the combined gas law to find a new pressure, volume, temperature, or amount.
  • Convert temperatures to kelvins for every gas law calculation.
  • Build the right equation for any pair of changing variables by crossing out the constants, and solve for an unknown in a denominator by flipping both sides.
  • Predict a relationship by plugging in easy numbers, and state an answer in a one-sentence interpretation.

Squeeze a balloon and it gets smaller; warm it and it gets bigger; blow more air into it and it grows. Each of these everyday observations is a gas law, a mathematical relationship between two of the four gas variables (P, V, T, and n) while the other two stay constant.

Direct and Inverse Relationships

  • In a direct relationship, both variables move in the same direction (one doubles, the other doubles). The formula is a ratio: A1/B1 = A2/B2.
  • In an inverse relationship, they move in opposite directions (one doubles, the other halves). The formula is a product: A1B1 = A2B2.

Boyle's Law: Pressure and Volume

At constant temperature and amount of gas, pressure and volume are inversely related: squeeze a gas into half the volume and its pressure doubles, because the same particles hit the walls twice as often.

\( P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 \)

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Compressing a gas

Problem. A 255 mL sample of gas at 1.00 atm is compressed at constant temperature until its pressure is 2.35 atm. What is its new volume?

Solution.

\( V_2 = \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{(1.00\ \text{atm})(255\ \text{mL})}{2.35\ \text{atm}} = 109\ \text{mL} \)

Check. Pressure went up, so volume went down, as an inverse relationship requires. In one sentence: when the pressure increases from 1.00 atm to 2.35 atm, the gas is compressed from 255 mL to 109 mL.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Mateo's portable oxygen

Problem. For his trip to radiology, Mateo is switched to a portable oxygen cylinder with an internal volume of 4.70 L, filled to 136 atm. (a) What volume would this oxygen occupy at 1.00 atm and the same temperature? (b) At 2.0 L/min, about how long could it last?

Solution. (a) Boyle's law:

\( V_2 = \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{(136\ \text{atm})(4.70\ \text{L})}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 639\ \text{L} \)

(b) 639 L ÷ 2.0 L/min = 3.2 × 102 min, a little over 5 hours.

Check. In practice a cylinder is replaced well before it is empty, and gauge readings, temperature, and the gas left at the end all reduce the usable time, so respiratory therapists use a cylinder-specific factor and a safety margin. (Context only; not a clinical calculation.)

Biology Connection: Breathing Is Boyle's Law

Your lungs have no muscles of their own; they follow Boyle's law. When the diaphragm contracts and moves down and the rib muscles lift the chest, the volume of the chest cavity and lungs increases, so the pressure inside the lungs drops a few mmHg below atmospheric pressure, and air flows in. When the diaphragm relaxes, the volume decreases, the pressure rises slightly above atmospheric, and air flows out. During an asthma attack, narrowed airways make that flow harder, so the person must create larger pressure differences, which is why breathing becomes such hard work.

Charles's Law: Volume and Temperature

At constant pressure and amount of gas, volume and Kelvin temperature are directly related: heated particles move faster and push the walls outward.

\( \dfrac{V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{T_2} \qquad (T \text{ in K}) \)

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Warming inhaled air

Problem. You breathe in 2.50 L of air at 22.0 °C. If the pressure stays constant, what volume does the air occupy after it warms to body temperature, 37.0 °C?

Solution. T1 = 22.0 + 273.15 = 295.2 K; T2 = 37.0 + 273.15 = 310.2 K.

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 2.50\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{310.15\ \text{K}}{295.15\ \text{K}} = 2.63\ \text{L} \)

Check. The temperature went up, so the volume went up, but only slightly, because 15 °C is a small change on the Kelvin scale.

Gay-Lussac's Law: Pressure and Temperature

At constant volume and amount of gas, pressure and Kelvin temperature are directly related. This is why aerosol cans carry the warning "Do not store above 120 °F." It also explains your car's tires. On a hot summer day the air inside heats up and the tire pressure rises; on a cold winter morning the air molecules slow down, the pressure drops, and the low-tire-pressure warning light can come on even though no air leaked out.

\( \dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2} \)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): A spray can in a hot car

Problem. The gas in a spray can is at 1.50 atm at 25.0 °C. The can is left in a car that heats up to 65.0 °C. What is the new pressure?

Solution.

\( P_2 = P_1 \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 1.50\ \text{atm} \times \dfrac{338.15\ \text{K}}{298.15\ \text{K}} = 1.70\ \text{atm} \)

Check. Hotter gas in a rigid can means higher pressure. (Using °C, 65.0/25.0, would wrongly predict the pressure more than doubles.)

When the unknown is on the bottom: flip both sides

In P1/T1 = P2/T2, the unknown temperature T2 sits in a denominator, which makes the algebra awkward. A quick trick: if each side is a single fraction, flip both sides. The equation is still true, and now the unknown is on top:

\( \dfrac{P_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2}{T_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{T_1}{P_1} = \dfrac{T_2}{P_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_2 = \dfrac{T_1 P_2}{P_1} \)

Multiply both sides by P2 and T2 is alone. The same move works for Charles's law (T1/V1 = T2/V2) and for the combined gas law.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Finding a temperature

Problem. A sealed can of gas is at 1.25 atm and 22.0 °C. At what temperature, in °C, would its pressure reach 1.80 atm?

Solution. Given: P1 = 1.25 atm, T1 = 22.0 + 273.15 = 295.15 K, P2 = 1.80 atm. Desired: T2. Volume and moles are constant, so Gay-Lussac's law applies. Flip both sides and solve:

\( T_2 = \dfrac{T_1 P_2}{P_1} = \dfrac{(295.15\ \text{K})(1.80\ \text{atm})}{1.25\ \text{atm}} = 425\ \text{K} \qquad 425 - 273.15 = 152\ {}^\circ\text{C} \)

Check. The pressure went up, so the temperature went up (direct relationship). Pressure rose by a factor of 1.44, and so did the Kelvin temperature (295 K × 1.44 = 425 K); the Celsius temperature did not rise by that factor, which is why gas laws use kelvins.

Avogadro's Law: Volume and Moles

At constant pressure and temperature, volume and the number of moles are directly related: more gas particles take up more room. (Some students remember it as "avocado's law.") Blow into an empty balloon and it inflates; keep blowing, more moles go in, and it gets bigger. Suck the air back out and it shrinks. Equal volumes of any two gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of particles.

\( \dfrac{V_1}{n_1} = \dfrac{V_2}{n_2} \)

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Adding gas to a balloon

Problem. A balloon holds 0.0750 mol of helium in 1.80 L. More helium is added until the balloon holds 0.120 mol at the same temperature and pressure. What is its new volume?

Solution.

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = 1.80\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{0.120\ \text{mol}}{0.0750\ \text{mol}} = 2.88\ \text{L} \)

Check. More moles, more volume.

The Combined Gas Law

When pressure, volume, and temperature all change, the three laws combine into one:

\( \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \dfrac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} \)

The combined gas law contains Boyle's, Charles's, and Gay-Lussac's laws: cross out whichever variable stays constant and the simpler law is left. (Including n gives P1V1/(n1T1) = P2V2/(n2T2), which also contains Avogadro's law.) Pressures only have to be in the same unit on both sides, and so do volumes, but temperatures must be in kelvins.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): A weather balloon

Problem. A weather balloon released from Fresno holds 5.00 L of helium at 755 mmHg and 25.0 °C. What is its volume when it rises to where the pressure is 375 mmHg and the temperature is −15.0 °C?

Solution. T1 = 298.15 K; T2 = 258.15 K.

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 5.00\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{755\ \text{mmHg}}{375\ \text{mmHg}} \times \dfrac{258.15\ \text{K}}{298.15\ \text{K}} = 8.72\ \text{L} \)

Check. The pressure roughly halved (volume should about double) while the temperature dropped a little (volume shrinks a little), so a bit less than 10 L makes sense.

Cross out what stays constant

You do not need to memorize a separate formula for every pair of variables. Start from the full equation with all four variables,

\( \dfrac{P_1 V_1}{n_1 T_1} = \dfrac{P_2 V_2}{n_2 T_2} \)

and cross out every variable the problem says stays the same (it has the same value on both sides, so it cancels). What is left is the law you need, even for a pair that has no famous name, such as moles and temperature.

Example \(\PageIndex{8}\): Air escaping from a heated flask

Problem. An open flask contains 0.0410 mol of air at 20.0 °C. The flask is heated to 85.0 °C. Its volume does not change, and because it is open, the pressure inside stays equal to the room's pressure. How many moles of air are left in the flask?

Solution. Given: n1 = 0.0410 mol, T1 = 293.15 K, T2 = 358.15 K. Desired: n2. P and V are constant (the same P and V on both sides), so they cancel:

\( \dfrac{P V}{n_1 T_1} = \dfrac{P V}{n_2 T_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{1}{n_1 T_1} = \dfrac{1}{n_2 T_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_1 T_1 = n_2 T_2 \)

\( n_2 = \dfrac{n_1 T_1}{T_2} = \dfrac{(0.0410\ \text{mol})(293.15\ \text{K})}{358.15\ \text{K}} = 0.0336\ \text{mol} \)

Check. Moles and temperature are inversely related here: as the air heats, some of it is pushed out of the open flask, so fewer moles remain. In one sentence: when the flask is heated from 20.0 °C to 85.0 °C, the air in it drops from 0.0410 mol to 0.0336 mol.

Professor Tip: Plug In Easy Numbers

Not sure how one variable affects another? Make up easy numbers and let the equation tell you. "If the pressure doubles at constant temperature, what happens to the volume?" Use P1 = 1 atm, V1 = 1 L, P2 = 2 atm in P1V1 = P2V2: V2 = (1)(1)/2 = ½ L. The volume is cut in half. The same trick works for any gas law and for any formula in this course.

Professor Tip

Before you calculate, predict. Keep an example in mind for each pair: a chip bag puffing up in the mountains (P and V, inverse), a balloon shrinking in liquid nitrogen (T and V, direct), a spray can bursting in a hot car (T and P, direct), and a balloon you keep blowing into (n and V, direct). Then work every gas-law problem in five steps: (1) write the givens and the desired quantity, with subscripts 1 and 2 for anything that changes; (2) identify which variables change; (3) decide whether their relationship is direct or inverse; (4) turn that relationship into an equation (cross out the constants); (5) solve for the desired quantity, with every temperature in kelvins. Finish with a one-sentence interpretation, such as "When the pressure increases from 1.00 atm to 3.28 atm, the volume is compressed from 425 mL to 130. mL," and check that it moved in the direction you predicted.

Clinical Connection: Nursing in Air Transport

Flight nurses and paramedics on medical helicopters and planes deal with Boyle's law at altitude. Cabin pressure falls as the aircraft climbs, so any trapped gas expands: the air-filled cuff of an endotracheal tube, the air in an IV bag or a pressure bag, air in a splint, and gas trapped in a patient's body, such as a pneumothorax (air in the chest outside the lung). Crews check and adjust cuff pressures during flight, and some fill cuffs with saline instead of air. (Context only; follow the transport program's protocols.)

Common Mistakes

  • Using °C in a gas law: always convert to kelvins.
  • Setting up Boyle's law as a ratio: P and V are inverse, so multiply (P1V1 = P2V2).
  • Mixing units between state 1 and state 2 (mmHg and atm, or mL and L).
  • Skipping the prediction: an answer that moves the wrong way signals an upside-down ratio.
  • Hunting for a formula that is not on the list: start from P1V1/(n1T1) = P2V2/(n2T2) and cross out the constants.
  • Converting the answer temperature incorrectly: a gas law gives T in kelvins; subtract 273.15 if the question asks for °C.

Summary

  • Boyle: P1V1 = P2V2 (inverse). Charles: V1/T1 = V2/T2. Gay-Lussac: P1/T1 = P2/T2. Avogadro: V1/n1 = V2/n2.
  • Combined gas law: P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2; cross out what stays constant.
  • Temperatures must be in kelvins.
  • For any pair of variables, cross the constants out of P1V1/(n1T1) = P2V2/(n2T2). If the unknown is in a denominator, flip both sides.
  • Plug in easy numbers to predict a relationship; finish with a one-sentence interpretation.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

The gas in a sealed syringe occupies 10.0 mL at 1.00 atm. The plunger is pulled back until the volume is 25.0 mL at the same temperature. What is the new pressure?

Answer

P2 = (1.00)(10.0) ÷ 25.0 = 0.400 atm.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A rigid tank of nitrogen is at 2.15 atm at 20.0 °C. What is its pressure at 45.0 °C?

Answer

P2 = 2.15 × 318.15 ÷ 293.15 = 2.33 atm.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A 3.25 L balloon at 27.0 °C is dipped in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C. Assuming constant pressure, what is its new volume?

Answer

T1 = 300.15 K; T2 = −196 + 273.15 = 77 K (no decimal places). V2 = 3.25 × 77 ÷ 300.15 = 0.83 L.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A rigid, sealed cylinder holds 1.85 mol of gas at 2.00 atm. Gas is let out at constant temperature until the pressure is 0.500 atm. Which variables are constant? Build the equation by crossing out the constants, and find the moles left.

Answer

V and T are constant, so P1/n1 = P2/n2 (direct). Flip both sides: n1/P1 = n2/P2, so n2 = (1.85)(0.500)/2.00 = 0.463 mol.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Use easy numbers to predict: at constant pressure and moles, what happens to the volume of a gas if its Kelvin temperature is cut in half?

Answer

Try V1 = 1 L, T1 = 200 K, T2 = 100 K in V1/T1 = V2/T2: V2 = (1)(100)/200 = ½ L. The volume is cut in half (direct relationship).
