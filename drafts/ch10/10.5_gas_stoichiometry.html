Learning Objectives

  • Use molar volume at STP, or the ideal gas law at other conditions, to connect gas volumes to moles in a reaction.
  • Find the mass or volume of one substance from the mass or volume of another.
  • Use volume ratios for gases measured at the same temperature and pressure.
  • Work backward from a volume of gas collected over water to the mass of a reactant.
  • Find the limiting reactant in a reaction that makes a gas, and the volume of gas formed.

Many reactions make or use gases: fuels burn in oxygen, your cells release carbon dioxide, and a car's airbag inflates with nitrogen in a few hundredths of a second. Stoichiometry (Chapter 8) tells you the moles; the gas laws turn moles into liters.

The Road Map with Gases

Every stoichiometry problem passes through the mole ratio. Gases simply add another on-ramp and off-ramp:

  • At STP (273 K, 1 atm): 1 mol of gas = 22.4 L (Section 8.3).
  • At any other conditions: n = PV/RT to go from a gas volume to moles, or V = nRT/P to go from moles to a gas volume.

\( \text{given (g, L, or L of gas)} \;\rightarrow\; \text{mol A} \;\xrightarrow{\text{mole ratio}}\; \text{mol B} \;\rightarrow\; \text{wanted (g, or L of gas via } V = nRT/P) \)

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Inflating an airbag

Problem. Early airbags inflated with nitrogen from the decomposition of sodium azide:

2 NaN3(s) → 2 Na(s) + 3 N2(g)

What volume of N2 forms from 65.0 g of NaN3 (65.01 g/mol) at 1.00 atm and 25.0 °C?

Solution. Moles of N2:

\( {65.0\;\text{g NaN}}_3 \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol NaN}_3}{65.01\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{3\;\text{mol N}_2}{2\;\text{mol NaN}_3} = {1.500\;\text{mol N}}_2 \)

Volume, with T = 298.15 K:

\( V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{({1.500\;\text{mol}})(0.082058)({298.15\;\text{K}})}{1.00\;\text{atm}} = {36.7\;\text{L}} \)

Check. At room temperature a mole of gas fills about 24.5 L, and 1.5 mol fills about 37 L, enough for a driver's airbag.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): CO2 from glucose

Problem. Your cells oxidize glucose: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O. What volume of CO2 at body temperature (37.0 °C) and 1.00 atm comes from 10.0 g of glucose (180.156 g/mol)?

Solution.

\( {10.0\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol glucose}}{180.156\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{6\;\text{mol CO}_2}{1\;\text{mol glucose}} = {0.3330\;\text{mol CO}}_2 \)

\( V = \dfrac{({0.3330\;\text{mol}})(0.082058)({310.15\;\text{K}})}{1.00\;\text{atm}} = {8.48\;\text{L}} \)

Check. About a third of a mole at body temperature (about 25 L/mol) is about 8.5 L of CO2, all of which leaves through your lungs.

Working Backward: From a Gas Collected Over Water

The road map runs in both directions. If you measure the volume of a gas a reaction produced, PV = nRT gives its moles, the mole ratio gives the moles of a reactant, and the molar mass gives grams. When the gas is collected over water, it is "wet": the bottle holds the gas plus water vapor, and the pressure you measure is their total. Subtract the vapor pressure of water first (Dalton's law, Section 10.6), so that the pressure in PV = nRT belongs to the gas you care about.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): How much aluminum reacted?

Problem. Aluminum metal reacts with hydrochloric acid:

2 Al(s) + 6 HCl(aq) → 2 AlCl3(aq) + 3 H2(g)

A student collects 365 mL of H2 over water at 30.0 °C. The total pressure in the collection bottle is 748 mmHg, and the vapor pressure of water at 30.0 °C is 31.8 mmHg. What mass of aluminum reacted?

Solution. Step 1, the dry gas's pressure. PH2 = 748 − 31.8 = 716 mmHg (rounded to the ones place, like 748); 716 ÷ 760 = 0.9421 atm.

Step 2, convert to ideal-gas units. V = 0.365 L; T = 30.0 + 273.15 = 303.15 K.

Step 3, moles of H2.

\( n_{\text{H}_2} = \dfrac{PV}{RT} = \dfrac{({0.9421\;\text{atm}})({0.365\;\text{L}})}{(0.082058)({303.15\;\text{K}})} = {0.01382\;\text{mol H}}_2 \)

Step 4, mole ratio and molar mass.

\( {0.01382\;\text{mol H}}_2 \times \dfrac{2\;\text{mol Al}}{3\;\text{mol H}_2} \times \dfrac{26.98\;\text{g Al}}{1\;\text{mol Al}} = {0.249\;\text{g Al}} \)

Check. Every number in PV = nRT describes the H2, so n is moles of H2, not of Al; the mole ratio does the switch. Forgetting to subtract the water vapor would make the answer about 4% too high.

Limiting Reactant with a Gas Product

When a problem gives amounts of two reactants, one of them runs out first (Chapter 8). A fast way to find it: take each reactant to moles of the gas product and stop. The reactant that makes fewer moles of product is the limiting reactant, and that smaller number of moles is the one that goes into V = nRT/P (or 22.4 L/mol at STP). (Section 8.3 showed reactants given as a solution volume and molarity; the method is the same.)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Carbon monoxide from iron ore

Problem. In a blast furnace, iron(III) oxide reacts with carbon:

Fe2O3(s) + 3 C(s) → 2 Fe(l) + 3 CO(g)

A lab-scale test heats 10.0 g of Fe2O3 with 2.00 g of C. Which reactant is limiting, and what volume of CO forms, measured at STP?

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Molar masses for Example 4
SubstanceMolar mass (g mol−1)
Fe2O3159.70
C12.011

Solution. Take each reactant to moles of CO:

\( {10.0\;\text{g Fe}}_2\text{O}_3 \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol Fe}_2\text{O}_3}{159.70\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{3\;\text{mol CO}}{1\;\text{mol Fe}_2\text{O}_3} = {0.1879\;\text{mol CO}} \)

\( {2.00\;\text{g C}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol C}}{12.011\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{3\;\text{mol CO}}{3\;\text{mol C}} = {0.1665\;\text{mol CO}} \)

Carbon makes less CO, so carbon is the limiting reactant, and 0.1665 mol of CO forms:

\( {0.1665\;\text{mol CO}} \times \dfrac{22.4\;\text{L}}{1\;\text{mol}} = {3.73\;\text{L CO}} \)

Check. The limiting reactant is decided by moles of product, not by grams: there are fewer grams of carbon, but that is not why it is limiting. Carbon monoxide is a toxic gas, which is one reason furnaces and gas appliances must be vented.

Volume Ratios

When all the gases in a reaction are measured at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes contain equal numbers of moles (Avogadro's law). The coefficients then give volume ratios directly, with no need for moles or R.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Making ammonia

Problem. For N2(g) + 3 H2(g) → 2 NH3(g), what volume of NH3 can 4.50 L of H2 make, and what volume of N2 does it need, all at the same temperature and pressure?

Solution.

\( {4.50\;\text{L H}}_2 \times \dfrac{2\;\text{L NH}_3}{3\;\text{L H}_2} = {3.00\;\text{L NH}}_3 \qquad {4.50\;\text{L H}}_2 \times \dfrac{1\;\text{L N}_2}{3\;\text{L H}_2} = {1.50\;\text{L N}}_2 \)

Check. The coefficients 1 : 3 : 2 apply to liters only because every gas is at the same T and P.

Professor Tip

Our goal is the mole. Find the moles of what you are given, use the mole ratio, and only at the end turn moles into the unit asked for. Look for the words "at STP": if they are there, 22.4 L/mol is your shortcut; if a different temperature or pressure is given, use PV = nRT. And watch for volumes in mL; R needs liters. The mistake most people make the first time is putting the moles of the given substance (for example, glucose) into PV = nRT. The n in PV = nRT must be the moles of the gas you are asked about, which you get only after the mole ratio.

Biology Connection: Microbiology and Anaerobic Jars

Some bacteria, such as Clostridium species, are obligate anaerobes: oxygen kills them, so they must be grown without it. A common method is an anaerobic jar. A sealed jar holds the culture plates and a gas-generating packet that releases hydrogen and carbon dioxide. A palladium catalyst in the jar makes the hydrogen react with the oxygen in the jar's air, 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O, using it up, while the CO2 helps the bacteria grow. Gas stoichiometry tells the manufacturer how much hydrogen is needed to remove the oxygen from a jar of a given volume.

Major Connection: Engineering

An airbag must inflate fully in about 30 milliseconds, so its designers use gas stoichiometry and the ideal gas law to size the propellant charge: too little gas and the bag is soft; too much and it can injure. Sodium azide (Example 1) was used in early airbags but is toxic, so modern inflators use other propellants or stored compressed gas. The calculation is the same: grams of propellant, to moles of gas, to liters at the temperature and pressure inside the bag.

Common Mistakes

  • Skipping the mole ratio and putting moles of the given substance into PV = nRT for the product.
  • Using 22.4 L/mol when the gas is not at STP.
  • Using volume ratios for a solid or liquid: they work only for gases at the same T and P.
  • Forgetting to convert °C to K in V = nRT/P.
  • Using the total pressure of a gas collected over water: subtract the vapor pressure of water first.
  • Picking the limiting reactant by grams: compare the moles of product each reactant could make.

Summary

  • Gas stoichiometry: given → moles → mole ratio → moles → liters (22.4 L/mol at STP, or V = nRT/P).
  • For gases at the same T and P, coefficients give volume ratios.
  • Backward: Pgas = Ptotal − Pwater; n = PV/RT; mole ratio; grams.
  • Limiting reactant: the reactant that makes fewer moles of the gas product; use that amount in V = nRT/P.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). What volume of H2 forms from 0.500 g of Mg (24.31 g/mol) at 0.985 atm and 22.0 °C?

Hint

Our goal is the mole: grams of Mg to moles, mole ratio to H2, then V = nRT/P with T in kelvins. This is not STP, so not 22.4 L/mol.

Answer

0.500 ÷ 24.31 = 0.02057 mol Mg → 0.02057 mol H2. V = (0.02057)(0.082058)(295.15) ÷ 0.985 = 0.506 L (506 mL).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Heating limestone, CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). What volume of CO2 at STP comes from 30.0 g of CaCO3 (100.09 g/mol)?

Hint

Grams of CaCO3 to moles, then the mole ratio to CO2. The gas is at STP, so 22.4 L/mol turns moles into liters.

Answer

30.0 ÷ 100.09 = 0.2997 mol CO2; × 22.4 L/mol = 6.71 L.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

For 2 CO(g) + O2(g) → 2 CO2(g), what volume of O2 is needed to burn 6.00 L of CO, both at the same temperature and pressure?

Hint

Both gases are at the same temperature and pressure, so the coefficients are volume ratios. Read the ratio of O2 to CO from the equation.

Answer

6.00 L CO × (1 L O2 / 2 L CO) = 3.00 L O2.

At the same T and P, equal volumes hold equal moles (Avogadro's law), so the 1 : 2 coefficient ratio of O2 to CO is also a volume ratio, and liters go straight to liters.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g). A student collects 250. mL of H2 over water at 20.0 °C and a total pressure of 765 mmHg (water vapor pressure 17.5 mmHg). What mass of zinc (65.39 g/mol) reacted?

Hint

Subtract water's vapor pressure from the total first, so P belongs to the dry H2. Then n = PV/RT (mL to L, °C to K), the mole ratio, and the molar mass.

Answer

PH2 = 765 − 17.5 = 748 mmHg = 0.9842 atm. n = (0.9842)(0.250) ÷ [(0.082058)(293.15)] = 0.01023 mol H2 = 0.01023 mol Zn (1 : 1). Mass = 0.01023 × 65.39 = 0.669 g Zn.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

2 KClO3(s) → 2 KCl(s) + 3 O2(g). A student says, "I have 0.0400 mol of KClO3, so I'll put n = 0.0400 into V = nRT/P." What is wrong, and what n should be used?

Hint

Which substance does V = nRT/P describe here? The n must be moles of that gas, which you get only after the mole ratio from the balanced equation.

Answer

The gas is O2, not KClO3. Use the mole ratio first: 0.0400 mol KClO3 × (3 mol O2 / 2 mol KClO3) = 0.0600 mol O2. That is the n for V = nRT/P.
