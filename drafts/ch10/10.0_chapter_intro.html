Patient Case: Mateo on Oxygen

After the smoke week (Chapter 7), Mateo Herrera's pulse oximeter read SpO2 91% (Chapter 3), and the team in the emergency department admitted him overnight. A respiratory therapist fitted a nasal cannula, two soft prongs that sit just inside the nostrils, and set the flowmeter to 2 L/min of oxygen, as ordered.

Every part of that plan is a gas calculation. Room air is about 21% oxygen, so at sea level the oxygen in each breath pushes with a partial pressure of about 160 mmHg; the extra oxygen from the cannula raises it. When Mateo is wheeled down the hall for a chest X-ray, he goes on a small portable cylinder in which oxygen is squeezed to more than 100 times atmospheric pressure, and how long that cylinder lasts at 2 L/min follows from Boyle's law. Even the breath itself is gas physics: his diaphragm enlarges his chest, the pressure in his lungs drops, and air rushes in.

In this chapter you will learn why gases behave so predictably, how pressure is measured, and how pressure, volume, temperature, and moles are tied together in the gas laws. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Use kinetic molecular theory to explain the properties of gases (Section 10.1).
  • Describe how pressure is measured and convert among atm, mmHg, torr, kPa, and psi (Section 10.2).
  • Use Boyle's, Charles's, Gay-Lussac's, Avogadro's, and the combined gas law (Section 10.3).
  • Use the ideal gas law to find P, V, n, or T, and find the molar mass or density of a gas (Section 10.4).
  • Do stoichiometry with gas volumes, at STP and at other conditions (Section 10.5).
  • Use Dalton's law of partial pressures, including gases collected over water (Section 10.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Respiratory therapists, anesthesia teams, divers, and engineers work with gas laws every day: oxygen delivery, ventilators, anesthetic gas mixtures, scuba tanks, tires, and airbags. Your own breathing, the exchange of O2 and CO2 in your lungs, and the bubbles in a soda all follow the same few rules. This chapter builds on the mole (Chapter 5), stoichiometry (Chapter 8), and temperature and vapor pressure (Chapter 9), and it prepares you for solutions (Chapter 11) and equilibrium (Chapter 13).
