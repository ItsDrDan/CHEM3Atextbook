Learning Objectives

  • Explain gas behavior with kinetic molecular theory and convert among pressure units.
  • Use the simple gas laws, the combined gas law, and the ideal gas law.
  • Find molar mass and density of a gas, and do gas stoichiometry at STP and other conditions.
  • Use Dalton's law, mole fractions, and the water-vapor correction.

How to Use This Guide

Read the Key Ideas after the sections, use the video moments for anything still unclear, work the examples with the answers covered, and then go to the Practice Problems. Almost every gas problem starts with the same two questions: is the gas changing (combined gas law) or not (PV = nRT), and are all the units ready (K, L, atm, mol)? Key Ideas

1. Kinetic molecular theory

Tiny, far-apart particles in constant random motion, with elastic collisions and no attractions. Collisions with the walls cause pressure; average kinetic energy is proportional to Kelvin temperature. (Section 10.1)

2. Pressure units

1 atm = 760 mmHg = 760 torr = 101.325 kPa = 14.7 psi. Air pressure drops with altitude. (Section 10.2)

3. The gas laws

Boyle: P1V1 = P2V2 (inverse). Charles, Gay-Lussac, and Avogadro: direct. Combined: P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2. Temperatures in kelvins. (Section 10.3)

4. The ideal gas law

PV = nRT with R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K). At STP (273 K, 1 atm), 1 mol = 22.4 L. M = m/n; d = m/V. (Section 10.4)

5. Gas stoichiometry

Given → moles → mole ratio → moles → liters (22.4 L/mol at STP, otherwise V = nRT/P). At the same T and P, coefficients are volume ratios. (Section 10.5)

6. Dalton's law

Ptotal = sum of partial pressures; Pi = XiPtotal. Over water, Pgas = Patm − PH2O. (Section 10.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): A diver's bubble

Problem. A scuba diver breathes out a 15.0 mL bubble at a depth where the pressure is 3.25 atm and the water is 12.0 °C. What is the bubble's volume when it reaches the surface, where the pressure is 1.00 atm and the water is 22.0 °C?

Solution. The gas changes, so use the combined gas law. T1 = 285.15 K; T2 = 295.15 K.

\( V_2 = V_1 \times \dfrac{P_1}{P_2} \times \dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = 15.0\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{3.25\ \text{atm}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} \times \dfrac{295.15\ \text{K}}{285.15\ \text{K}} = 50.5\ \text{mL} \)

Check. Lower pressure and higher temperature both make the bubble bigger. The same expansion happens to air in a diver's lungs on the way up, which is why divers must breathe out as they ascend.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Oxygen collected over water

Problem. Heating 1.225 g of KClO3 (122.55 g/mol) decomposes it completely:

2 KClO3(s) → 2 KCl(s) + 3 O2(g)

The O2 is collected over water at 25.0 °C when the room pressure is 758 mmHg (water's vapor pressure at 25.0 °C is 23.8 mmHg). What volume of gas is collected?

Solution. Moles of O2: 1.225 ÷ 122.55 × 3/2 = 0.01499 mol. Pressure of the O2: 758 − 23.8 = 734 mmHg = 0.9658 atm.

\( V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{(0.01499\ \text{mol})(0.082058)(298.15\ \text{K})}{0.9658\ \text{atm}} = 0.380\ \text{L} \)

Check. Only the O2's partial pressure goes with the O2's moles. The collected volume (380 mL) holds both the O2 and the water vapor.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Oxygen at a Sierra campground

Problem. A barometer at a high Sierra campground reads 589 mmHg. Dry air is 20.9% O2. What is the partial pressure of O2, and how does it compare with 159 mmHg at sea level?

Solution.

\( P_{\text{O}_2} = (0.209)(589\ \text{mmHg}) = 123\ \text{mmHg} \)

That is about 23% lower than at sea level.

Check. The percentage of oxygen is the same at every altitude; only the total pressure, and so each partial pressure, drops. Each breath delivers fewer O2 molecules.

Common Mistakes

  • Using °C instead of K in any gas law.
  • Writing Boyle's law as a ratio, or Charles's law as a product; predict the direction first.
  • Using mL, mmHg, or grams with R = 0.082058 L·atm/(mol·K).
  • Using 22.4 L/mol when the gas is not at STP.
  • Forgetting to subtract water vapor for a gas collected over water.
  • Skipping the mole ratio in gas stoichiometry.
