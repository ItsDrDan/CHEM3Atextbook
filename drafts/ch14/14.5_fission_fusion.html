  • Describe nuclear fusion in the Sun and compare fusion with fission.

On the coast of San Luis Obispo County, about a three-hour drive from Fresno, a single power plant supplies close to a tenth of the electricity used in California, without burning any fuel. Ninety-three million miles away, the Sun has been shining for about 4.6 billion years without burning anything either. Both run on nuclear reactions: the power plant splits heavy nuclei, and the Sun joins light ones. In both, a little mass disappears and a great deal of energy appears.

Nuclear Fission

In 1938, German chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with neutrons and were surprised to find barium, an element about half as heavy, among the products. Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained what had happened: the uranium nucleus had split in two. They called it nuclear fission, the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two smaller nuclei.

When uranium-235 absorbs a slow neutron, it becomes very unstable and splits into two medium-sized nuclei, two or three neutrons, and a large amount of energy. One typical split is:

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + \text{energy} \)

A uranium-235 nucleus can split in many different ways, giving dozens of different pairs of fragments, but every fission equation balances by the same rules as in Section 14.2. The coefficient 3 in front of the neutron means 3 neutrons, so it counts 3 × 1 = 3 toward the mass numbers.

Challenge: Balancing Fission and Fusion Equations

In CHEM 3A, the main nuclear-equation skills are the simple ones from Section 14.2: alpha, beta, positron, and gamma decay, electron capture, and bombardment. On this page, the important ideas are what fission and fusion are, chain reactions, and how a reactor works. Completing a full fission or fusion equation, as in the examples and exercises marked Challenge, uses exactly the same top-and-bottom rule, so try them once the main skills feel easy.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\) (Challenge): Find the missing fragment

Problem. Complete the fission equation:

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{143}_{54}\text{Xe} + {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Solution. Left side: A = 1 + 235 = 236; Z = 0 + 92 = 92. Right side so far: A = 143 + 3 = 146; Z = 54 + 0 = 54. Missing: A = 236 − 146 = 90; Z = 92 − 54 = 38. Element 38 is strontium: \( {}^{90}_{38}\text{Sr} \).

Check. 1 + 235 = 143 + 90 + 3 = 236, and 92 = 54 + 38. Strontium-90 is one of the long-lived fission products in nuclear waste.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\) (Challenge): How many neutrons?

Problem. How many neutrons are released in this fission?

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{137}_{55}\text{Cs} + {}^{96}_{37}\text{Rb} + x\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Solution. The atomic numbers already balance (55 + 37 = 92). Mass numbers: 236 = 137 + 96 + x, so x = 236 − 233 = 3.

Check. Fission of uranium-235 typically releases 2 or 3 neutrons.

Where does the energy come from? If you add up the masses of the products very precisely, they are slightly less than the masses of the reactants. That small amount of missing mass has been converted to energy, according to Albert Einstein's equation E = mc2 (energy equals mass times the speed of light squared). Because the speed of light is so large, a tiny mass becomes an enormous energy: fissioning a gram of uranium-235 releases millions of times more energy than burning a gram of coal or natural gas.

Chain Reactions and Critical Mass

Each fission is started by one neutron and releases two or three more. If those neutrons strike other uranium-235 nuclei, they cause more fissions, which release still more neutrons. This self-sustaining process is a chain reaction. In the figure below, 1 neutron leads to 3, then 9, then 27, and the number keeps multiplying.

In a small piece of uranium, most neutrons escape through the surface before they hit another nucleus, and the chain dies out. The critical mass is the smallest amount of fissionable material that can keep a chain reaction going.

  • In an atomic bomb, two or more pieces that are each below the critical mass are slammed together into a supercritical mass, and the chain reaction multiplies uncontrollably in a fraction of a second. The bombs used in 1945 at the end of World War II, developed by the Manhattan Project, used uranium-235 and plutonium-239.
  • In a nuclear reactor, the chain reaction is kept steady: on average exactly one neutron from each fission goes on to cause another fission.

Nuclear Power

A nuclear power plant is a steam power plant whose heat comes from fission instead of burning fuel. The main parts are:

  • Fuel rods: long metal tubes filled with pellets of uranium oxide in which a few percent of the uranium is uranium-235. (Natural uranium is less than 1% uranium-235; this low enrichment is why a reactor cannot explode like a bomb.)
  • Control rods: rods made of neutron-absorbing elements such as boron or cadmium. Lowering them into the core absorbs neutrons and slows or stops the chain reaction; raising them speeds it up.
  • Moderator and coolant: in most reactors, water flows through the core. It slows the neutrons so U-235 can capture them (the moderator), and it carries the heat away (the coolant).
  • Steam generator, turbine, and generator: the hot water boils water in a separate loop; the steam spins a turbine connected to an electric generator.
  • Condenser and cooling water: the used steam is cooled back to liquid water by ocean, river, or cooling-tower water and returns to be boiled again.
  • Containment building: a thick steel-and-concrete structure around the reactor that keeps radioactive material inside if something goes wrong.

Nuclear power plants release no carbon dioxide while they run, and a small amount of fuel lasts a long time. Their main drawbacks are cost, the risk of serious accidents (Chernobyl in Ukraine in 1986 and Fukushima Daiichi in Japan in 2011), and nuclear waste. Spent fuel rods contain fission products such as strontium-90 and cesium-137 and heavier elements such as plutonium, some of which stay dangerously radioactive for thousands of years. Spent fuel is first cooled for years in deep pools of water and then stored in steel-and-concrete "dry casks." The United States does not yet have a permanent underground storage site, so most spent fuel is stored at the power plants where it was used.

Major Connection: Engineering and Diablo Canyon

Diablo Canyon Power Plant, operated by Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) in San Luis Obispo County, is California's last operating nuclear plant. In 2024 it produced 18,379 gigawatt-hours of electricity, 8.50% of the electricity generated in California and 9.92% of the state's total power mix, according to the California Energy Commission; natural gas supplied 34.01% of the mix and solar 21.30%. A 2022 state law (SB 846) set the path for keeping the plant running through 2030 instead of closing it in 2025. Keeping a reactor safe involves nuclear, mechanical, electrical, civil, and materials engineers.

Sources: California Energy Commission, "2024 Total System Electric Generation" and "CEC Determines Diablo Canyon Power Plant Needed to Support Grid Reliability" (2023) (energy.ca.gov).

Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus. Like fission, it converts a little mass into a great deal of energy; per gram of fuel, fusion releases even more energy than fission. Fusion powers the Sun and every other star. In the Sun's core, at about 15 million °C, hydrogen nuclei fuse in a series of steps whose overall result is:

\( 4\,{}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + 2\,{}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} + \text{energy} \)

The fusion reaction researchers are trying to use on Earth joins two heavier isotopes of hydrogen, deuterium (hydrogen-2) and tritium (hydrogen-3):

\( {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + \text{energy} \)

Fusion is hard because both nuclei are positive and repel each other strongly. To make them collide hard enough to fuse, the fuel must be heated to around 100 million °C and held together, which no material container can do. In December 2022, the National Ignition Facility at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in Livermore, California, used powerful lasers to make a tiny capsule of fuel release more fusion energy than the laser energy delivered to it, a first. A practical fusion power plant is still years away.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Fission or fusion?

Problem. Classify each reaction as fission or fusion. (a) \( {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{3}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)   (b) \( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{239}_{94}\text{Pu} \rightarrow {}^{134}_{54}\text{Xe} + {}^{103}_{40}\text{Zr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Solution. (a) Two light nuclei join into a heavier one: fusion. (b) A neutron splits a heavy nucleus into two medium-sized ones: fission.

Check. Both are balanced: (a) 2 + 2 = 3 + 1 and 1 + 1 = 2 + 0; (b) 1 + 239 = 134 + 103 + 3 = 240 and 94 = 54 + 40.

Professor Tip

Fission sounds like "fissure," a crack: a big nucleus splits apart, usually after being hit by a neutron. Fusion sounds like "fuse": small nuclei join together, usually hydrogen isotopes, and it needs star-like temperatures. In both, the products have a little less mass than the reactants, and that missing mass is the energy (E = mc2). If you are given a challenge problem that asks you to balance a fission or fusion equation, use the same top-and-bottom rule as any nuclear equation.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the coefficient on the neutrons (challenge fission equations): 3 \( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \) adds 3 to the mass-number total.
  • Forgetting the neutron on the left (challenge fission equations): fission of uranium-235 starts when it absorbs a neutron.
  • Mixing up fission and fusion: fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion joins light nuclei.
  • Thinking control rods are fuel: control rods absorb neutrons to slow the reaction; the fuel rods hold the uranium.

Summary

  • Fission: a neutron splits uranium-235 into two smaller nuclei, 2–3 neutrons, and energy. Balancing a full fission or fusion equation is a challenge skill, done like any nuclear equation; the main equation skills are simple decay, electron capture, and bombardment (Section 14.2).
  • The released neutrons can cause a chain reaction; the critical mass is the least fuel that sustains it. Bombs let it run away; reactors keep it steady with control rods.
  • A nuclear power plant uses fission heat to make steam that spins a turbine and generator; spent fuel is long-lived radioactive waste.
  • Fusion joins light nuclei (hydrogen to helium in the Sun) and needs extremely high temperatures; in both fission and fusion, a little mass becomes energy (E = mc2).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\) (Challenge)

Complete the fission equation: \( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{139}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Hint

Challenge item: use the top-and-bottom rule. The left side totals 1 + 235 on top; remember the 3 in front of the neutrons counts 3 on top. Then find the element from Z.

Answer

A: 236 − 139 − 3 = 94. Z: 92 − 56 = 36, krypton: \( {}^{94}_{36}\text{Kr} \).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A reactor operator needs to slow the chain reaction. Should the control rods be raised or lowered? Explain.

Hint

What do control rods absorb? Fewer of those particles means fewer new fissions.

Answer

Lowered further into the core: the rods absorb neutrons, so fewer neutrons are left to cause new fissions.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Why does fusion need temperatures of millions of degrees, while fission can start at room temperature?

Hint

Fusion joins two nuclei; what charge do nuclei carry, and what happens when like charges are pushed together? What charge does the neutron that starts fission have?

Answer

Fusion must push two positive nuclei together against their strong repulsion, which takes extremely fast (hot) nuclei. Fission is started by a neutron, which has no charge and is not repelled by the nucleus.
