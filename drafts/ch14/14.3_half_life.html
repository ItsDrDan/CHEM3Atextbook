Learning Objectives

  • Define half-life and read a decay curve.
  • Find the number of half-lives from the elapsed time and the half-life.
  • Calculate the amount (or activity) remaining after a whole number of half-lives.
  • Work backward to find the elapsed time, the half-life, or the starting amount.
  • Set up a half-life problem with a half-life equality as a conversion factor, and solve percent-only problems with an arrow map.

When Mateo's lung scan is done, the technetium-99m in his body does not have to be removed. It simply decays, half of it every 6 hours, until almost none is left. Choosing a tracer for a scan means choosing a half-life: long enough to finish the imaging, short enough that the radioactivity disappears soon afterward. This section shows how to calculate how much of a radioisotope is left.

What Half-Life Means

The half-life (t1/2) of a radioisotope is the time it takes for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. After one half-life, 50% is left; after a second half-life, half of that, or 25%, is left; and so on. Each nucleus decays at a random moment, but a sample contains so many nuclei that the fraction decaying in each half-life is extremely predictable. The half-life does not depend on how much of the isotope you start with, on temperature, or on what compound the atom is in.

Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years. Isotopes used inside the body for diagnosis have short half-lives, so the patient's dose ends quickly. The table below lists some radioisotopes and their half-lives.

Calculating with Whole Numbers of Half-Lives

In this course, every half-life problem uses a whole number of half-lives (1, 2, 3, …). Two relationships do all the work:

\( n = \dfrac{\text{time elapsed}}{\text{half-life}} \qquad\qquad \text{amount remaining} = \text{initial amount} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n} \)

Multiplying by \( \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n} \) is the same as dividing by 2 a total of n times, so you can also make a table and halve the amount once per half-life. The "amount" can be a mass (mg, g), an activity (mCi, Bq), or a percent; the math is the same. The number of half-lives is an exact count, so the answer keeps the significant figures of the starting amount.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Mateo's tracer after 12 hours

Problem. Suppose the technetium-99m in Mateo's scan had an activity of 4.00 mCi when it was given. The half-life of technetium-99m is 6.0 hours. What activity is left 12 hours later, and what fraction is that?

Solution. Number of half-lives:

\( n = \dfrac{12\;\text{h}}{6.0\;\text{h}} = 2 \qquad 4.00\;\text{mCi} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2} = 1.00\;\text{mCi} \)

As a table:

Check. Two half-lives leave one-fourth (25%), the same fraction for any starting amount. (The real amount left in his body is even smaller, because some of the tracer also leaves through the kidneys. Context only.)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Iodine-131 after 24.0 days

Problem. A sample contains 80.0 mg of iodine-131 (half-life 8.0 days). How many milligrams remain after 24.0 days?

Solution.

\( n = \dfrac{24.0\;\text{days}}{8.0\;\text{days}} = 3 \qquad 80.0\;\text{mg} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{3} = 80.0\;\text{mg} \times \tfrac{1}{8} = 10.0\;\text{mg} \)

80.0 mg → 40.0 mg → 20.0 mg → 10.0 mg

(100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5%; each arrow is 8.0 days)

Check. Three arrows, three half-lives, and the amount was halved three times. Writing the percent under each amount, as in the second line, is a good habit: it works the same way when a problem gives only percents (Example 7).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): How long until only a little is left?

Problem. A dose of fluorine-18 (half-life 110 minutes) has an activity of 40.0 mCi when it is prepared. How long will it take for the activity to fall to 5.00 mCi?

Solution. Halve until you reach 5.00 mCi and count the steps:

40.0 → 20.0 → 10.0 → 5.00 mCi   (3 half-lives)

\( \text{time} = n \times t_{1/2} = 3 \times 110\;\text{min} = 330\;\text{min}\;\;(5.5\;\text{h}) \)

Check. 5.00 is one-eighth of 40.0, and \( \tfrac{1}{8} = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^3 \). This is why a PET center must use fluorine-18 within a few hours of making it.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Finding the half-life from data

Problem. A 200. mg sample of a radioisotope decays to 25.0 mg in 30.0 hours. What is its half-life?

Solution. 200. → 100. → 50.0 → 25.0 mg is 3 half-lives.

\( t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\text{time elapsed}}{n} = \dfrac{30.0\;\text{h}}{3} = 10.0\;\text{h} \)

Check. Three half-lives of 10.0 h each add up to 30.0 h.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Working backward to the starting amount

Problem. Twenty-four hours after a technetium-99m tracer was prepared, 0.500 mg of technetium-99m remains. How much was there at the start? (Half-life 6.0 h.)

Solution. n = 24.0 h ÷ 6.0 h = 4. Going backward in time, double once per half-life:

\( 0.500\;\text{mg} \times 2^{4} = 0.500\;\text{mg} \times 16 = 8.00\;\text{mg} \)

Check. Forward from 8.00 mg: 4.00, 2.00, 1.00, 0.500 mg in four half-lives.

Setting Up a Half-Life Problem Step by Step

A half-life can be written as an equality, just like the conversion factors in Chapter 1. For strontium-90, 1 half-life = 28.8 years, so you can write

\( \dfrac{1\;\text{half-life}}{28.8\;\text{years}} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \dfrac{28.8\;\text{years}}{1\;\text{half-life}} \)

and let the units tell you which one to use. A four-step plan keeps longer problems organized: (1) write the known and unknown quantities; (2) plan the path from one to the other; (3) write the half-life equality and any other conversions; (4) set up and solve.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Strontium-90, step by step

Problem. Strontium-90, a product of nuclear fission, has a half-life of 28.8 years. A soil sample contained 40.0 mg of strontium-90. How many milligrams remain after 115.2 years?

Solution.

  1. Known: 40.0 mg Sr-90 at the start; 115.2 years elapsed. Unknown: mg Sr-90 remaining.
  2. Plan: years → number of half-lives → mg remaining.
  3. Equality: 1 half-life = 28.8 years.
  4. Set up and solve:

\( 115.2\;\text{years} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{half-life}}{28.8\;\text{years}} = 4\;\text{half-lives} \qquad 40.0\;\text{mg} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{4} = 2.50\;\text{mg} \)

40.0 mg → 20.0 → 10.0 → 5.00 → 2.50 mg

Check. Years cancel and leave half-lives, so the factor is right side up. Four arrows, four half-lives, one-sixteenth of the start. Strontium behaves like calcium in the body and settles in bone, which is why this long-lived isotope in fallout is a health concern.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): Only a percent is given

Problem. The tritium (hydrogen-3, half-life 12.3 years) in a glowing exit sign decays until only 6.25% of the original amount is left. How long does that take?

Solution. No mass is given, so map out the percents and count the arrows:

100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% → 6.25%   (4 arrows = 4 half-lives)

\( 4\;\text{half-lives} \times \dfrac{12.3\;\text{years}}{1\;\text{half-life}} = 49.2\;\text{years} \)

Check. (1/2)4 = 1/16 = 0.0625 = 6.25%. Starting from 100% works for any sample size, because the fraction left after n half-lives does not depend on how much you start with. (Exit signs are labeled with a replacement date long before this, because they dim as the tritium decays.)

Example \(\PageIndex{8}\): A brachytherapy source

Problem. Some cancer centers treat tumors with a machine that briefly moves a tiny, sealed iridium-192 source into or next to the tumor (high-dose-rate brachytherapy). Iridium-192 has a half-life of 74 days. A new source has an activity of 10.0 Ci. What is its activity after 148 days?

Solution.

\( 148\;\text{days} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{half-life}}{74\;\text{days}} = 2\;\text{half-lives} \qquad 10.0\;\text{Ci} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2} = 2.50\;\text{Ci} \)

Check. Two half-lives leave one-fourth. As the source weakens, each treatment has to run longer to deliver the same dose, which is why centers replace iridium-192 sources every few months. (Context only.)

Professor Tip

Before you multiply anything, find n. If the problem gives the time, divide by the half-life. If it gives the starting and ending amounts, halve the starting amount step by step and count the steps. Then sanity-check the direction: going forward in time the amount must get smaller; going backward to find a starting amount, it must get bigger. A table with columns for time, half-lives, and amount is the safest way to show your work. Or draw an arrow map: write the amounts in a row with an arrow between each pair, label every arrow with the half-life (for iodine-131, "8.0 days"), and write the percent remaining under each amount. Then count the arrows.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

A ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan compares ventilation (where inhaled air reaches in the lungs) with perfusion (where blood flows through the lung's capillaries). For the ventilation images, the patient breathes a radioactive gas or an aerosol (a fine mist), commonly made with technetium-99m, through a mouthpiece; respiratory therapists are often the ones who coach patients through the breathing. For the perfusion images, a technetium-99m tracer of tiny albumin particles is injected into a vein, and the particles briefly lodge in the small blood vessels of the lungs. A gamma camera images both. An area that gets air but no blood flow (a "mismatch") suggests a pulmonary embolism, a clot blocking a lung artery. Technetium-99m works well here because its gamma rays leave the body to reach the camera, and its 6-hour half-life leaves time to image, then decays quickly. Lung disease such as asthma can change the ventilation images, which is one reason the scan is read together with the patient's history. (Context only.)

Source: Derenoncourt P-R, et al., "Ventilation-Perfusion Scan: A Primer for Practicing Radiologists," RadioGraphics 41 (2021): 2047–2070.

Common Mistakes

  • Subtracting half the original amount each time: each half-life halves what is left (80 → 40 → 20), not the original (80 → 40 → 0).
  • Using the time as n: n is time ÷ half-life, with matching units (hours with hours, days with days).
  • Counting the starting amount as a half-life: in 80 → 40 → 20 → 10, count the arrows (3), not the numbers (4).
  • Halving when working backward: to find a starting amount, double once per half-life.
  • Flipping the half-life equality: to turn years into half-lives, years go on the bottom (1 half-life / 28.8 years) so they cancel.

Summary

  • Half-life: the time for half of a radioisotope's nuclei to decay; it is constant for each isotope.
  • n = time ÷ half-life; amount remaining = initial × (1/2)n; for this course n is always a whole number.
  • Time = n × half-life; half-life = time ÷ n; starting amount = remaining × 2n. The half-life is an equality (1 half-life = t1/2) that works as a conversion factor.
  • When only a percent is given, map 100% → 50% → 25% → … and count the arrows.
  • Diagnostic tracers such as technetium-99m (6.0 h) and fluorine-18 (110 min) have short half-lives, so the radioactivity is gone soon after the scan.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A patient receives 12.0 mCi of technetium-99m (half-life 6.0 h). What activity remains after 18 hours?

Hint

Find the number of half-lives first: time ÷ half-life. Then halve the activity once for each half-life (an arrow map works well).

Answer

n = 18 ÷ 6.0 = 3. 12.0 → 6.00 → 3.00 → 1.50 mCi.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

How many days does it take for 64.0 mg of iodine-131 (half-life 8.0 days) to decay to 4.00 mg?

Hint

Draw an arrow map from the starting mass, halving each time until you reach 4.00 mg. Count the arrows, then multiply by the half-life.

Answer

64.0 → 32.0 → 16.0 → 8.00 → 4.00 mg is 4 half-lives; 4 × 8.0 days = 32 days.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A sample of cobalt-60 (half-life 5.27 years) has a mass of 2.40 g. What mass of cobalt-60 remains after 15.81 years?

Hint

Divide the time by the half-life to get the number of half-lives (a whole number), then multiply the mass by (1/2) that many times.

Answer

n = 15.81 ÷ 5.27 = 3. 2.40 g × (1/2)3 = 0.300 g.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

What percent of a fluorine-18 sample is left after 550 minutes? (Half-life 110 min.)

Hint

Count half-lives (time ÷ half-life), then start an arrow map at 100% and halve once per half-life.

Answer

n = 550 ÷ 110 = 5. (1/2)5 = 1/32 = 3.125% (about 3.1%).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Iodine-123 (half-life 13 hours) is used to image the thyroid. A dose has an activity of 8.00 mCi when it is prepared. Use the half-life as a conversion factor to find the number of half-lives in 39 hours, and then find the activity left.

Hint

Write the half-life as an equality, 1 half-life = 13 h, and use it as a conversion factor on 39 h. Then halve 8.00 mCi once per half-life.

Answer

39 h × (1 half-life / 13 h) = 3 half-lives. 8.00 → 4.00 → 2.00 → 1.00 mCi.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

A sample of phosphorus-32 (half-life 14.3 days) used to label DNA has decayed to 12.5% of its original activity. How many days have passed?

Hint

Make a percent arrow map from the whole sample, halving until you reach 12.5%. Count the arrows and multiply by the half-life.

Answer

100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% is 3 half-lives. 3 × 14.3 days = 42.9 days.
