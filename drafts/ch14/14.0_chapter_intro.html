Patient Case: Mateo's Lung Scan

A week after a long drive, Mateo Herrera, the 34-year-old irrigation technician with asthma you met in earlier chapters, notices that he is short of breath in a way that feels different from his usual asthma. His inhaler helps less than it should, and one calf is sore. His doctor worries about a pulmonary embolism, a blood clot that has traveled to the lungs and blocks blood flow to part of them. To check, she orders a ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan: images that compare where air reaches in his lungs (ventilation) with where blood reaches (perfusion).

The scan uses a tiny amount of a radioactive tracer made with technetium-99m. The tracer gives off gamma rays that pass out of his body to a camera that maps where the tracer went. Technetium-99m has a half-life of about 6 hours: every 6 hours, half of whatever is left decays. Twelve hours after his scan is two half-lives, so only one-fourth of the tracer's technetium-99m is still there. You will do that calculation, and see why this tracer is chosen, in Section 14.3. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not medical advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Describe the discovery of radioactivity, the units used for radiation, and how radiation is detected (Section 14.1).
  • Write and balance nuclear equations for alpha, beta, positron, and gamma decay, and compare the penetrating and ionizing power of each (Section 14.2).
  • Calculate the amount of a radioisotope remaining after a whole number of half-lives, and the time, half-life, or starting amount (Section 14.3).
  • Explain radiocarbon dating and use whole numbers of carbon-14 half-lives to estimate ages (Section 14.4).
  • Describe nuclear fission, chain reactions, nuclear power, and nuclear fusion in the Sun (Section 14.5).
  • Explain how ionizing radiation affects living tissue and how radioisotopes are used in diagnosis, therapy, and sterilization (Section 14.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Every other chapter in this book has been about electrons: how atoms gain, lose, and share them. This chapter is about the nucleus. When a nucleus changes, the element itself can change, and the energy released is millions of times larger than in a chemical reaction. That energy powers the Sun and nuclear power plants, and in small, carefully measured amounts it lets health care workers see inside the body and treat disease. Radiologic technologists, nuclear medicine technologists, dental hygienists, respiratory therapists, and nurses all work with radiation or with patients who have received it. This chapter builds on atomic structure and isotope symbols (Chapter 2) and on light and electromagnetic radiation (Chapter 3).
