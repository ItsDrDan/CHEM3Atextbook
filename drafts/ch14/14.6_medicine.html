Learning Objectives

  • Explain how ionizing radiation damages cells and why rapidly dividing cells are most sensitive.
  • Describe the effects of low and high radiation doses, the sources of background radiation, and the hazard of radon.
  • Use time, distance, and shielding to reduce a radiation dose.
  • Describe how radioisotopes are used in diagnosis (technetium-99m, PET with fluorine-18), therapy (iodine-131, external beam), and sterilization (gamma rays), and distinguish X-rays from gamma rays.

The same property that makes radiation dangerous makes it useful in medicine. Ionizing radiation damages cells, so it must be handled carefully, and it can be aimed at cancer cells to destroy them. It passes through the body, so a camera outside can see a tracer inside. This section brings together everything in the chapter (decay types, penetrating power, and half-life) to explain how radiation affects living things and how health care puts it to work.

How Radiation Affects Living Tissue

When ionizing radiation passes through a cell, it knocks electrons off the molecules in its path. Most of a cell is water, so the most common first step is the ionization of water:

\( \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\ \text{radiation}\ } \text{H}_2\text{O}^{+} + \text{e}^{-} \)

The H2O+ ion quickly breaks apart into very reactive fragments called free radicals (molecules with an unpaired electron, such as the hydroxyl radical, ·OH). Radiation and free radicals can break bonds in proteins, membranes, and, most importantly, DNA. A cell can often repair the damage. If it cannot, the cell may die, or it may survive with a mutation that years later contributes to cancer.

Cells that divide rapidly are the most sensitive, because damaged DNA is copied before it can be repaired. These include cells in the bone marrow (which makes blood cells), the lining of the intestines, the skin, hair follicles, the reproductive organs, and a developing fetus. Cancer cells also divide rapidly, which is why radiation can be used to treat cancer.

Biology Connection: Radiation and DNA

DNA is a double helix: two strands held together by hydrogen bonds between base pairs (Chapter 9). Radiation can break one strand or, more seriously, both strands at once. Cells have repair enzymes that splice single-strand breaks back together using the other strand as a template, but double-strand breaks are harder to repair correctly. A cell with too much damage usually triggers its own programmed death (apoptosis), which protects the body. Mistakes in repair can change genes that control cell division, the first step toward cancer. This is also why pregnant patients are asked before any imaging: the rapidly dividing cells of an embryo are especially sensitive.

Background Radiation and Radon

Everyone is exposed to background radiation all the time: cosmic rays from space (more at high altitude and on airplane flights), radioactive elements in rocks, soil, and building materials, potassium-40 and carbon-14 in food and in our own bodies (Section 14.2), and, the largest natural source, radon gas. Radon-222 forms underground from the decay of uranium-238 through radium-226 (Example 1 in Section 14.2). It is a gas, so it can seep out of the soil and into buildings through cracks in foundations. Radon and its solid decay products are alpha emitters; when they are inhaled, they lodge in the lungs and deliver their highly ionizing alpha particles directly to lung tissue. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) estimates that radon causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths a year in the United States, making it the leading cause of lung cancer after smoking. The only way to know a home's radon level is to test it.

Valley Connection: Radon in Tulare County Homes

Radon concentrations are reported in picocuries per liter (pCi/L) of air. One picocurie is 10−12 curie, or 0.037 decays per second, so the EPA's recommended action level of 4.0 pCi/L means about 0.15 radon nuclei decaying every second in each liter of indoor air. Homes at or above that level should be fixed, usually with a fan and pipe that vent soil gas from under the foundation.

Between December 2009 and April 2010, the California Department of Public Health (CDPH) Indoor Radon Program tested 498 homes in Tulare County. Results ranged from 0.5 to 33.0 pCi/L. The California Geological Survey (CGS) combined those results with measurements of uranium in soil and rock and with geologic maps to draw a radon potential map of western Tulare County (Special Report 238, 2016). Geologic units where 20% or more of tested homes reached 4.0 pCi/L were mapped as high radon potential; they cover 33.3% of western Tulare County, and CGS estimated that about 95,000 people there live in homes at or above 4.0 pCi/L. In the foothill and mountain communities to the east, including Three Rivers and Springville, 6 of 31 homes tested (19.4%) were at or above 4.0 pCi/L. CGS has also mapped radon potential for Sierra foothill counties such as Amador, Calaveras, Tuolumne, and western El Dorado, Placer, and Nevada Counties. The maps are advisory: they show where testing matters most, but every zone contains some high and some low homes, so the only way to know is to test.

Sources: California Geological Survey, Special Report 238, "Radon Potential in Western Tulare County, California" (2016) and "Radon" (conservation.ca.gov); California Department of Public Health, "Radon in California" (cdph.ca.gov).

Time, Distance, and Shielding

Anyone who works near radiation protects themselves, and their patients, with three tools:

  • Time: the dose is proportional to the time spent near the source. Half the time, half the dose.
  • Distance: radiation spreads out as it travels, so the dose rate falls quickly with distance. From a small source, doubling the distance cuts the dose rate to one-fourth (the inverse-square law); tripling it cuts the dose rate to one-ninth.
  • Shielding: a barrier matched to the radiation (Section 14.2): lead aprons, lead-glass windows, lead "pigs" (containers) and syringe shields for gamma emitters; plastic or aluminum for beta emitters.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Stepping back and spending less time

Problem. The dose rate 1.0 m from a gamma source is 36 μSv per hour. (a) What is the dose rate 2.0 m away? (b) What dose does a worker receive by standing 2.0 m away for 0.50 hour?

Solution. (a) Twice the distance gives one-fourth the dose rate:

\( 36\ \mu\text{Sv/h} \times \tfrac{1}{4} = 9.0\ \mu\text{Sv/h} \)

(b) Dose = dose rate × time:

\( 9.0\ \tfrac{\mu\text{Sv}}{\text{h}} \times 0.50\ \text{h} = 4.5\ \mu\text{Sv} \)

Check. Distance cut the dose rate by 4 and time cut the dose in half, so the dose is one-eighth of the 36 μSv a worker would get standing 1.0 m away for a full hour.

Radioisotopes in Diagnosis

A tracer is a radioisotope, often attached to a molecule that the body handles in a known way, given in a tiny amount so that its location can be imaged. A good diagnostic tracer:

  • gives off gamma rays (or positrons that produce gamma rays), which pass out of the body to the camera; alpha and beta particles would be stopped inside the body and add dose without making an image;
  • has a short half-life (hours, not years), so the activity is gone soon after the scan (Section 14.3);
  • goes to the organ of interest because of its chemistry.

Technetium-99m (half-life 6.0 h) is used in most nuclear medicine scans. It is attached to different carrier molecules to image bones, the heart muscle, the kidneys, and, in Mateo's V/Q scan, blood flow and airflow in the lungs. Iodine-123 is used to image the thyroid.

In positron emission tomography (PET), the tracer is usually fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), a glucose molecule in which one −OH group is replaced by a fluorine-18 atom. Cells take up FDG as if it were glucose. Each fluorine-18 nucleus emits a positron (Section 14.2), which travels a millimeter or so before meeting an electron. The two annihilate, and their mass becomes two gamma rays that fly off in opposite directions. A ring of detectors around the patient catches both gamma rays at the same instant and works out where they came from.

Biology Connection: Why FDG Lights Up Active Tissue

Glucose is the main fuel for cellular respiration, and tissues that are working hard take up more of it through glucose transporter proteins in their membranes. Many cancers, the brain, and actively contracting heart muscle use glucose at high rates. FDG enters cells through the same transporters, and the first enzyme of glycolysis attaches a phosphate to it, as it does to glucose. But the altered molecule cannot continue through glycolysis and cannot easily leave the cell, so it is trapped. The PET image is therefore a map of glucose use: bright spots show tissue with high metabolic activity, such as a tumor. That is also why patients fast before a PET scan, so blood glucose does not compete with the tracer.

Radioisotopes in Therapy

Treatment uses the opposite strategy from imaging: the goal is to deliver as much ionizing radiation as possible to diseased cells while sparing healthy ones.

  • Iodine-131 for the thyroid. The thyroid gland uses iodine to make its hormones (thyroxine, T4, and triiodothyronine, T3), so it actively collects iodide from the blood. A patient who swallows iodine-131 concentrates it in the thyroid, where its beta particles, which travel less than about 2 millimeters in tissue, destroy overactive thyroid tissue (hyperthyroidism, as in Graves' disease) or remaining thyroid cancer cells. Its gamma rays also allow imaging.
  • External beam radiation therapy. Beams of high-energy X-rays from a machine called a linear accelerator, or gamma rays from cobalt-60 sources, are aimed at a tumor from several directions so the beams overlap at the tumor. "Gamma Knife" radiosurgery focuses about 200 cobalt-60 beams on a small target in the brain.
  • Brachytherapy. Small sealed sources, such as iodine-125 seeds or iridium-192, are placed in or next to a tumor so the dose stays local.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

After a patient receives radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy with iodine-131, some of the iodine-131 not taken up by the thyroid leaves the body in urine, saliva, and sweat, and the patient's body gives off gamma rays for several days. Nurses and patients therefore use the same time, distance, and shielding ideas from this section. The American Thyroid Association describes typical precautions: keeping about 6 feet from other people, especially children and pregnant women; sleeping alone; not sharing utensils or preparing food for others; and limiting time in public places. Depending on the reason for treatment and the dose, precautions typically last from about 3 days to 2 weeks, which fits iodine-131's 8.0-day half-life plus the body's elimination of iodine. In the hospital, care follows the facility's radiation safety protocol, such as posted rooms, time limits at the bedside, and special handling of linens and waste. (Context only; always follow the order and facility policy.)

Source: American Thyroid Association, "Radioactive Iodine" (thyroid.org).

Sterilizing Medical Supplies and Food

Gamma rays from cobalt-60 are used to sterilize (kill all microorganisms on) single-use medical supplies such as syringes, gloves, sutures, and surgical gowns. Because gamma rays are so penetrating, products can be sterilized after they are sealed in their final packaging and boxed. The same method is used to irradiate some foods, such as spices, some fruits and vegetables, and meat, to kill bacteria and insects and to slow spoilage. Irradiated food in the United States carries the international Radura symbol. Neither the supplies nor the food become radioactive: gamma rays pass through and deposit energy, but they do not change any nuclei.

Biology Connection: Microbiology of Gamma Sterilization

Gamma rays kill microorganisms the same way they damage human cells: by breaking DNA, directly and through free radicals made from water. A single bacterium has only one copy of its chromosome and limited repair, so enough breaks stop it from dividing. Organisms differ in how much radiation they can survive. Bacterial spores, which are dry and tough, and the remarkable bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans, which can rebuild a shattered chromosome, need much larger doses than ordinary bacteria. Sterilization doses are chosen so that the chance of a single surviving microbe on a product is extremely small, the same "sterility assurance" idea used for steam autoclaves.

X-Rays and Gamma Rays

X-rays and gamma rays are both high-energy electromagnetic radiation (Chapter 3), and both are ionizing. The difference is where they come from:

  • X-rays are made outside the nucleus, in an X-ray tube, when fast electrons slam into a metal target. The tube can be switched off, and then no radiation is produced.
  • Gamma rays come from the nucleus during radioactive decay. A radioisotope cannot be switched off; it decays at the rate set by its half-life.

In X-ray imaging and CT (computed tomography) scans, a beam passes through the patient from outside; dense tissue such as bone absorbs more X-rays and shows up white. In nuclear medicine, the source is inside the patient, and the gamma camera shows where the tracer went, which reveals how an organ is working, not just its shape.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Radiologic technologists take X-ray and CT images; nuclear medicine technologists prepare and give tracers such as technetium-99m and operate gamma cameras and PET scanners. Both apply time, distance, and shielding every day. An X-ray technologist steps behind a lead-lined barrier or lead-glass window during the exposure and uses collimation (narrowing the beam) so only the needed area is exposed. A nuclear medicine technologist draws up doses behind lead-glass shields, uses tungsten or lead syringe shields, carries vials in lead "pigs," and keeps distance from patients who have just received a tracer, since the patient is now the gamma source. Short-lived waste, such as used syringes, is often held in shielded storage until it has decayed for about 10 half-lives (Section 14.3). Dosimeter badges (Section 14.1) confirm that doses stay as low as reasonably achievable. (Context only.)

Professor Tip

For any medical use of radiation, ask three questions and the answer usually follows. Imaging or treating? Imaging needs radiation that escapes the body (gamma or positron); treating needs radiation that stays local (beta, or a focused beam). How long should it last? Pick the half-life to match. Where should it go? The chemistry decides: iodine to the thyroid, glucose (FDG) to active cells.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking irradiated food or sterilized supplies become radioactive: gamma rays pass through without changing any nuclei.
  • Choosing an alpha or beta emitter for imaging: their particles do not leave the body; imaging needs gamma rays.
  • Halving the dose when the distance doubles: from a small source, doubling the distance gives one-fourth the dose rate.
  • Calling X-rays and gamma rays the same thing: both are high-energy light, but X-rays come from a machine that can be turned off, and gamma rays come from decaying nuclei.

Summary

  • Ionizing radiation ionizes water and makes free radicals that damage DNA; rapidly dividing cells (bone marrow, intestines, skin, fetus, cancer) are most sensitive.
  • Small doses (imaging, background of about 3 mSv per year) have no immediate effects; large doses received at once (about 1 Sv or more) cause radiation sickness.
  • Radon-222 from uranium in rocks and soil is the largest natural source of exposure and the second-leading cause of lung cancer; the EPA action level is 4.0 pCi/L.
  • Reduce dose with time, distance (2× distance = ¼ dose), and shielding; ALARA.
  • Diagnosis uses short-lived gamma or positron emitters (technetium-99m, fluorine-18 FDG for PET); therapy uses iodine-131, external beams, or implanted seeds; cobalt-60 gamma rays sterilize supplies and food.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Why is technetium-99m a better choice than iodine-131 for imaging the lungs?

Answer

Technetium-99m gives off only gamma rays, which leave the body to reach the camera, and its 6.0-hour half-life means the activity is gone within a couple of days. Iodine-131 also gives off beta particles, which stay in the body and add dose without helping the image, and its 8.0-day half-life keeps it active much longer.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A technologist receives a dose rate of 20. μSv/h standing 1.0 m from a patient who just received a tracer. What is the dose rate at 2.0 m? At 3.0 m?

Answer

At 2.0 m: 20. × 1/4 = 5.0 μSv/h. At 3.0 m: 20. × 1/9 = 2.2 μSv/h.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Explain why the thyroid gland, and almost no other organ, takes up iodine-131.

Answer

The thyroid uses iodine to make the hormones T4 and T3, so its cells actively collect iodide from the blood. The body cannot tell radioactive iodine from stable iodine, so iodine-131 is concentrated in the thyroid too.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Why does radon in a home pose a much greater health risk than the same amount of an alpha emitter sealed in a container on a shelf?

Answer

Alpha particles cannot get through a container or the outer layer of skin. Radon is a gas that is breathed in, so it and its decay products sit in the lungs and deliver their highly ionizing alpha particles directly to living lung cells.
