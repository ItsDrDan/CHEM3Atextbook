Learning Objectives

  • Describe radioactivity, its units, and how radiation is detected.
  • Write and balance nuclear equations for alpha, beta, positron, and gamma decay, electron capture, and bombardment, and compare penetrating and ionizing power.
  • Do half-life and radiocarbon-dating calculations with whole numbers of half-lives.
  • Describe fission, nuclear power, fusion, and the effects and medical uses of radiation.

How to Use This Guide

Read the Key Ideas after the sections, use the video moments for anything still unclear, work the examples with the answers covered, and then go to the Practice Problems. Two skills carry most of the chapter: balancing a nuclear equation (add the top numbers, add the bottom numbers) and counting half-lives (halve once per half-life). Key Ideas

1. Radioactivity and its units

Unstable nuclei give off radiation to become more stable. Proton repulsion grows as a nucleus gets bigger; small stable nuclei have about 1 neutron per proton, heavy ones up to about 1.5. Too many neutrons → beta decay; too few → positron emission or electron capture; too big → alpha decay. A radioisotope is named by its mass number (iodine-131, \( {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \)). Activity: curie, becquerel (1 Bq = 1 decay/s). Dose: gray, sievert (mSv), rem. Detectors: Geiger counter, scintillation counter, dosimeter badge. (Section 14.1)

2. Decay types and nuclear equations

Chemical reactions rearrange electrons; nuclear reactions change the nucleus and can change the element. Alpha \( {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \): A − 4, Z − 2. Beta \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \): Z + 1. Positron \( {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \): Z − 1. Gamma \( {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \): no change. These simple decays, plus electron capture and bombardment, are the main nuclear-equation skills. In electron capture, \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \) is written on the left, as a reactant. Mass numbers and atomic numbers each add up to the same totals on both sides; count protons and neutrons in parent and daughter to check. In bombardment, "bombarded with" puts the particle on the reactant side. Penetrating power gamma > beta > alpha; ionizing power the reverse; shield with paper, aluminum, lead (water or concrete for neutrons). Swallowed alpha emitters are the most harmful, as the Radium Girls showed. (Section 14.2)

3. Half-life

n = time ÷ half-life (the half-life is an equality you can use as a conversion factor); amount left = initial × (1/2)n, with n a whole number. For percent-only problems, map 100% → 50% → 25% → … and count the arrows. Work backward by doubling. (Section 14.3)

4. Radiocarbon dating

Living things keep a constant carbon-14 ratio; after death it decays with a half-life of 5730 years. Age = n × 5730 years; useful to about 50,000 years, once-living material only. (Section 14.4)

5. Fission and fusion

Fission: a neutron splits uranium-235 into two smaller nuclei and 2–3 neutrons, which can start a chain reaction (critical mass). Reactors control it with control rods. Fusion joins light nuclei, as in the Sun. Both turn a little mass into energy (E = mc2). Know what fission and fusion are and how a reactor is controlled; completing a full fission or fusion equation is a challenge skill (same balancing rules). (Section 14.5)

6. Radiation and health

Ionizing radiation damages DNA; rapidly dividing cells are most sensitive. Radon is the largest natural source. Protect with time, distance, and shielding (ALARA). (Section 14.6)

7. Radiation in medicine

Diagnosis: short-lived gamma or positron emitters (technetium-99m, fluorine-18 FDG and gallium-68 for PET); a tracer is chosen by its decay type and half-life. Therapy: iodine-131, external beams. Gamma rays sterilize supplies and food. X-rays come from a tube that can be switched off; gamma rays come from nuclei. Contrast agents (iodine, insoluble BaSO4) block X-rays and are not radioactive. (Section 14.7)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Decay equation, then half-life

Problem. (a) Write the balanced equation for the beta decay of iodine-131. (b) A hospital receives 40.0 mg of iodine-131 (half-life 8.0 days). How much remains after 16.0 days?

Solution.

\( \text{(a)}\;{}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \rightarrow {}^{131}_{54}\text{Xe} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \qquad \text{(b)}\;n = \dfrac{16.0\;\text{days}}{8.0\;\text{days}} = 2,\quad {40.0\;\text{mg}} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2} = {10.0\;\text{mg}} \)

Check. (a) 131 = 131 + 0 and 53 = 54 − 1. (b) 40.0 → 20.0 → 10.0 mg, two halvings.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Identify the decay, then the time

Problem. (a) Identify the missing particle: \( {}^{68}_{31}\text{Ga} \rightarrow {}^{68}_{30}\text{Zn} + \;? \) (b) A PET dose of fluorine-18 (half-life 110 min) has an activity of 20.0 mCi. How long until it is 5.00 mCi?

Solution. (a) Missing A = 68 − 68 = 0; missing Z = 31 − 30 = +1. That is a positron, \( {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \): positron emission. (b) 20.0 → 10.0 → 5.00 mCi is 2 half-lives; 2 × 110 min = 220 min.

Check. Positron emission lowers Z by 1 with no change in A, as from Ga (31) to Zn (30). 5.00 is one-fourth of 20.0.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Radiocarbon age

Problem. A fragment of a woven sandal has 25.0% of the carbon-14 of living plant fiber. Estimate its age.

Solution. 100% → 50.0% → 25.0% is 2 half-lives.

\( \text{age} = 2 \times {5730\;\text{years}} = {1.15 \times 10^{4}\;\text{years}} \)

Check. The answer is within the roughly 50,000-year range of the method, and 5730 years has three significant figures.

Common Mistakes

  • Writing the beta particle as \( {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \) (that is a positron) or identifying the new element by its mass number instead of its atomic number.
  • Thinking gamma emission changes the element.
  • Putting the bombarding particle on the product side; "bombarded with" means it is a reactant.
  • Assuming the most penetrating radiation is also the most ionizing.
  • Subtracting instead of halving in half-life problems, or counting the starting amount as a half-life (count the arrows, not the numbers).
  • Thinking contrast agents or MRI involve radioactivity.
  • Dating rocks or metal with carbon-14; writing too many significant figures with 5730 years.
  • Mixing up fission and fusion, or control rods and fuel rods.
