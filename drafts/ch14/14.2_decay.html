Learning Objectives

  • Compare chemical and nuclear reactions.
  • Write the symbols for alpha particles, beta particles, positrons, gamma rays, neutrons, and protons.
  • Describe what happens in the nucleus in alpha decay, beta decay, positron emission, electron capture, and gamma emission.
  • Complete and balance nuclear equations by conserving mass number and atomic number, and check them by counting protons and neutrons.
  • Write bombardment (transmutation) equations from a word description.
  • Compare the penetrating and ionizing power of alpha, beta, and gamma radiation and choose a shield for each.

Technetium-99m, the tracer in Mateo's lung scan, is made in the hospital's nuclear pharmacy from molybdenum-99 that arrives in a shielded container. One element turning into another sounds like alchemy, but it happens every time a nucleus decays. In this section you will learn the kinds of decay, how to write a balanced nuclear equation for each, and why some radiation is stopped by a sheet of paper while other radiation needs lead.

Chemical Reactions and Nuclear Reactions

Every reaction in Chapters 7 and 8 was a chemical reaction: atoms traded, shared, or rearranged their electrons, and the nuclei never changed. That is why an oxygen atom on the left of a chemical equation is still an oxygen atom on the right. A nuclear reaction changes the nucleus itself: protons and neutrons are gained, lost, or converted into each other, so the element can change into a different one. The table below compares the two.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Chemical reactions versus nuclear reactions
FeatureChemical reactionNuclear reaction
What changesElectrons are transferred or shared; bonds break and formProtons and neutrons in the nucleus change
Element identityNever changes: the same elements on both sidesCan change: a new element can form
IsotopesBehave almost the same (C-12 and C-14 react alike)Behave very differently (C-12 is stable; C-14 decays)
What you balanceAtoms of each element, and chargeMass numbers (top) and atomic numbers (bottom); ion charges are ignored
Energy involvedSmall: kilojoules per moleEnormous: millions of times larger per mole (Section 14.5)
Changed by temperature or a catalyst?Yes (Chapter 13)No: a radioisotope decays at the same rate no matter what

The Kinds of Nuclear Radiation

An unstable nucleus can give off four main kinds of radiation. Each has a symbol written like an isotope symbol, with its mass number on top and its atomic number (or charge) on the bottom, so it can be counted in a nuclear equation.

Particles and radiation in nuclear equations
RadiationSymbolsWhat it isMass number, charge
Alpha particle\( {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \) or \( \alpha \)A helium nucleus: 2 protons and 2 neutrons4, +2
Beta particle\( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \) or \( \beta \) (\( \beta^- \))A high-energy electron made in the nucleus0, −1
Positron\( {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \) or \( \beta^+ \)The antimatter partner of the electron: same mass, positive charge0, +1
Gamma ray\( {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \) or \( \gamma \)High-energy electromagnetic radiation (light), pure energy0, 0
Neutron\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)A neutron, used to bombard nuclei and released in fission1, 0
Proton\( {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \) or \( {}^{1}_{1}\text{p} \)A proton (a hydrogen-1 nucleus)1, +1

Balancing Nuclear Equations

A nuclear equation shows the starting nucleus (the parent) on the left and the new nucleus (the daughter) and the radiation on the right. Nuclear equations are easier to balance than chemical equations, because there are only two rules:

  • The mass numbers (top numbers) add up to the same total on both sides.
  • The atomic numbers (bottom numbers, or charges) add up to the same total on both sides.

To complete a nuclear equation:

  1. Write the incomplete equation with the symbols you know, including A and Z for each one.
  2. Find what is missing: subtract to get the missing mass number and atomic number.
  3. Identify the new nucleus from its atomic number on the periodic table (the atomic number is the element's "fingerprint").
  4. Check that the top numbers and the bottom numbers each add up on both sides.

Alpha decay

In alpha decay, the nucleus gives off an alpha particle. The new nucleus has a mass number 4 lower and an atomic number 2 lower. Alpha decay is common for very heavy nuclei such as uranium, radium, and radon.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Alpha decay of radium-226

Problem. Radium-226, found in soil and rock, decays by alpha emission. Write the balanced nuclear equation.

Solution. Radium is element 88. Write what you know, with a blank for the new nucleus:

\( {}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

Mass number: 226 = ? + 4, so ? = 222. Atomic number: 88 = ? + 2, so ? = 86. Element 86 is radon.

\( {}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

Check. Top: 222 + 4 = 226. Bottom: 86 + 2 = 88. This is how radon gas forms underground (Section 14.6). Count the particles to see what really happened: radium-226 has 88 protons and 226 − 88 = 138 neutrons; radon-222 has 86 protons and 136 neutrons. The nucleus lost 2 protons and 2 neutrons, exactly the alpha particle.

Beta decay

In beta decay, a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton and an electron, and the electron is thrown out as a beta particle:

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{1}_{1}\text{p} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

The nucleus keeps the same mass number but gains a proton, so its atomic number goes up by 1. Beta decay is common for nuclei with too many neutrons.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Beta decay of iodine-131

Problem. Iodine-131 is a beta emitter. Write the balanced nuclear equation.

Solution. Iodine is element 53.

\( {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \rightarrow {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

Mass number: 131 = ? + 0, so ? = 131. Atomic number: 53 = ? + (−1), so ? = 54. Element 54 is xenon.

\( {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \rightarrow {}^{131}_{54}\text{Xe} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

Check. Top: 131 + 0 = 131. Bottom: 54 + (−1) = 53. In beta decay the new element is always one place to the right on the periodic table. Counting particles: iodine-131 has 53 protons and 78 neutrons; xenon-131 has 54 protons and 77 neutrons. One neutron became one proton, which is what beta decay is.

Positron emission and electron capture

In positron emission, a proton changes into a neutron and a positron, and the positron leaves the nucleus. The mass number stays the same and the atomic number goes down by 1. Positron emitters such as fluorine-18 are used in PET scans (Section 14.7).

\( {}^{1}_{1}\text{p} \rightarrow {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \)

A nucleus can make the same change another way: in electron capture, the nucleus pulls in one of the atom's own inner electrons, which combines with a proton to make a neutron. The electron is written on the left side, as a reactant. Iodine-125, used in small radioactive "seeds" placed in some prostate tumors, decays this way.

\( {}^{125}_{53}\text{I} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \rightarrow {}^{125}_{52}\text{Te} \)

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Positron emission of fluorine-18

Problem. Fluorine-18 decays by positron emission. Write the balanced nuclear equation.

Solution. Fluorine is element 9.

\( {}^{18}_{9}\text{F} \rightarrow {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \)

Mass number: 18 = ? + 0, so ? = 18. Atomic number: 9 = ? + 1, so ? = 8. Element 8 is oxygen.

\( {}^{18}_{9}\text{F} \rightarrow {}^{18}_{8}\text{O} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \)

Check. Top: 18 + 0 = 18. Bottom: 8 + 1 = 9. Positron emission moves the element one place to the left. Counting particles: fluorine-18 has 9 protons and 9 neutrons; oxygen-18 has 8 protons and 10 neutrons. One proton became one neutron.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): One nucleus, two paths

Problem. Potassium-40, the main radioisotope in your own body, decays in two ways. Most of its nuclei emit a beta particle; about 1 in 9 instead capture an electron. Write both equations.

Solution. Potassium is element 19. In beta decay the electron is a product; in electron capture it is a reactant.

\( \text{Beta decay:}\;\;{}^{40}_{19}\text{K} \rightarrow {}^{40}_{20}\text{Ca} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \qquad\qquad \text{Electron capture:}\;\;{}^{40}_{19}\text{K} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \rightarrow {}^{40}_{18}\text{Ar} \)

Beta decay: bottom, 19 = ? + (−1), so ? = 20, calcium. Electron capture: bottom, 19 + (−1) = 18, argon.

Check. The same −1 sends Z in opposite directions depending on which side of the arrow it is on: up by 1 as a product, down by 1 as a reactant. The argon-40 made this way builds up inside potassium-bearing rocks, and geologists use it to date them (potassium–argon dating).

Gamma emission

After a decay, the new nucleus is often left with extra energy, like an excited electron in Chapter 3. It releases that energy as a gamma ray. A nucleus waiting to do this is metastable, shown by an "m" after the mass number. Gamma emission changes neither the mass number nor the atomic number; the element stays the same. Technetium-99m is made by beta decay of molybdenum-99 and then gives off the gamma ray that a gamma camera detects:

\( {}^{99}_{42}\text{Mo} \rightarrow {}^{99\text{m}}_{43}\text{Tc} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \qquad\qquad {}^{99\text{m}}_{43}\text{Tc} \rightarrow {}^{99}_{43}\text{Tc} + {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \)

Gamma rays also come out along with many alpha and beta decays. Because they do not change A or Z, they are often left out of the balanced equation. If a problem asks for them, write them in; the zeros change nothing.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Beta and gamma together

Problem. Iron-59, used in research on how the body absorbs iron, gives off a beta particle and a gamma ray at the same time. Write the balanced equation, and describe what happened in the nucleus.

Solution. Iron is element 26.

\( {}^{59}_{26}\text{Fe} \rightarrow {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \)

Top: 59 = ? + 0 + 0, so ? = 59. Bottom: 26 = ? + (−1) + 0, so ? = 27, cobalt.

\( {}^{59}_{26}\text{Fe} \rightarrow {}^{59}_{27}\text{Co} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \)

Check. Iron-59 has 26 protons and 33 neutrons; cobalt-59 has 27 protons and 32 neutrons. A neutron became a proton (the beta decay), and the new nucleus released its extra energy as a gamma ray, which changed neither number.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Identify the missing particle

Problem. Complete the equation and name the kind of decay.

\( {}^{32}_{15}\text{P} \rightarrow {}^{32}_{16}\text{S} + \;? \)

Solution. Mass number: 32 = 32 + ?, so the particle has A = 0. Atomic number: 15 = 16 + ?, so the particle has Z = −1. A particle with A = 0 and charge −1 is a beta particle, \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \). This is beta decay.

Check. The atomic number went up by 1 (P to S) with no change in mass number, which is the signature of beta decay.

Professor Tip: What Really Happened?

After you balance a nuclear equation, count the protons (Z) and neutrons (A − Z) in the parent and the daughter. The counts tell the story, and they catch mistakes:

  • Lost 2 protons and 2 neutrons: alpha.
  • One fewer neutron, one more proton: beta (n → p).
  • One fewer proton, one more neutron: positron emission or electron capture (p → n).
  • No change at all: gamma.

Making radioisotopes by bombardment

Most medical radioisotopes do not occur in nature. They are made by bombarding a stable nucleus with a small particle (a neutron, proton, or alpha particle) in a nuclear reactor or a particle accelerator called a cyclotron. (The cyclotron was invented in Berkeley, California, and many large medical centers now have one on site to make PET tracers.) Changing one element into another this way is called transmutation. In 1934, Irène Joliot-Curie (Marie Curie's daughter) and Frédéric Joliot made the first artificial radioisotope by hitting aluminum with alpha particles. The same two rules balance these equations.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): A bombardment reaction

Problem. Aluminum-27 is bombarded with an alpha particle. The products are a neutron and a new nucleus. Identify the new nucleus.

Solution.

\( {}^{27}_{13}\text{Al} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Mass numbers: 27 + 4 = 31 = ? + 1, so ? = 30. Atomic numbers: 13 + 2 = 15 = ? + 0, so ? = 15. Element 15 is phosphorus: \( {}^{30}_{15}\text{P} \).

Check. Left: 31 and 15. Right: 30 + 1 = 31 and 15 + 0 = 15. Phosphorus-30 is radioactive; this was Joliot-Curie's discovery.

Bombardment problems are often written as sentences. Translate the words first:

  • "Bombarded with," "collides with," "hit by," or "captures": the particle is a reactant, written on the left with the target nucleus.
  • "Producing," "releasing," "emitting," or "giving off": these are products, on the right.
  • Usually one side is complete. Add up its top numbers and bottom numbers, and those totals must appear on the other side too.

Example \(\PageIndex{8}\): A bombardment word problem

Problem. When boron-10 is bombarded with an alpha particle, a neutron and a radioactive isotope are produced. Write the balanced equation and identify the isotope.

Solution. Boron is element 5. "Bombarded with an alpha particle" puts He-4 on the left; "a neutron and an isotope are produced" puts both on the right.

\( {}^{10}_{5}\text{B} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Left: top 10 + 4 = 14, bottom 5 + 2 = 7. Right: ? + 1 = 14, so ? = 13; ? + 0 = 7, so ? = 7, nitrogen.

\( {}^{10}_{5}\text{B} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow {}^{13}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Check. Right: 13 + 1 = 14 and 7 + 0 = 7. Nitrogen-13 has 7 protons and only 6 neutrons, too few, so it is a positron emitter; it is used today as a PET tracer for blood flow to the heart.

Example \(\PageIndex{9}\): Find the bombarding particle

Problem. Argon-40 is struck by a particle, producing potassium-43 and a proton. What was the particle?

Solution. This time the right side is complete. Argon is element 18 and potassium is 19.

\( {}^{40}_{18}\text{Ar} + {}^{?}_{?}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{43}_{19}\text{K} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \)

Right: top 43 + 1 = 44, bottom 19 + 1 = 20. Left: 40 + ? = 44, so ? = 4; 18 + ? = 20, so ? = 2. A particle with A = 4 and Z = 2 is an alpha particle, \( {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \).

Check. Left: 40 + 4 = 44 and 18 + 2 = 20, matching the right.

Professor Tip

Memorize the four radiation symbols; most exams do not give them to you. Then every nuclear equation is just two addition problems, one on top and one on the bottom. Watch the signs: a beta particle counts as −1 on the bottom, so the new atomic number is one higher than the parent's. Always look up the new element by its atomic number, never by its mass number.

Penetrating Power and Ionizing Power

Radiation is dangerous because it is ionizing: it knocks electrons out of the atoms and molecules it hits, leaving behind reactive ions that can break bonds in DNA and other molecules (Section 14.6). The kinds of radiation differ in two ways:

  • Penetrating power: how far the radiation travels into matter before it is stopped.
  • Ionizing power: how many ions it makes along the way.

These two go in opposite directions. A heavy, slow, doubly charged alpha particle slams into nearly every molecule it meets, so it makes a great many ions in a very short distance and is stopped quickly. Think of it as hitting a sheet of paper and stopping there, but tearing up that surface badly. A gamma ray has no mass and no charge and travels at the speed of light, so it plows straight through most matter, passing most atoms without interacting and ionizing fewer molecules along its path. Beta particles, light and fast, fall in between.

The table below compares the three main kinds of radiation and the shielding that protects against each.

Penetrating power, ionizing power, and shielding
RadiationPenetrating powerIonizing powerStopped by
Alpha (α)Lowest: a few centimeters of airHighestPaper, clothing, the outer layer of skin
Beta (β)Medium: a few meters of air, a few millimeters into tissueMediumAluminum foil or sheet, wood, heavy clothing, a lab coat and gloves
Gamma (γ)Highest: passes through the bodyLowestLead or thick concrete (reduced, not fully stopped)
Neutron (n)High: no charge, so it passes through metal and tissueIndirect: it knocks nuclei loose, and they ionizeWater, concrete, or plastic (materials rich in hydrogen); some hydrogen nuclei capture a neutron and become deuterium

Which radiation is "most dangerous" depends on where the source is. Outside the body, gamma rays are the biggest concern, because they reach internal organs; alpha particles cannot get through the dead outer layer of skin. Inside the body, if an alpha emitter is inhaled or swallowed, it is the most damaging, because all of its ionizing power is delivered to the nearby living cells. That is why radon gas in homes and the radium that poisoned factory workers who painted glowing watch dials in the 1920s were so harmful.

Biology Connection: The Radium Girls

After radium was discovered, it became a fad. Its faint glow made it seem magical, and in the early 1900s companies sold radium in cosmetics, health tonics, and even drinking cups. In the late 1910s and 1920s, factories in the United States hired hundreds of young women to paint watch and instrument dials with glow-in-the-dark radium paint, so that the numbers could be read in the dark, for example by soldiers and pilots. To paint fine lines, the workers were taught to shape the brush tip with their lips, so with every dial they swallowed a little radium.

Radium is in Group 2, the same group as calcium, and the body handles it like calcium: it is deposited in bone. There, radium-226, an alpha emitter, delivered all of its ionizing power to the bone and the blood-forming marrow around it, year after year. Many of the dial painters developed anemia, crumbling jawbones (bone tissue death, called necrosis), and bone cancers, and many died young. Their lawsuits in the late 1920s helped establish workers' rights to compensation for occupational illness and pushed the development of radiation safety standards. Their story is the clearest lesson in this section: an alpha emitter outside the body is stopped by skin, but swallowed or inhaled, it is the most dangerous radiation of all.

Biology Connection: You Are Slightly Radioactive

Your body needs potassium for nerve signals and muscle contraction, and a small fraction of all potassium, about 0.012%, is radioactive potassium-40. Potassium-40 decays mostly by beta emission, with a half-life of about 1.25 billion years:

\( {}^{40}_{19}\text{K} \rightarrow {}^{40}_{20}\text{Ca} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

An adult's body contains enough potassium-40 for roughly 4,000 nuclei to decay every second. Bananas, potatoes, and beans, all rich in potassium, are slightly radioactive for the same reason. The dose is small and is part of the natural background radiation everyone receives.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating the beta particle as +1: a beta particle is \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \); the positron is \( {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \).
  • Naming the new element from its mass number: the atomic number (bottom number) identifies the element.
  • Changing the element in gamma emission: gamma rays change neither A nor Z.
  • Balancing only the top numbers: both the mass numbers and the atomic numbers must add up.
  • Thinking the most penetrating radiation is also the most ionizing: the two are opposite; alpha is the most ionizing and least penetrating.
  • Putting the bombarding particle on the product side: "bombarded with" or "collides with" means the particle is a reactant.
  • Treating a nuclear equation like a chemical one: in a nuclear reaction the element can change, and you balance mass numbers and atomic numbers, not atoms.

Summary

  • Alpha \( {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \): A − 4, Z − 2. Beta \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \): A same, Z + 1. Positron \( {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \): A same, Z − 1. Gamma \( {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \): A and Z unchanged.
  • Electron capture puts \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \) on the reactant side; the atomic number goes down by 1.
  • Chemical reactions rearrange electrons and never change the element; nuclear reactions change the nucleus and can make a new element.
  • In every nuclear equation, the mass numbers and the atomic numbers each add up to the same totals on both sides. Counting protons and neutrons before and after shows what happened: alpha loses 2 p and 2 n, beta turns n into p, positron emission and electron capture turn p into n.
  • In bombardment, the particle that hits the target is a reactant; usually one side of the equation is complete.
  • Penetrating power: gamma > beta > alpha. Ionizing power: alpha > beta > gamma. Shields: paper or skin for alpha, aluminum or a lab coat for beta, lead or concrete for gamma, water or concrete for neutrons. Swallowed or inhaled alpha emitters are the most harmful (the Radium Girls).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Americium-241, used in household smoke detectors, decays by alpha emission. Write the balanced nuclear equation.

Hint

An alpha particle is 42He. Subtract 4 from the top number and 2 from the bottom number, then look up the new element by its Z.

Answer

Americium is element 95. 241 − 4 = 237; 95 − 2 = 93, neptunium: \( {}^{241}_{95}\text{Am} \rightarrow {}^{237}_{93}\text{Np} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Cobalt-60, used in radiation therapy and to sterilize medical supplies, is a beta emitter. Write the balanced nuclear equation.

Hint

A beta particle is 0−1e. The top number stays the same; what must the daughter's bottom number be so the bottom numbers add up?

Answer

Cobalt is element 27. A stays 60; Z becomes 27 + 1 = 28, nickel: \( {}^{60}_{27}\text{Co} \rightarrow {}^{60}_{28}\text{Ni} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Oxygen-15 is a positron emitter used in some PET studies of blood flow. Write the balanced nuclear equation.

Hint

A positron is 0+1e. Keep the top number; the daughter's Z plus 1 must equal oxygen's Z. Look up that element.

Answer

Oxygen is element 8. A stays 15; Z becomes 8 − 1 = 7, nitrogen: \( {}^{15}_{8}\text{O} \rightarrow {}^{15}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Complete the equation: \( {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{6}\text{C} + \;? \)

Hint

Add the top numbers and the bottom numbers on the left, then subtract what is already on the right. Which particle has the leftover A and Z?

Answer

Left: A = 15, Z = 7. Right so far: A = 14, Z = 6. The missing particle has A = 1 and Z = 1, a proton, \( {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \). This reaction makes carbon-14 in the atmosphere (Section 14.4).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Which shielding would you choose for each source, and why? (a) a pure alpha emitter in a sealed vial; (b) a beta emitter; (c) a gamma emitter.

Hint

Think about penetrating power: alpha is stopped by paper, beta by a few millimeters of light material, and gamma needs something dense.

Answer

(a) The vial wall, paper, or gloves are enough, since alpha is stopped by paper; the danger is breathing in or swallowing it. (b) Aluminum, plastic, or wood, plus a lab coat and gloves. (c) Lead or thick concrete, because gamma rays pass through lighter materials.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

Nickel-58 is bombarded with a proton, producing an alpha particle and a radioactive isotope. Write the balanced equation.

Hint

The proton is 11H and the alpha particle is 42He. Total the left side, subtract the alpha particle, and look up the element with the leftover Z.

Answer

Left: 58 + 1 = 59 and 28 + 1 = 29. Right: ? + 4 = 59, so A = 55; ? + 2 = 29, so Z = 27, cobalt: \( {}^{58}_{28}\text{Ni} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{55}_{27}\text{Co} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{7}\)

Technetium-98 captures two neutrons and then releases an alpha particle. What isotope forms?

Hint

Two neutrons add 2 to the top and nothing to the bottom. Total the left side, subtract the alpha particle (4 on top, 2 on bottom), and identify the element.

Answer

Left: 98 + 2(1) = 100 and 43 + 2(0) = 43. Right: ? + 4 = 100, so A = 96; ? + 2 = 43, so Z = 41, niobium: niobium-96, \( {}^{96}_{41}\text{Nb} \).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{8}\)

Carbon-11, a PET tracer, decays to boron-11. Count the protons and neutrons in each nucleus and decide which kind of decay took place.

Hint

Protons = Z and neutrons = A − Z for each nucleus. If A stays the same and a proton turned into a neutron, which decay does that?

Answer

Carbon-11: 6 protons, 5 neutrons. Boron-11: 5 protons, 6 neutrons. A proton became a neutron and A did not change, so this is positron emission (electron capture would give the same daughter): \( {}^{11}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{11}_{5}\text{B} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \).
