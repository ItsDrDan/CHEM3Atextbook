Learning Objectives

  • Describe how radioisotopes are used in diagnosis (technetium-99m, PET with fluorine-18) and explain what makes a good imaging tracer.
  • Describe how radioisotopes and radiation beams are used in therapy (iodine-131, external beam, brachytherapy) and in sterilization (gamma rays).
  • Distinguish X-rays from gamma rays.

The same property that makes radiation dangerous makes it useful in medicine. Ionizing radiation damages cells (Section 14.6), and it can be aimed at cancer cells to destroy them. It passes through the body, so a camera outside can see a tracer inside. This section brings together everything in the chapter (decay types, penetrating power, half-life, and the time, distance, and shielding rules of Section 14.6) to explain how health care puts radiation to work.

Radioisotopes in Diagnosis

A tracer is a radioisotope, often attached to a molecule that the body handles in a known way, given in a tiny amount so that its location can be imaged. A good diagnostic tracer:

  • gives off gamma rays (or positrons that produce gamma rays), which pass out of the body to the camera; alpha and beta particles would be stopped inside the body and add dose without making an image;
  • has a short half-life (hours, not years), so the activity is gone soon after the scan (Section 14.3);
  • goes to the organ of interest because of its chemistry.

Technetium-99m (half-life 6.0 h) is used in most nuclear medicine scans. It is attached to different carrier molecules to image bones, the heart muscle, the kidneys, and, in Mateo's V/Q scan, blood flow and airflow in the lungs. Iodine-123 is used to image the thyroid.

In positron emission tomography (PET), the tracer is usually fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), a glucose molecule in which one −OH group is replaced by a fluorine-18 atom. Cells take up FDG as if it were glucose. Each fluorine-18 nucleus emits a positron (Section 14.2), which travels a millimeter or so before meeting an electron. The two annihilate, and their mass becomes two gamma rays that fly off in opposite directions. A ring of detectors around the patient catches both gamma rays at the same instant and works out where they came from.

Biology Connection: Why FDG Lights Up Active Tissue

Glucose is the main fuel for cellular respiration, and tissues that are working hard take up more of it through glucose transporter proteins in their membranes. Many cancers, the brain, and actively contracting heart muscle use glucose at high rates. FDG enters cells through the same transporters, and the first enzyme of glycolysis attaches a phosphate to it, as it does to glucose. But the altered molecule cannot continue through glycolysis and cannot easily leave the cell, so it is trapped. The PET image is therefore a map of glucose use: bright spots show tissue with high metabolic activity, such as a tumor. That is also why patients fast before a PET scan, so blood glucose does not compete with the tracer.

Radioisotopes in Therapy

Treatment uses the opposite strategy from imaging: the goal is to deliver as much ionizing radiation as possible to diseased cells while sparing healthy ones.

  • Iodine-131 for the thyroid. The thyroid gland uses iodine to make its hormones (thyroxine, T4, and triiodothyronine, T3), so it actively collects iodide from the blood. A patient who swallows iodine-131 concentrates it in the thyroid, where its beta particles, which travel less than about 2 millimeters in tissue, destroy overactive thyroid tissue (hyperthyroidism, as in Graves' disease) or remaining thyroid cancer cells. Its gamma rays also allow imaging.
  • External beam radiation therapy. Beams of high-energy X-rays from a machine called a linear accelerator, or gamma rays from cobalt-60 sources, are aimed at a tumor from several directions so the beams overlap at the tumor. "Gamma Knife" radiosurgery focuses about 200 cobalt-60 beams on a small target in the brain.
  • Brachytherapy. Small sealed sources, such as iodine-125 seeds or iridium-192, are placed in or next to a tumor so the dose stays local.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

After a patient receives radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy with iodine-131, some of the iodine-131 not taken up by the thyroid leaves the body in urine, saliva, and sweat, and the patient's body gives off gamma rays for several days. Nurses and patients therefore use the same time, distance, and shielding ideas from Section 14.6. The American Thyroid Association describes typical precautions: keeping about 6 feet from other people, especially children and pregnant women; sleeping alone; not sharing utensils or preparing food for others; and limiting time in public places. Depending on the reason for treatment and the dose, precautions typically last from about 3 days to 2 weeks, which fits iodine-131's 8.0-day half-life plus the body's elimination of iodine. In the hospital, care follows the facility's radiation safety protocol, such as posted rooms, time limits at the bedside, and special handling of linens and waste. Sterilizing Medical Supplies and Food

Gamma rays from cobalt-60 are used to sterilize (kill all microorganisms on) single-use medical supplies such as syringes, gloves, sutures, and surgical gowns. Because gamma rays are so penetrating, products can be sterilized after they are sealed in their final packaging and boxed. The same method is used to irradiate some foods, such as spices, some fruits and vegetables, and meat, to kill bacteria and insects and to slow spoilage. Irradiated food in the United States carries the international Radura symbol. Neither the supplies nor the food become radioactive: gamma rays pass through and deposit energy, but they do not change any nuclei.

Biology Connection: Microbiology of Gamma Sterilization

Gamma rays kill microorganisms the same way they damage human cells: by breaking DNA, directly and through free radicals made from water. A single bacterium has only one copy of its chromosome and limited repair, so enough breaks stop it from dividing. Organisms differ in how much radiation they can survive. Bacterial spores, which are dry and tough, and the remarkable bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans, which can rebuild a shattered chromosome, need much larger doses than ordinary bacteria. Sterilization doses are chosen so that the chance of a single surviving microbe on a product is extremely small, the same "sterility assurance" idea used for steam autoclaves.

X-Rays and Gamma Rays

X-rays and gamma rays are both high-energy electromagnetic radiation (Chapter 3), and both are ionizing. The difference is where they come from:

  • X-rays are made outside the nucleus, in an X-ray tube, when fast electrons slam into a metal target. The tube can be switched off, and then no radiation is produced.
  • Gamma rays come from the nucleus during radioactive decay. A radioisotope cannot be switched off; it decays at the rate set by its half-life.

In X-ray imaging and CT (computed tomography) scans, a beam passes through the patient from outside; dense tissue such as bone absorbs more X-rays and shows up white. In nuclear medicine, the source is inside the patient, and the gamma camera shows where the tracer went, which reveals how an organ is working, not just its shape.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Radiologic technologists take X-ray and CT images; nuclear medicine technologists prepare and give tracers such as technetium-99m and operate gamma cameras and PET scanners. Both apply time, distance, and shielding every day. An X-ray technologist steps behind a lead-lined barrier or lead-glass window during the exposure and uses collimation (narrowing the beam) so only the needed area is exposed. A nuclear medicine technologist draws up doses behind lead-glass shields, uses tungsten or lead syringe shields, carries vials in lead "pigs," and keeps distance from patients who have just received a tracer, since the patient is now the gamma source. Short-lived waste, such as used syringes, is often held in shielded storage until it has decayed for about 10 half-lives (Section 14.3). Dosimeter badges (Section 14.1) confirm that doses stay as low as reasonably achievable. (Context only.)

Professor Tip

For any medical use of radiation, ask three questions and the answer usually follows. Imaging or treating? Imaging needs radiation that escapes the body (gamma or positron); treating needs radiation that stays local (beta, or a focused beam). How long should it last? Pick the half-life to match. Where should it go? The chemistry decides: iodine to the thyroid, glucose (FDG) to active cells.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking irradiated food or sterilized supplies become radioactive: gamma rays pass through without changing any nuclei.
  • Choosing an alpha or beta emitter for imaging: their particles do not leave the body; imaging needs gamma rays.
  • Calling X-rays and gamma rays the same thing: both are high-energy light, but X-rays come from a machine that can be turned off, and gamma rays come from decaying nuclei.

Summary

  • Diagnosis uses short-lived gamma or positron emitters (technetium-99m, fluorine-18 FDG for PET); the tracer's chemistry decides where it goes.
  • Therapy uses iodine-131, external beams, or implanted seeds; cobalt-60 gamma rays sterilize supplies and food without making them radioactive.
  • X-rays come from a tube that can be switched off; gamma rays come from decaying nuclei.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Why is technetium-99m a better choice than iodine-131 for imaging the lungs?

Answer

Technetium-99m gives off only gamma rays, which leave the body to reach the camera, and its 6.0-hour half-life means the activity is gone within a couple of days. Iodine-131 also gives off beta particles, which stay in the body and add dose without helping the image, and its 8.0-day half-life keeps it active much longer.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Explain why the thyroid gland, and almost no other organ, takes up iodine-131.

Answer

The thyroid uses iodine to make the hormones T4 and T3, so its cells actively collect iodide from the blood. The body cannot tell radioactive iodine from stable iodine, so iodine-131 is concentrated in the thyroid too.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A CT scanner and a technetium-99m bone scan both make images with high-energy electromagnetic radiation. For each, state where the radiation comes from and whether it can be switched off.

Answer

A CT scanner uses X-rays made in an X-ray tube outside the patient, when fast electrons hit a metal target; switching the tube off stops the X-rays. A bone scan uses gamma rays from technetium-99m nuclei inside the patient; they cannot be switched off and fade only as the tracer decays (half-life 6.0 h) and leaves the body.
