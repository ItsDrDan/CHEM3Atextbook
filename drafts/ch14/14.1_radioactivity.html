  • Describe how radioactivity was discovered by Becquerel and the Curies.
  • Define radioactivity, radiation, and radioisotope, and write the symbol for a radioisotope from its name.
  • Explain why some nuclei are unstable, using proton repulsion and the neutron-to-proton ratio (qualitatively), and predict the likely decay type for a nucleus with too many or too few neutrons.
  • Match the units curie, becquerel, gray, and sievert (or rem) to what each one measures.
  • Describe how a Geiger counter, a scintillation counter, and a dosimeter badge detect radiation.

Clip a small badge to the collar of every radiologic technologist, dental hygienist, and nuclear medicine technologist at work, and once a month it reports how much radiation that person received. Radiation cannot be seen, heard, felt, or smelled, so people who work with it depend on instruments and on a few carefully defined units. This section tells how radioactivity was discovered, what it is, and how it is measured and detected.

The Discovery of Radioactivity

In 1895 the German physicist Wilhelm Röntgen found that a glowing vacuum tube gave off invisible rays that passed through paper, wood, and flesh but were stopped by bone and metal. He called them X-rays. One of his first X-ray images, of his wife Anna Bertha's hand, showed the bones of her fingers and the ring she was wearing, but barely any of the flesh around them. Within weeks doctors were using X-rays to find broken bones and swallowed objects.

In 1896 the French physicist Henri Becquerel wrapped a photographic plate in black paper and set a uranium salt on top of it. When he developed the plate, it was fogged in the shape of the crystals, even though no light had reached it. The uranium itself was giving off rays, with no tube and no electricity. Two years later, Marie and Pierre Curie showed that the rays came from the uranium atoms themselves and found two new elements that gave off even stronger rays: polonium (named for Marie's home country, Poland) and radium. Marie Curie named the effect radioactivity. Becquerel and the Curies shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics, and Marie Curie received a second Nobel Prize, in Chemistry, in 1911 for discovering polonium and radium. She was the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. Nobody yet knew how harmful radiation could be; she handled radioactive samples for decades with little protection and died in 1934 of a blood disorder (aplastic anemia) linked to her long exposure.

Radioactivity and Radioisotopes

Recall from Chapter 2 that the nucleus holds protons and neutrons. The protons all repel one another, and the nuclear force between protons and neutrons holds the nucleus together. When the balance between these forces is off, for example when a nucleus has too many neutrons, too few, or simply too many particles, the nucleus is unstable. An unstable nucleus becomes more stable by giving off particles or energy, called radiation. This process is radioactivity (also called radioactive decay).

  • A radioisotope is an isotope whose nucleus is radioactive. An element can have both stable and radioactive isotopes: carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable, but carbon-14 is radioactive.
  • Radioactive isotopes become more and more common as nuclei get bigger, especially past about atomic number 20, where a nucleus needs extra neutrons to stay together. Every isotope of every element beyond bismuth (atomic number 83), such as radon, radium, and uranium, is radioactive.
  • A radioisotope is named with its mass number, just like any isotope: iodine-131, technetium-99m, carbon-14. The "m" in technetium-99m means metastable, a nucleus with extra energy that it will soon release (Section 14.2).

The isotope symbol from Chapter 2 is written the same way for a radioisotope: the mass number A (protons + neutrons) as a superscript and the atomic number Z (protons) as a subscript, both on the left of the element symbol.

\( {}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \qquad\qquad {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \;\;\text{(iodine-131)} \)

Nuclear Stability: Why Some Nuclei Decay

Why should a nucleus fall apart at all? Inside the nucleus, every proton pushes on every other proton, because like charges repel. Holding against that push is the nuclear force (also called the strong force), a very strong attraction among protons and neutrons that works only over very short distances, about the width of a nucleus. Neutrons add to the "glue" without adding any repulsion.

In a small nucleus, every particle is close to every other one, so the glue easily wins. As a nucleus grows, the repulsion grows with it: every new proton is pushed by all the protons already there, but the short-range glue reaches only its nearest neighbors. Picture holding apples in your arms. Four or five are easy to cradle. Fifty or sixty, with no basket, start tumbling out no matter how careful you are. A very large nucleus is in the same spot: it is hard for it to hold all of its particles together.

An unstable nucleus can also come apart in more than one way. Smash a Lego sculpture against the wall and it breaks into different pieces each time; an unstable nucleus can likewise lose an alpha particle, emit a beta particle or a positron, or release a gamma ray (Section 14.2). Whatever the path, decay is the nucleus "relaxing" toward a more stable arrangement. Every decay moves the nucleus toward a more stable arrangement.

The neutron-to-proton ratio

Whether a nucleus is stable depends a great deal on its neutron-to-proton ratio, the number of neutrons divided by the number of protons. You will not calculate stability in this course, but the pattern is worth knowing:

  • Small stable nuclei have about 1 neutron per proton: helium-4 (2 protons, 2 neutrons), lithium-6 (3 and 3), carbon-12 (6 and 6).
  • Larger stable nuclei need extra neutrons to spread the protons apart and add glue, so the ratio climbs toward about 1.5 neutrons per proton for the heaviest stable nuclei. Lead-208, for example, has 82 protons and 126 neutrons.
  • If you plot the stable nuclei on a graph of neutrons versus protons, they fall in a narrow band, the band of stability. Nuclei outside the band are radioactive.

Where a nucleus sits relative to the band predicts how it decays:

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Why a nucleus is unstable and how it usually decays
Problem with the nucleusUsual decayWhat it fixesExample
Too many neutrons (above the band)Beta decayA neutron becomes a proton: fewer neutrons, more protonsCarbon-14 (6 p, 8 n) → nitrogen-14 (7 p, 7 n)
Too few neutrons (below the band)Positron emission or electron captureA proton becomes a neutronFluorine-18 (9 p, 9 n) → oxygen-18 (8 p, 10 n)
Simply too big (more than 83 protons)Alpha decaySheds 2 protons and 2 neutrons at onceRadium-226 → radon-222

One decay is often not enough. Uranium-238 goes through a decay series of 14 steps (alpha and beta decays) through elements such as radium and radon before it finally reaches stable lead-206. Each daughter nucleus is a little closer to stable, and the chain stops only when a stable nucleus forms.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Symbol for a medical radioisotope

Problem. Iodine-131 is used to diagnose and treat thyroid disease. Write its symbol and find the number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus.

Solution. Iodine is element 53 on the periodic table, so Z = 53. The name gives A = 131.

\( {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \qquad \text{protons} = 53 \qquad \text{neutrons} = 131 - 53 = 78 \)

Check. Protons plus neutrons, 53 + 78, equals the mass number 131.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Stable and radioactive isotopes

Problem. Potassium-39 is stable, but potassium-40 is radioactive. How are the two nuclei different?

Solution. Both are potassium, so both have 19 protons. Potassium-39 has 39 − 19 = 20 neutrons; potassium-40 has 40 − 19 = 21 neutrons. The one extra neutron makes potassium-40 unstable.

Check. Isotopes of the same element always have the same number of protons and differ only in neutrons (Chapter 2).

Measuring Radiation

Two different questions come up when you work with a radioactive source: how radioactive is the source? and how much radiation did a person absorb? Each has its own units. The table below lists them, with the older unit first and the SI unit second.

Units for radiation
QuantityWhat it measuresUnits
ActivityHow many nuclei in a sample decay each secondcurie (Ci): 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 decays per second; becquerel (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay per second
Absorbed doseEnergy absorbed per kilogram of tissuerad; gray (Gy): 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad
Equivalent (biological) doseAbsorbed dose adjusted for how much harm that kind of radiation does to the bodyrem; sievert (Sv): 1 Sv = 100 rem; doses are usually given in millisieverts (mSv)

Medical tracers are usually described by activity, in millicuries (mCi) or megabecquerels (MBq). A person's dose is described in millisieverts. For scale, the average person in the United States receives about 3 mSv per year from natural sources such as radon, cosmic rays, rocks, and the potassium-40 in their own body, and about 6.2 mSv per year in all once medical imaging is included.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Converting activity units

Problem. A tracer has an activity of 10.0 mCi. What is its activity in becquerels?

Solution. 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq is an exact definition, so it does not limit the significant figures.

\( {10.0\;\text{mCi}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{Ci}}{1000\;\text{mCi}} \times \dfrac{3.7 \times 10^{10}\;\text{Bq}}{1\;\text{Ci}} = {3.70 \times 10^{8}\;\text{Bq}} \)

Check. A becquerel is a tiny unit (one decay per second), so even a small medical tracer has hundreds of millions of becquerels. Three significant figures come from 10.0 mCi.

Professor Tip

Keep the two families of units apart. Curies and becquerels describe the source: how fast it is decaying. Grays, sieverts, rads, and rems describe the person: how much radiation they absorbed. A memory hook: becquerel and curie were scientists who studied radioactive sources; the "s" in sievert can remind you of "someone's dose."

Detecting Radiation

All radiation detectors use the same idea: radiation knocks electrons off atoms and molecules in its path, making ions (that is why it is called ionizing radiation), or it gives them extra energy. The detector turns those ions or that energy into something you can read.

  • Geiger counter. A sealed metal tube holds a gas with a wire down the middle. When beta particles or gamma rays enter, they ionize the gas, and the ions and electrons let a small pulse of electric current flow. Each pulse is counted as a click. Geiger counters are used to check work areas and spills.
  • Scintillation counter. Radiation striking certain crystals, such as sodium iodide, makes them give off tiny flashes of light (scintillations). A light detector counts the flashes. The gamma cameras that take nuclear medicine images, such as Mateo's lung scan, are built around large scintillation crystals.
  • Film badges and dosimeters. Becquerel's fogged photographic plate became the film badge: the more radiation the film absorbs, the darker it gets when developed. Most of today's dosimeters (badges that record a person's total dose over time) use special crystals that store the energy and release it as light when they are read in a lab.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Every radiologic technologist and nuclear medicine technologist wears a dosimeter, a small badge that records the radiation dose the wearer receives, usually clipped at the collar or chest. A worker who declares a pregnancy is usually given a second badge worn at the waist, to track the dose to the fetus. The badges are exchanged on a schedule, often monthly or quarterly, and read by a laboratory, and the reports go to the facility's radiation safety officer. U.S. federal rules limit the occupational dose of a radiation worker to 50 mSv (5 rem) per year, and well-run departments keep doses far below that. The guiding principle is ALARA, "as low as reasonably achievable": every exposure, for patients and staff, should be justified and kept as small as the job allows. Technologists working with tracers also carry or keep nearby a survey meter, often a Geiger counter, to check their hands, work surfaces, and waste containers for contamination. (Context only; workers follow their facility's radiation safety program.)

Source: U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission, 10 CFR 20.1201, Occupational dose limits for adults (nrc.gov).

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Dental hygienists take radiographs (X-ray images) of the teeth every day, so it helps to know how small the doses are. According to RadiologyInfo.org (American College of Radiology and Radiological Society of North America), an intraoral dental X-ray gives an effective dose of about 0.005 mSv, about the same as 1 day of natural background radiation; a panoramic X-ray gives about 0.025 mSv (about 3 days); and a chest X-ray gives about 0.1 mSv (about 10 days). Natural background is about 3 mSv per year. Practices around patient shielding have changed: a 2024 expert panel report in the Journal of the American Dental Association concluded that thyroid collars are no longer recommended for dental imaging, partly because a collar can block the X-ray beam and force a retake. State rules and office policy still set the requirements in each practice, so hygienists follow those, and every image must be justified under ALARA. (Context only.)

Sources: RadiologyInfo.org, "Radiation Dose" (radiologyinfo.org); American Dental Association, "X-Rays/Radiographs" (ada.org).

Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up activity and dose: curies and becquerels describe the source; grays and sieverts describe the dose a person absorbs.
  • Thinking every isotope of an element is radioactive: carbon-12 is stable; carbon-14 is radioactive.
  • Getting neutrons wrong: neutrons = A − Z, and Z comes from the periodic table, not from the name.
  • Thinking stable nuclei always have equal numbers of protons and neutrons: that is true only for small nuclei; heavy stable nuclei have up to about 1.5 neutrons per proton.
  • Thinking radiation detectors "see" the radiation: they detect the ions or light the radiation produces.

Summary

  • Becquerel discovered radioactivity in uranium in 1896; Marie and Pierre Curie found polonium and radium and named the effect.
  • Proton repulsion grows as a nucleus gets bigger. Small stable nuclei have about 1 neutron per proton, heavy ones up to about 1.5; nuclei with too many neutrons tend to beta decay, too few to positron emission or electron capture, and very heavy ones to alpha decay.
  • Unstable nuclei give off radiation to become more stable; a radioisotope is a radioactive isotope, named by its mass number (iodine-131, \( {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} \)).
  • Activity: curie, becquerel (1 Bq = 1 decay/s). Absorbed dose: rad, gray. Biological dose: rem, sievert (mSv).
  • Geiger counters count ion pulses in a gas; scintillation counters count flashes of light; dosimeter badges record a worker's total dose.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Fluorine-18 is used in PET scans. Write its symbol and give the number of protons and neutrons.

Hint

The number in the name is the mass number A. Find Z (the protons) on the periodic table, then neutrons = A − Z.

Answer

Fluorine is element 9: \( {}^{18}_{9}\text{F} \), with 9 protons and 18 − 9 = 9 neutrons.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which unit would appear on (a) a radiologic technologist's monthly dosimeter report, and (b) the label of a vial of technetium-99m tracer: millicuries or millisieverts?

Hint

Ask what each unit describes: a curie measures how radioactive the source is (activity); a sievert measures the dose a person receives.

Answer

(a) Millisieverts, because the badge records the dose the person received. (b) Millicuries, because the label describes how radioactive the source is.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A sample has an activity of 2.50 × 106 Bq. What is its activity in microcuries (μCi)? (1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq, exact.)

Hint

Divide by the becquerels in one curie to get curies, then convert curies to microcuries (1 Ci = 106 μCi). Keep the sig figs of the given activity.

Answer

2.50 × 106 Bq ÷ 3.7 × 1010 Bq/Ci = 6.757 × 10−5 Ci = 67.6 μCi (three significant figures from 2.50).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Phosphorus-31 (15 protons, 16 neutrons) is stable. (a) Phosphorus-32 has one extra neutron. Which kind of decay would you expect, and what element forms? (b) Phosphorus-30 has one neutron too few. Which kind of decay would you expect?

Hint

Compare each isotope with the stable P-31. Which decay turns a neutron into a proton, and which turns a proton into a neutron? Then find the new Z on the periodic table.

Answer

(a) Too many neutrons: beta decay, in which a neutron becomes a proton. Z goes from 15 to 16, so sulfur-32 forms (Section 14.2, Example 6). (b) Too few neutrons: positron emission (or electron capture), in which a proton becomes a neutron, giving silicon-30.
