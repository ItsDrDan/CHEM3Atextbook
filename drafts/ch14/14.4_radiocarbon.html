Learning Objectives

  • Explain how carbon-14 forms in the atmosphere and why living things keep a constant amount of it.
  • Explain why the carbon-14 in a sample decreases after death.
  • Estimate the age of a once-living sample from the fraction of carbon-14 left, using whole numbers of half-lives.
  • Describe the limits of radiocarbon dating and name other isotopes used for dating.

In 1991, hikers in the Alps found a man frozen in a glacier. Was he a missing climber from the last century, or someone far older? Radiocarbon dating of his body and his gear answered the question: the "Iceman" died roughly 5300 years ago. The same method has dated the Dead Sea Scrolls, ancient campfires, and bones from early human sites. It works because every living thing carries a tiny, steady amount of radioactive carbon-14, and the clock starts when it dies.

Carbon-14 in Living Things

Carbon-14 is made continuously in the upper atmosphere. Cosmic rays from space produce neutrons, and when a neutron hits a nitrogen-14 nucleus, it knocks out a proton:

\( {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{6}\text{C} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \)

The carbon-14 combines with oxygen to form carbon dioxide, which mixes through the atmosphere. Only about one carbon atom in a trillion is carbon-14, but that small fraction stays nearly constant, because carbon-14 is made about as fast as it decays.

Plants take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis, so the carbon in their sugars, starch, and cellulose has the same carbon-14 to carbon-12 ratio as the air. Animals eat the plants (or eat animals that ate plants), so their tissues have that same ratio too. As long as an organism is alive, it keeps exchanging carbon with its surroundings, and its ratio stays constant.

Biology Connection: The Carbon in You

The carbon atoms in your proteins, DNA, and fats all came from food, and every food chain starts with plants (or algae) fixing carbon dioxide from the air by photosynthesis. Your body is also constantly breaking down and rebuilding its molecules, so as long as you eat, your carbon-14 to carbon-12 ratio matches that of the living world around you. When an organism dies, the exchange stops. The carbon-14 in its bones, shells, wood, seeds, or the cotton and linen made from its fibers is no longer replaced, and from that moment it decays with a half-life of 5730 years. Radiocarbon dating measures how far that clock has run.

Dating a Once-Living Sample

After death, carbon-14 decays back to nitrogen-14 by beta emission:

\( {}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

Scientists compare the carbon-14 in the sample (its ratio to carbon-12, or its activity per gram of carbon) with the carbon-14 in living material today. The fraction that remains tells how many half-lives have passed, and the age follows:

\( \text{age} = n \times 5730\ \text{years} \)

The half-life 5730 years has three significant figures (the trailing zero has no decimal point), so ages are written to three significant figures, in scientific notation when needed.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): A wooden tool

Problem. A wooden digging stick has one-fourth as much carbon-14 per gram of carbon as living wood. How old is it?

Solution. 1 → 1/2 → 1/4 is 2 half-lives.

\( \text{age} = 2 \times 5730\ \text{years} = 11{,}460\ \text{years} = 1.15 \times 10^{4}\ \text{years} \)

Check. One-fourth left means two halvings, so the age is two half-lives, a bit over 11,000 years.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): A bone from a cave

Problem. The collagen in an animal bone has 12.5% of the carbon-14 found in living bone. Estimate its age.

Solution. 100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% is 3 half-lives.

\( \text{age} = 3 \times 5730\ \text{years} = 17{,}190\ \text{years} = 1.72 \times 10^{4}\ \text{years} \)

Check. 12.5% = 1/8 = (1/2)3.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Using activity data

Problem. Carbon from a living plant gives 16.0 counts per minute per gram of carbon on a detector. Carbon from an ancient seed gives 1.00 count per minute per gram on the same detector. How old is the seed?

Solution. 16.0 → 8.00 → 4.00 → 2.00 → 1.00 counts per minute is 4 half-lives.

\( \text{age} = 4 \times 5730\ \text{years} = 22{,}920\ \text{years} = 2.29 \times 10^{4}\ \text{years} \)

Check. 1.00 is 1/16 of 16.0, and 1/16 = (1/2)4.

Professor Tip

Radiocarbon problems are half-life problems in disguise. Turn whatever you are given (a fraction, a percent, or two activities) into the number of halvings, n, then multiply by 5730 years. Do not try to date anything that was never alive: rocks, metal tools, and pottery clay contain no carbon from a living thing (though food residue or charcoal found with them can be dated).

Limits of Radiocarbon Dating

  • Age range. After about 9 half-lives (roughly 50,000 years), less than 0.2% of the original carbon-14 is left, too little to measure reliably. Older samples need other methods.
  • Once-living samples only. Wood, charcoal, bone, shell, seeds, cloth, paper, and leather can be dated; stone and metal cannot.
  • The starting ratio was not perfectly constant. The amount of carbon-14 in the air has varied somewhat over thousands of years. Scientists correct for this by calibrating against objects of known age, such as tree rings that can be counted year by year. Since the 1800s, burning fossil fuels, whose carbon is far too old to contain any carbon-14, has diluted the carbon-14 in the air, and nuclear weapons tests in the 1950s and 1960s added extra carbon-14.

Other Isotopes Used for Dating

The same half-life reasoning works with other radioisotopes. The choice depends on how old the object might be: the half-life should be comparable to the age being measured. The table below lists a few examples.

Isotopes used for dating
Isotope (decays to)Half-lifeUsed to date
Hydrogen-3, tritium (helium-3)12.3 yearsHow recently groundwater fell as rain; aged wines
Carbon-14 (nitrogen-14)5730 yearsOnce-living things up to about 50,000 years old
Potassium-40 (argon-40)1.25 × 109 yearsVolcanic rocks, layers around ancient human fossils
Uranium-238 (lead-206)4.5 × 109 yearsThe oldest rocks and meteorites; the age of Earth, about 4.5 billion years

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking carbon-14 starts decaying only at death: it decays all the time; while alive, the organism keeps replacing it.
  • Dating rocks or metal with carbon-14: only once-living material works.
  • Multiplying the fraction by 5730: first find the number of half-lives, then multiply n by 5730 years.
  • Too many significant figures: 5730 years has three, so 2 × 5730 = 1.15 × 104 years.

Summary

  • Carbon-14 forms when cosmic-ray neutrons hit nitrogen-14; it enters living things as CO2 through photosynthesis and food chains.
  • Living things keep a constant carbon-14 to carbon-12 ratio; after death, carbon-14 decays (half-life 5730 years) and the ratio falls.
  • Age = n × 5730 years, where n is the number of halvings from living material to the sample.
  • Radiocarbon dating works to about 50,000 years and only for once-living material; potassium-40 and uranium-238 date much older rocks.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A piece of charcoal from an ancient fire pit has 50.0% of the carbon-14 of living wood. How old is it?

Hint

What fraction of carbon-14 is left after exactly one half-life? Compare that with the charcoal's percent.

Answer

50.0% is one half-life: 5730 years.

One half-life leaves 50% of the original carbon-14, and the half-life of carbon-14 is 5730 years.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A shell has 6.25% of the carbon-14 of a living shell. Estimate its age.

Hint

Build a percent arrow map from the full amount down to 6.25%. Count the arrows, multiply by 5730 years, and report three significant figures.

Answer

100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 → 6.25% is 4 half-lives; 4 × 5730 = 22,920 years = 2.29 × 104 years.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Could carbon-14 be used to find the age of (a) a bronze spearpoint, (b) a woven basket, (c) a dinosaur bone about 70 million years old? Explain.

Hint

Carbon-14 dating works only on things that were once alive, and only up to about 50,000 years. Check each object against both conditions.

Answer

(a) No: bronze was never alive. (b) Yes: the plant fibers were once alive, and the basket is likely within the 50,000-year range. (c) No: after millions of years essentially no carbon-14 remains; rock layers around it are dated with longer-lived isotopes such as potassium-40.
