14.1 Radioactivity and Detecting Radiation

Problem 14.1

What did Henri Becquerel observe in 1896 that led to the discovery of radioactivity?

A. A glowing vacuum tube gave off invisible rays that passed through flesh but not bone.
B. A photographic plate wrapped in black paper was fogged by a uranium salt placed on top of it, with no light and no electricity.
C. Uranium ore contained two new elements, polonium and radium, that gave off stronger rays than uranium.
D. Aluminum bombarded with alpha particles became radioactive.

Hint

Radioactivity comes from the atoms themselves. Which choices describe other discoveries: X-rays, the Curies' new elements, or radioactivity made by bombardment?

Answer

B. The uranium itself was giving off rays that went through the paper and exposed the plate. Nothing had to switch it on, which is the hallmark of radioactivity: it comes from the atoms (their nuclei) themselves.

  • A is Röntgen's discovery of X-rays (1895). X-rays come from a tube that needs electricity, not from radioactive atoms.
  • C is the later work of Marie and Pierre Curie (1898), who named the effect radioactivity.
  • D is Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot's discovery of artificial radioactivity (1934).

Problem 14.2

Write the nuclear symbol (with A and Z) for each radioisotope and give the number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus: (a) technetium-99m, used in most nuclear medicine scans; (b) cobalt-60, used to sterilize medical supplies; (c) radon-222, the radioactive gas found in some homes; (d) americium-241, used in household smoke detectors.

Hint

The number in each name is A. Find Z on the periodic table; neutrons = A − Z. The "m" does not change either count.

Answer

Look up Z (the number of protons) on the periodic table; the name gives A. Neutrons = A − Z.

(a) \( {}^{99\text{m}}_{43}\text{Tc} \qquad \text{(b)}\;{}^{60}_{27}\text{Co} \qquad \text{(c)}\;{}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \qquad \text{(d)}\;{}^{241}_{95}\text{Am} \)

(a) 43 protons, 99 − 43 = 56 neutrons. The "m" (metastable) means the nucleus has extra energy to release as a gamma ray; it does not change the count of protons or neutrons.

(b) 27 protons, 60 − 27 = 33 neutrons.

(c) 86 protons, 222 − 86 = 136 neutrons.

(d) 95 protons, 241 − 95 = 146 neutrons. Every isotope beyond bismuth (Z = 83), including radon and americium, is radioactive.

Problem 14.3

Which unit measures the absorbed dose, the energy of radiation absorbed per kilogram of tissue (1 unit = 1 J/kg)?

A. Becquerel (Bq)
B. Gray (Gy)
C. Curie (Ci)
D. Sievert (Sv)

Hint

Sort the four units: which measure the activity of a source, which measures energy absorbed per kilogram, and which adjusts for biological effect?

Answer

B. 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 rad. The gray describes the energy the tissue absorbed.

  • A measures activity, how many nuclei in the source decay each second (1 Bq = 1 decay per second). It says nothing about how much energy a person absorbed.
  • C also measures the activity of the source (1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 decays per second).
  • D is a dose unit, but it is the equivalent (biological) dose: the absorbed dose adjusted for how much harm that kind of radiation does. It is the unit on a dosimeter report, not the plain energy per kilogram.

Problem 14.4

(a) A technetium-99m dose for a bone scan is labeled 25.0 mCi. What is its activity in megabecquerels (MBq)? (b) A fluorine-18 dose for a PET scan is labeled 555 MBq. What is its activity in millicuries?

Hint

(a) Go mCi → Ci → Bq → MBq with conversion factors (1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq). (b) First find how many MBq equal 1 mCi.

Answer

(a) \( {25.0\;\text{mCi}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{Ci}}{1000\;\text{mCi}} \times \dfrac{3.7 \times 10^{10}\;\text{Bq}}{1\;\text{Ci}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{MBq}}{10^{6}\;\text{Bq}} = {925\;\text{MBq}} \)

(b) \( {555\;\text{MBq}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mCi}}{37\;\text{MBq}} = {15.0\;\text{mCi}} \)

(a) 925 MBq; (b) 15.0 mCi. The curie–becquerel relationship is an exact definition, so the three significant figures come from 25.0 mCi and 555 MBq. A check: 1 mCi = 3.7 × 107 Bq = 37 MBq, so the number of MBq is always 37 times the number of mCi.

Problem 14.5

A dental hygienist's dosimeter report shows a dose of 0.85 mSv for the year. (a) Express this dose in millirem (mrem). (b) What percent of the federal occupational limit of 50 mSv per year is it? (c) About how many chest X-rays (about 0.1 mSv each) give the same dose? (d) Natural background gives about 3 mSv per year. Is her work dose larger or smaller than her background dose?

Hint

(a) 1 mSv = 100 mrem. (b) Divide by the limit and multiply by 100%. (c) Divide by the dose of one X-ray. (d) Compare with 3 mSv.

Answer

(a) \( {0.85\;\text{mSv}} \times \dfrac{100\;\text{mrem}}{1\;\text{mSv}} = {85\;\text{mrem}} \)    (1 Sv = 100 rem, so 1 mSv = 100 mrem)

(b) \( \dfrac{0.85\;\text{mSv}}{50\;\text{mSv}} \times 100\% = 1.7\% \)

(a) 85 mrem; (b) 1.7% of the limit.

(c) 0.85 mSv ÷ 0.1 mSv ≈ 8.5, so about 8 or 9 chest X-rays (the 0.1 mSv value is only approximate).

(d) Smaller: 0.85 mSv is less than a third of the roughly 3 mSv she receives each year from natural sources. Small work doses like this are the goal of ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable). (Context only; workers follow their facility's radiation safety program.)

Problem 14.6

The potassium-40 in an adult's body gives an activity of about 4.0 × 103 Bq, and the potassium-40 in one banana gives about 15 Bq. (a) Express the body's activity in microcuries (μCi). (b) Express the banana's activity in picocuries (pCi). (c) About how many bananas would have the same potassium-40 activity as an adult's body? (d) Is eating a banana a reason to worry about radiation? Explain briefly.

Hint

(a) Bq → Ci → μCi with 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq. (b) 1 pCi = 0.037 Bq. (c) Divide the body's activity by one banana's. Keep two significant figures.

Answer

(a) \( {4.0 \times 10^{3}\;\text{Bq}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{Ci}}{3.7 \times 10^{10}\;\text{Bq}} \times \dfrac{10^{6}\;\mu\text{Ci}}{1\;\text{Ci}} = 0.11\;\mu\text{Ci} \)

(b) \( {15\;\text{Bq}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{pCi}}{0.037\;\text{Bq}} = {4.1 \times 10^{2}\;\text{pCi}} \)

(a) 0.11 μCi (0.108 unrounded); (b) 4.1 × 102 pCi (405 unrounded); two significant figures, set by 4.0 × 103 Bq and 15 Bq.

(c) 4.0 × 103 Bq ÷ 15 Bq ≈ 267, so about 270 bananas.

(d) No. The body keeps its potassium level steady, so eating a banana does not build up potassium-40; any extra potassium is excreted. This activity is part of the natural background radiation everyone receives, and the curie and becquerel describe the source, not a harmful dose.

Problem 14.7

The gamma camera that makes the images in a nuclear medicine scan is built around which kind of detector?

A. A Geiger counter, a gas-filled tube that clicks each time radiation ionizes the gas
B. A scintillation detector, a large crystal (such as sodium iodide) that gives off flashes of light where gamma rays strike it
C. A film badge, which darkens in proportion to the total radiation it absorbs
D. A dosimeter badge clipped to the patient's collar

Hint

An image needs to show where the gamma rays came from, live. Which detector records the position of each gamma ray as it strikes?

Answer

B. Gamma rays leaving the patient strike the crystal and make tiny flashes of light (scintillations). Light detectors behind the crystal record where each flash happened, and the computer builds those positions into an image of where the tracer went.

  • A counts radiation but cannot show where it came from. Geiger counters are survey meters for checking hands, work surfaces, and spills.
  • C records only a total dose over weeks, after it is developed. It cannot make a live image.
  • D records the dose received by the person wearing it, usually a worker, not the location of a tracer inside a patient.

14.2 Radioactive Decay and Nuclear Equations

Problem 14.8

Polonium-210, an alpha emitter found in tobacco smoke, decays by alpha emission. What is the other product?

\( {}^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow \;? + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

A. \( {}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb} \)
B. \( {}^{206}_{83}\text{Bi} \)
C. \( {}^{210}_{82}\text{Pb} \)
D. \( {}^{214}_{86}\text{Rn} \)

Hint

Use the top-and-bottom rule: subtract the alpha particle's 4 from the top and 2 from the bottom. Then check each choice's top and bottom numbers.

Answer

A. Mass number: 210 = ? + 4, so ? = 206. Atomic number: 84 = ? + 2, so ? = 82. Element 82 is lead.

\( {}^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow {}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

Check: top, 206 + 4 = 210; bottom, 82 + 2 = 84.

  • B lowers the atomic number by only 1. An alpha particle carries away 2 protons, so Z drops by 2.
  • C changes Z correctly but leaves the mass number at 210, so the top numbers do not add up: 210 + 4 = 214, not 210. Mass number must be conserved.
  • D adds the alpha particle to the parent instead of subtracting it. In alpha decay the alpha particle leaves, so the daughter is lighter.

Problem 14.9

Write a balanced nuclear equation for the beta decay of each radioisotope: (a) strontium-90, a fission product in nuclear waste; (b) hydrogen-3 (tritium), used in glowing exit signs; (c) cesium-137, another fission product (its daughter forms in the metastable state, so write it with an "m").

Hint

In beta decay the top number stays the same and Z goes up by 1. Find each parent's Z, add 1, and look up the new element.

Answer

In beta decay a neutron becomes a proton and the electron leaves as \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \). The mass number stays the same and the atomic number goes up by 1, one place to the right on the periodic table.

(a) \( {}^{90}_{38}\text{Sr} \rightarrow {}^{90}_{39}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

(b) \( {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{3}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

(c) \( {}^{137}_{55}\text{Cs} \rightarrow {}^{137\text{m}}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

(a) Strontium (38) becomes yttrium-90 (39). (b) Hydrogen (1) becomes helium-3 (2). (c) Cesium (55) becomes barium-137m (56), which then gives off a gamma ray. Check each: the top numbers match (90, 3, 137), and the bottom numbers add up: 39 + (−1) = 38, 2 + (−1) = 1, 56 + (−1) = 55.

Problem 14.10

Carbon-11 is a positron emitter used in some PET scans. What does it become?

\( {}^{11}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow \;? + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \)

A. \( {}^{11}_{7}\text{N} \)
B. \( {}^{11}_{5}\text{B} \)
C. \( {}^{10}_{5}\text{B} \)
D. \( {}^{7}_{4}\text{Be} \)

Hint

A positron has 0 on top and +1 on the bottom. Which choice keeps A the same and makes the bottom numbers add up?

Answer

B. Mass number: 11 = ? + 0, so ? = 11. Atomic number: 6 = ? + 1, so ? = 5, boron.

\( {}^{11}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{11}_{5}\text{B} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \)

  • A treats the positron as a beta particle and raises Z. A positron counts +1 on the bottom, so the daughter has one fewer proton.
  • C lowers the mass number by 1, as if the positron had a mass number of 1. Positrons, like electrons, have mass number 0.
  • D is what alpha decay would give (A − 4, Z − 2), not positron emission.

Problem 14.11

Thallium-201, used in some heart scans, decays by electron capture. (a) Write the balanced nuclear equation. (b) Electron capture and positron emission both change the nucleus in the same way. What is that change, and how do the two equations look different?

Hint

(a) In electron capture, write 0−1e on the left, as a reactant. (b) Compare what happens to A and Z in each, and which side the electron or positron appears on.

Answer

(a) Thallium is element 81. In electron capture the electron is a reactant, written on the left:

\( {}^{201}_{81}\text{Tl} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \rightarrow {}^{201}_{80}\text{Hg} \)

Check: top, 201 + 0 = 201; bottom, 81 + (−1) = 80, mercury.

(b) In both, a proton becomes a neutron: the mass number stays the same and the atomic number goes down by 1. In electron capture the nucleus pulls in an inner electron, so \( {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \) appears on the left. In positron emission a positron is thrown out, so \( {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \) appears on the right.

Problem 14.12

Complete each equation and name the kind of decay.

(a) \( {}^{214}_{82}\text{Pb} \rightarrow {}^{214}_{83}\text{Bi} + \;? \)    (b) \( {}^{214}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow {}^{210}_{82}\text{Pb} + \;? \)

(c) \( {}^{13}_{7}\text{N} \rightarrow {}^{13}_{6}\text{C} + \;? \)    (d) \( {}^{137\text{m}}_{56}\text{Ba} \rightarrow {}^{137}_{56}\text{Ba} + \;? \)

Hint

For each, subtract the right side from the left: top numbers give the missing A, bottom numbers give the missing Z. Match A and Z to a particle.

Answer

Subtract to find the missing mass number (top) and atomic number (bottom).

(a) A = 214 − 214 = 0; Z = 82 − 83 = −1: a beta particle, beta decay.

\( {}^{214}_{82}\text{Pb} \rightarrow {}^{214}_{83}\text{Bi} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

(b) A = 214 − 210 = 4; Z = 84 − 82 = 2: an alpha particle, alpha decay.

\( {}^{214}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow {}^{210}_{82}\text{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

(c) A = 13 − 13 = 0; Z = 7 − 6 = +1: a positron, positron emission.

\( {}^{13}_{7}\text{N} \rightarrow {}^{13}_{6}\text{C} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \)

(d) A = 0 and Z = 0, and the element did not change: a gamma ray, gamma emission from a metastable nucleus.

Lead-214 and polonium-214 are two of the short-lived decay products of radon (Section 14.6); nitrogen-13 is a PET tracer.

Problem 14.13

The americium-241 in smoke detectors is made in nuclear reactors. It forms when a heavier isotope of another element undergoes beta decay. Identify that parent nucleus and write the balanced equation.

\( ? \rightarrow {}^{241}_{95}\text{Am} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

Work backward: add the top numbers and the bottom numbers on the right side. The parent has those totals; look up the element by its Z.

Problem 14.14

Complete each bombardment reaction. (a) In boron neutron capture, a boron-10 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into lithium-7 and one other particle. (b) In a reactor, molybdenum-98 absorbs a neutron and gives off a gamma ray; the product is the parent of technetium-99m.

(a) \( {}^{10}_{5}\text{B} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{7}_{3}\text{Li} + \;? \)    (b) \( {}^{98}_{42}\text{Mo} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow \;? + {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \)

Hint

Total the top and bottom numbers on the left, then subtract what is already on the right. A gamma ray (0 on top, 0 on bottom) adds nothing.

Answer

(a) Left: A = 10 + 1 = 11, Z = 5 + 0 = 5. Right so far: A = 7, Z = 3. Missing: A = 4, Z = 2, an alpha particle.

(b) Left: A = 98 + 1 = 99, Z = 42 + 0 = 42. The gamma ray adds nothing. Missing: A = 99, Z = 42, molybdenum-99.

Adding a neutron raises the mass number by 1 without changing the element. Molybdenum-99 then beta decays to technetium-99m (Section 14.2).

Problem 14.15

Technetium-99m gives off a gamma ray: \( {}^{99\text{m}}_{43}\text{Tc} \rightarrow {}^{99}_{43}\text{Tc} + {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \). Which statement about this change is correct?

A. The mass number drops by 1, because the gamma ray carries away part of the nucleus.
B. The technetium becomes ruthenium, because the gamma ray raises the atomic number by 1.
C. The nucleus releases extra energy; the mass number, atomic number, and element all stay the same.
D. The gamma ray is an electron thrown out of the nucleus.

Hint

Compare the top and bottom numbers on both sides of the equation. What does the m in 99m mean, and what does the gamma ray carry away?

Answer

C. A gamma ray is high-energy electromagnetic radiation with mass number 0 and charge 0. The metastable nucleus simply drops to a lower-energy state, so it is still technetium-99, with 43 protons and 56 neutrons.

  • A gives the gamma ray a mass number. It has none, so A stays 99.
  • B gives the gamma ray a charge. With Z = 0 it cannot change the element; raising Z by 1 is beta decay.
  • D describes a beta particle. A gamma ray is light, not a particle with mass or charge.

Problem 14.16

Which radiation has the highest ionizing power and the lowest penetrating power?

A. Alpha particles
B. Beta particles
C. Gamma rays
D. They are all the same; ionizing and penetrating power do not depend on the kind of radiation.

Hint

Ionizing power and penetrating power run in opposite directions. Which radiation is heaviest and most highly charged?

Answer

A. An alpha particle is heavy, slow, and doubly charged, so it ionizes nearly every molecule it meets and loses its energy within a few centimeters of air or the dead outer layer of skin. Ionizing power and penetrating power run in opposite directions: alpha > beta > gamma for ionizing, gamma > beta > alpha for penetrating.

  • B is in the middle on both scales.
  • C is the reverse: gamma rays are the most penetrating and the least ionizing, because they have no mass and no charge and pass most atoms without interacting.
  • D is wrong; the differences in mass and charge are exactly why they differ, and why each needs a different shield.

Problem 14.18

A home smoke detector holds a tiny amount of americium-241, an alpha emitter, inside a small metal chamber in a plastic case. Why is the detector safe to mount on a ceiling?

A. Alpha particles are the most penetrating radiation, so they pass straight through people without leaving any energy behind.
B. Alpha particles cannot get through the chamber, the plastic case, or more than a few centimeters of air, so none reach people below; the hazard would come only from breaking open the source and swallowing or inhaling it.
C. Americium-241 stops being radioactive once it is sealed in a chamber.
D. Smoke detectors use X-rays made by the americium nucleus, and X-rays are harmless.

Hint

Rank alpha, beta, and gamma by penetrating power. What could stop an alpha particle before it reaches someone below the ceiling?

Answer

B. Alpha particles have the lowest penetrating power. Inside the chamber they ionize the air so a small current flows; smoke particles interrupt the current and set off the alarm. The particles never leave the detector.

  • A has penetrating power backward. Alpha is the least penetrating radiation, and when it does hit tissue it is the most ionizing.
  • C is wrong because radioactivity is a property of the nucleus. Sealing a source in a container changes nothing about its decay; it only blocks the alpha particles.
  • D is wrong twice: X-rays come from fast electrons hitting a metal target in an X-ray tube, not from nuclei, and they are ionizing, not harmless. Americium-241 gives off alpha particles (and some weak gamma rays).

14.3 Half-Life

Problem 14.19

A sealed glass bulb holds 48.0 ng of radon-222 (half-life 3.8 days). How much radon-222 remains after 11.4 days?

A. 6.00 ng
B. 8.00 ng
C. 24.0 ng
D. None; it has all decayed.

Hint

Count half-lives (time ÷ half-life), then halve 48.0 ng that many times. Each half-life halves what is left, not the original.

Answer

A.

\( n = \dfrac{11.4\;\text{days}}{3.8\;\text{days}} = 3 \qquad {48.0\;\text{ng}} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{3} = {48.0\;\text{ng}} \times \tfrac{1}{8} = {6.00\;\text{ng}} \)

48.0 ng → 24.0 ng → 12.0 ng → 6.00 ng

  • B divides by 2 × 3 = 6 instead of by 23 = 8. Each half-life halves what is left, so the factors multiply: (1/2)(1/2)(1/2).
  • C stops after one half-life. Three half-lives have passed.
  • D subtracts half of the original (24.0 ng) each period: 48.0 → 24.0 → 0. Each half-life removes half of what is left, so some always remains.

Problem 14.20

A biology research lab receives a vial of phosphorus-32 (half-life 14.3 days) with an activity of 12.0 mCi, used to label DNA. What is its activity 57.2 days later?

Hint

Find the number of half-lives: time ÷ half-life. Then halve 12.0 mCi once per half-life with an arrow map.

Answer

\( n = \dfrac{57.2\;\text{days}}{14.3\;\text{days}} = 4 \qquad {12.0\;\text{mCi}} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{4} = {12.0\;\text{mCi}} \times \tfrac{1}{16} = {0.750\;\text{mCi}} \)

12.0 → 6.00 → 3.00 → 1.50 → 0.750 mCi

0.750 mCi, three significant figures from 12.0 mCi (n is an exact count). Check: four arrows, four halvings, and 0.750 is one-sixteenth of 12.0.

Problem 14.21

A gamma irradiator that sterilizes surgical gloves and spices uses cobalt-60 (half-life 5.27 years). (a) How many years after installation is cobalt-60 added? (b) What is the activity then?

(a) One-fourth is (1/2)2, so 2 half-lives:

\( \text{time} = n \times t_{1/2} = 2 \times {5.27\;\text{years}} = {10.5\;\text{years}} \)

(b) \( {4.0 \times 10^{5}\;\text{Ci}} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2} = {1.0 \times 10^{5}\;\text{Ci}} \)

(a) 10.5 years (10.54 unrounded; three significant figures from 5.27 years); (b) 1.0 × 105 Ci (two significant figures from 4.0 × 105 Ci). A long half-life is why one source can run a sterilizing plant for years.

(a) Halve once per 6-hour half-life: 1:00 p.m., 3.20 mCi; 7:00 p.m., 1.60 mCi; 1:00 a.m., 0.800 mCi; 7:00 a.m. the next day, 0.400 mCi.

\( n = \dfrac{30\;\text{h}}{6.0\;\text{h}} = 5 \qquad {6.40\;\text{mCi}} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{5} = {6.40\;\text{mCi}} \times \tfrac{1}{32} = {0.200\;\text{mCi}} \)

0.200 mCi, which is 1/32 of the starting activity. A day and a quarter after the scan, about 97% of the tracer's technetium-99m has decayed, and his body also eliminates some of the tracer, so the real amount is even smaller. This is why a short half-life is chosen. (The case is for practicing chemistry only; it is not medical advice.)

Problem 14.26

A nuclear pharmacy has two vials of iodine-131 (half-life 8.0 days): one with 10.0 mCi and one with 2.0 mCi. Which statement is correct?

A. Each vial loses half of its activity in 8.0 days: the larger one falls to 5.00 mCi and the smaller one to 1.0 mCi.
B. The larger vial takes longer to lose half of its activity, because it has more nuclei to decay.
C. The smaller vial is completely gone after 8.0 days, because it has so little to start with.
D. Keeping the vials in a refrigerator would lengthen the half-life and slow the decay.

Hint

Does the half-life of an isotope depend on how much you have, or on temperature and storage? Apply the definition of half-life to each vial.

Answer

A. The half-life is a property of the isotope. It does not depend on how much of the isotope is present, on temperature, or on what compound it is in, so both vials halve in 8.0 days.

  • B makes the half-life depend on the amount. A bigger sample has more decays per second, but the fraction that decays in each half-life is the same.
  • C confuses the half-life with the time to decay completely. After one half-life, half (1.0 mCi) remains.
  • D applies the rule for chemical reaction rates to nuclear decay. Cooling does not change what happens inside a nucleus.

Problem 14.27

Problem 14.29

B. Charred acorns from a hearth used about 9000 years ago

D. A copper bead

Hint

Carbon-14 dating needs something that was once alive and an age under about 50,000 years. Check each choice against both.

Problem 14.30

The Iceman found in an Alpine glacier in 1991 died about 5300 years ago. Compared with the carbon-14 per gram of carbon in living tissue, how much carbon-14 is in his tissue?

A. More than 50%
B. Exactly 50%
C. 25%
D. All of it, 100%, because carbon-14 does not decay while it is frozen in ice

Compare 5300 years with the half-life of carbon-14, 5730 years. How much carbon-14 is left after exactly one half-life, and has that much time passed yet?

Answer

A. 5300 years is a little less than one half-life (5730 years), so less than half of his carbon-14 has decayed. More than 50% remains.

  • B would be true only if exactly one half-life, 5730 years, had passed.

Problem 14.31

(a) Explain why a living tree keeps a nearly constant ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12. (b) Explain why the ratio in a wooden beam cut from the tree falls over time. (c) Write the nuclear equation for what happens to the carbon-14 in the beam.

Hint

(a) What keeps adding fresh carbon-14 to a living tree? (b) What changes once it is cut? (c) Carbon-14 is a beta emitter; use the top-and-bottom rule.

Answer

(a) Carbon-14 is made continuously in the upper atmosphere when cosmic-ray neutrons hit nitrogen-14, and it becomes part of CO2. The tree takes in CO2 by photosynthesis the whole time it is alive, so the carbon-14 that decays in its wood is replaced, and its ratio stays the same as the air's.

(b) Once the tree is cut, the wood takes in no new carbon. The carbon-14 already there keeps decaying with a half-life of 5730 years, and nothing replaces it, so the ratio falls.

(c) Carbon-14 is a beta emitter:

\( {}^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\text{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

Carbon-14 decays all the time, in the living tree too; death only stops the replacement.

Problem 14.32

Problem 14.36

Volcanic rock layers are dated with potassium-40 (half-life 1.25 × 109 years), part of which decays to argon-40 by electron capture. (a) Write the balanced equation for the electron capture. (b) A volcanic rock has 25.0% of the potassium-40 it had when it formed. How old is the rock?

14.5 Fission, Nuclear Power, and Fusion

Problems marked Challenge ask you to balance a full fission or fusion equation. They use the same rules as the decay, electron capture, and bombardment equations in Section 14.2, which are the main skills; try them once those feel easy.

Problem 14.37

Which statement correctly describes how the Sun produces its energy?

A. Fission: heavy uranium nuclei in the Sun's core split into smaller nuclei.
B. Fusion: hydrogen nuclei join to form helium, and a little mass becomes energy.
C. Burning: hydrogen combines with oxygen in a chemical reaction.
D. Fusion: helium nuclei split into hydrogen nuclei, releasing neutrons.

Hint

Is the Sun's energy from a chemical or a nuclear change? Which nuclear process joins light nuclei and which splits heavy ones, and what is the Sun mostly made of?

Answer

B. In the Sun's core, at about 15 million °C, hydrogen nuclei fuse in steps whose overall result is \( 4\,{}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + 2\,{}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \). The products have slightly less mass than the reactants, and that missing mass is released as energy (E = mc2).

  • A swaps fission and fusion. Fission (splitting heavy nuclei) powers nuclear reactors, not stars.
  • C is a chemical reaction. Burning involves only electrons, and the Sun has far too little oxygen and would have burned out long ago; nuclear reactions release millions of times more energy.
  • D runs fusion backward. Fusion joins light nuclei into heavier ones; splitting helium would absorb energy, not release it.

Problem 14.38 (Challenge)

How many neutrons are released in this fission of uranium-235?

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{137}_{52}\text{Te} + {}^{97}_{40}\text{Zr} + x\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Hint

Challenge item: the bottom numbers already balance. Total the top numbers on each side; the difference is the number of neutrons.

Answer

Atomic numbers: 0 + 92 = 92 on the left, and 52 + 40 = 92 on the right, so they already balance (neutrons have Z = 0). Mass numbers: left, 1 + 235 = 236; right, 137 + 97 + x = 234 + x. So x = 236 − 234 = 2.

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{137}_{52}\text{Te} + {}^{97}_{40}\text{Zr} + 2\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Check: 137 + 97 + 2 = 236. Two or three neutrons per fission is typical, and they are what can keep a chain reaction going.

Problem 14.39 (Challenge)

Iodine-131 is one of the fission products of uranium-235. What is the missing fragment in this fission?

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} + \;? + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

A. \( {}^{102}_{39}\text{Y} \)
B. \( {}^{104}_{39}\text{Y} \)
C. \( {}^{101}_{39}\text{Y} \)
D. \( {}^{105}_{39}\text{Y} \)

Hint

Challenge item: total the left side (include the neutron that starts fission). Subtract the iodine and all three neutrons from the top, and the iodine's Z from the bottom.

Answer

A. Left: A = 1 + 235 = 236, Z = 92. Right so far: A = 131 + 3 × 1 = 134, Z = 53. Missing: A = 236 − 134 = 102, Z = 92 − 53 = 39, yttrium.

\( {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{131}_{53}\text{I} + {}^{102}_{39}\text{Y} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

  • B counts the three neutrons as only one mass unit. The coefficient 3 means 3 neutrons, 3 × 1 = 3 toward the mass numbers.
  • C forgets the neutron on the left. Fission starts when uranium-235 absorbs a neutron, so the left side totals 236, not 235.
  • D leaves out the three product neutrons entirely: 236 − 131 = 105.

Problem 14.40 (Challenge)

Complete each fusion reaction. Both are steps in the Sun's fusion of hydrogen.

(a) \( {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow \;? + {}^{0}_{0}\gamma \)    (b) \( {}^{3}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{3}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + 2\;? \)

(a) Left: A = 2 + 1 = 3, Z = 1 + 1 = 2. The gamma ray adds nothing, so the product has A = 3 and Z = 2, helium-3.

(b) Left: A = 3 + 3 = 6, Z = 2 + 2 = 4. Right so far: A = 4, Z = 2. The two missing particles together have A = 2 and Z = 2, so each has A = 1 and Z = 1: protons, \( {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \).

Problem 14.41

An operator at a nuclear power plant needs to slow the chain reaction in the reactor core. Which part of the reactor does she adjust, and how?

A. She lowers the control rods further into the core, because they absorb neutrons.
B. She lowers more fuel rods into the core, because extra uranium absorbs the extra neutrons.
C. She raises the control rods out of the core, because they release neutrons.
D. She opens the containment building, so neutrons can escape into the air.

Hint

Fewer free neutrons means fewer new fissions. Which part of the reactor controls how many neutrons are free, and which way should it move?

Answer

A. Control rods are made of neutron-absorbing elements such as boron or cadmium. The deeper they sit in the core, the more neutrons they absorb, so fewer neutrons are left to cause new fissions.

  • B mixes up fuel rods and control rods. More uranium-235 would make more fissions and speed the reaction up.
  • C has the rods backward: raising them out of the core lets more neutrons cause fissions, which speeds up the reaction. Control rods absorb neutrons; they do not release them.
  • D would release radioactive material. The containment building exists to keep it inside.

Problem 14.42

Where does the energy released in nuclear fission come from?

A. The uranium burns with oxygen inside the reactor.
B. The products have slightly less mass than the reactants, and the missing mass is converted to energy (E = mc2).
C. The products have more mass than the reactants, and energy is created along with the new mass.
D. All of it comes from the energy of the neutron that starts the fission.

Hint

Fission is a nuclear change, not burning. Compare the total mass of the products with the reactants: which way does it change when energy is released?

Answer

B. Adding up the masses very precisely, the fission fragments and neutrons weigh a little less than the uranium-235 and the neutron that started the fission. Because c2 is so large, that tiny mass becomes an enormous energy, millions of times more per gram than burning coal or natural gas.

  • A describes a chemical reaction. A reactor burns nothing; fission is a change in the nucleus.
  • C has the mass change backward. Mass is lost, not gained, when energy is released.
  • D cannot be right, because the neutron that starts fission is a slow neutron with very little energy, yet each fission releases a huge amount.

14.6 Radiation and Health

Problem 14.44

Which tissue is most sensitive to radiation damage?

A. Bone marrow, which makes blood cells
B. Adult nerve cells in the brain
C. Mature muscle cells
D. The dead outer layer of the skin

Hint

Radiation damages DNA most in cells that divide often. Which tissue makes new blood cells all the time?

Answer

A. Bone marrow cells divide rapidly. When radiation or the free radicals it makes damage DNA, a rapidly dividing cell copies the damaged DNA before it can be repaired. Falling blood cell counts are one of the first signs of a large dose.

  • B and C are made of cells that rarely divide, so they have time to repair damage before the DNA is copied. They are among the least sensitive tissues.
  • D is already dead and does not divide; it is what stops alpha particles from reaching living cells.

Problem 14.45

A patient who has received iodine-131 therapy (half-life 8.0 days) gives off gamma rays. Suppose the dose rate is 24 μSv/h at 1.0 m from the patient (a made-up value for practice). (a) What is the dose rate at 2.0 m? (b) What dose does a nurse receive standing 2.0 m away for 15 min, and how does that compare with standing 1.0 m away for the same time? (c) Ignoring the iodine the body eliminates, what is the dose rate at 1.0 m 8.0 days later?

(a) \( 24\;\mu\text{Sv/h} \times \tfrac{1}{4} = 6.0\;\mu\text{Sv/h} \)    (twice the distance gives one-fourth the dose rate)

(b) \( 6.0\;\tfrac{\mu\text{Sv}}{\text{h}} \times {0.25\;\text{h}} = 1.5\;\mu\text{Sv} \qquad \text{at 1.0 m:}\;24\;\tfrac{\mu\text{Sv}}{\text{h}} \times {0.25\;\text{h}} = 6.0\;\mu\text{Sv} \)

(a) 6.0 μSv/h; (b) 1.5 μSv, one-fourth of the 6.0 μSv she would get at 1.0 m (15 min = 0.25 h). Distance and time both lower the dose.

(c) 8.0 days is one half-life, so the dose rate is about 24 × 1/2 = 12 μSv/h, and really less, because the body also eliminates iodine in urine, saliva, and sweat. (Context only; care follows the order and the facility's radiation safety protocol.)

(a) \( 6.5\;\tfrac{\text{pCi}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{0.037\;\text{decays/s}}{1\;\text{pCi}} = 0.24\;\tfrac{\text{decays/s}}{\text{L}} \qquad 0.2405 \times 3600\;\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{h}} = 8.7 \times 10^{2}\;\tfrac{\text{decays/h}}{\text{L}} \)

(a) About 0.24 decays per second per liter, or 8.7 × 102 decays per hour per liter (two significant figures, set by 6.5 pCi/L).

(b) Yes, 6.5 pCi/L is above 4.0 pCi/L, so the EPA recommends fixing the home, usually with a fan and pipe that vent soil gas from under the foundation.

(c) \( {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow {}^{218}_{84}\text{Po} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

(d) Radon is a gas, so it is breathed in, and its solid decay products (such as polonium-218) lodge in the lungs. There, inside the body, the alpha particles deliver all of their very high ionizing power to living lung cells. Radon is the leading cause of lung cancer after smoking.

(e) n = 7.6 days ÷ 3.8 days = 2, so 1/4 (25%) is left. The lab corrects for this decay when it reports the result.

Problem 14.47

Right after the technetium-99m tracer for Mateo Herrera's V/Q lung scan is given, Mateo himself is a small gamma source. Suppose a nuclear medicine technologist measures 27 μSv/h at 0.50 m from him (a made-up value for practice). (a) What is the dose rate at 1.5 m? (b) What dose does the technologist receive standing 1.5 m away for 12 min? (c) Name the third way, besides time and distance, that the technologist lowers her dose while drawing up and handling doses, and give an example.

Hint

(a) How many times farther is 1.5 m than 0.50 m? Use the inverse-square law. (b) Dose = dose rate × time in hours. (c) Time, distance, and what else?

Answer

(a) \( 27\;\mu\text{Sv/h} \times \tfrac{1}{9} = 3.0\;\mu\text{Sv/h} \)    (1.5 m is three times 0.50 m, and tripling the distance gives one-ninth the dose rate)

(b) \( 3.0\;\tfrac{\mu\text{Sv}}{\text{h}} \times {0.20\;\text{h}} = 0.60\;\mu\text{Sv} \)

(a) 3.0 μSv/h; (b) 0.60 μSv (12 min = 0.20 h).

(c) Shielding: drawing up doses behind a lead-glass shield, using a tungsten or lead syringe shield, and carrying vials in a lead "pig." Lead is used because technetium-99m gives off gamma rays. (The case is for practicing chemistry only; it is not medical advice.)

14.7 Radiation in Medicine

Problem 14.48

A dental hygienist takes intraoral X-ray images (about 0.005 mSv each). Which statement about the X-rays is correct?

A. The X-rays come from radioactive nuclei inside the X-ray unit, so the unit is always giving them off.
B. The X-rays are made when fast electrons hit a metal target in the tube; when the unit is switched off, no X-rays are produced.
C. X-rays are not ionizing, so ALARA does not apply to dental images.
D. The patient's teeth become radioactive for a few hours after the images are taken.

Hint

X-rays and gamma rays are both electromagnetic radiation. Where is each made, and which one stops when the power is turned off?

Answer

B. X-rays and gamma rays are both high-energy electromagnetic radiation, but X-rays are made outside the nucleus, in a tube, and stop the moment the tube is switched off. Gamma rays come from decaying nuclei and cannot be switched off.

  • A describes a gamma source. An X-ray tube contains no radioactive material.
  • C is wrong: X-rays are ionizing radiation. The dose from one intraoral image is very small, about the same as a day of natural background, but every image still must be justified under ALARA.
  • D is wrong: X-rays pass through and deposit energy but do not change any nuclei, so nothing in the patient becomes radioactive.

Problem 14.49

Spices and surgical gloves are sterilized by gamma rays from cobalt-60 after they are sealed in their packages. Which statement is correct?

A. The gamma rays make the spices radioactive, so they must be stored until they decay.
B. Gamma rays are used because they are the most ionizing radiation, so they cannot get through the packaging.
C. Gamma rays pass through the packaging and break the DNA of microorganisms, but they change no nuclei, so the products do not become radioactive.
D. Alpha particles would work better, because they penetrate sealed boxes more easily than gamma rays.

Hint

Gamma rays are the most penetrating and least ionizing. Can they change a nucleus in the product they pass through?

Answer

C. Gamma rays are the most penetrating radiation, so they reach every part of a sealed, boxed product. They deposit energy, breaking DNA directly and through free radicals made from water, but they do not change any nuclei.

  • A is the most common misconception. Nothing in the spices absorbs a particle that changes its nucleus; the product is not radioactive.
  • B has gamma backward. Gamma rays are the least ionizing and most penetrating, which is exactly why they work through packaging.
  • D has alpha backward. Alpha particles are stopped by a sheet of paper, so they could not reach anything inside a box.

Problem 14.50

Which pairing of radioisotope and medical use makes the most sense?

A. Iodine-131 (beta and gamma, 8.0 days) to treat an overactive thyroid; technetium-99m (gamma, 6.0 h) to image the lungs
B. Technetium-99m to treat an overactive thyroid; iodine-131 to image the lungs
C. Carbon-14 (beta, 5730 years) to image the heart, because its long half-life gives plenty of time for the scan
D. Uranium-238 (alpha, 4.5 × 109 years) to image bones, because alpha particles are easy for a camera to detect

Hint

Treatment needs radiation that stays in the target organ; imaging needs gamma rays that leave the body and a short half-life. Which pairing fits both?

Answer

A. The thyroid collects iodide to make its hormones, so iodine-131 goes there, and its beta particles, which travel only about 2 mm in tissue, deliver their dose to the thyroid. Imaging needs gamma rays that leave the body and a short half-life, which technetium-99m has.

  • B swaps the roles. Technetium is not collected by the thyroid in a way that treats it, and iodine-131's beta particles would add dose to the lungs without helping the image.
  • C has the half-life reasoning backward: a 5730-year half-life would leave the patient radioactive for life, and carbon-14's beta particles do not leave the body to reach a camera.
  • D fails twice: alpha particles are stopped inside the body, so no camera outside can detect them, and inside the body they are the most damaging radiation.

Problem 14.51

Radium-223 (half-life 11.4 days) is used to treat cancer that has spread to bone. Like calcium, radium is taken up where bone is being rebuilt. (a) Radium-223 decays by alpha emission. Write the balanced equation. (b) A vial has an activity of 6.60 MBq on its calibration date. What is its activity 34.2 days later? (c) Alpha particles travel less than about 0.1 mm in tissue. Why is that an advantage for this treatment, and why does the vial need only light shielding?

Hint

(a) Alpha: subtract 4 on top and 2 on bottom. (b) Divide 34.2 days by the half-life, then halve 6.60 MBq that many times. (c) How far do alpha particles travel?

Answer

(a) Radium is element 88. A − 4 = 219; Z − 2 = 86, radon:

\( {}^{223}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{219}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

Check: 219 + 4 = 223 and 86 + 2 = 88.

(b) \( n = \dfrac{34.2\;\text{days}}{11.4\;\text{days}} = 3 \qquad {6.60\;\text{MBq}} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{3} = {0.825\;\text{MBq}} \)

(b) 0.825 MBq (6.60 → 3.30 → 1.65 → 0.825).

(c) The alpha particles deliver their very high ionizing power only to cells within a fraction of a millimeter of where the radium settled, so the dose stays at the bone surface and spares most nearby tissue. Outside the body, the same low penetrating power means the vial wall and a little plastic stop the alpha particles. (Context only; this is not treatment advice.)

A nuclear medicine department stocks the radioisotopes in the table below.

(a) Which isotope is best for a same-day bone scan imaged with a gamma camera, and why? (b) Which is used for PET scans? (d) A technetium-99m dose has an activity of 20.0 mCi at 6:00 a.m. The scan needs at least 5.00 mCi at the time of injection. What is the latest time it can be injected?

Problem 14.53

Charcoal from an ancient campfire has a carbon-14 to carbon-12 ratio of 5.00 × 10−15. Carbon in living wood today has a ratio of 1.28 × 10−12. (a) What fraction of the original carbon-14 remains? (b) How many half-lives have passed, and how old is the charcoal? (c) Is the result near the limit of the method? (d) Write the equation for the reaction in the upper atmosphere that makes carbon-14.

Hint

(a) Divide the sample ratio by the living ratio, then write it as (1/2)n. (b) Multiply n by 5730 years. (c) Compare with about 50,000 years. (d) Cosmic-ray neutrons hit nitrogen-14.

Answer

(a) \( \dfrac{5.00 \times 10^{-15}}{1.28 \times 10^{-12}} = 3.91 \times 10^{-3} = \dfrac{1}{256} \)

(b) 256 = 28, so 8 half-lives have passed:

\( \text{age} = 8 \times {5730\;\text{years}} = {45{,}840\;\text{years}} = {4.58 \times 10^{4}\;\text{years}} \)

(a) 1/256, about 0.391%; (b) 8 half-lives, 4.58 × 104 years.

(c) Yes. The method is useful to about 50,000 years (about 9 half-lives); with less than 0.4% of the carbon-14 left, the measurement is difficult and very sensitive to contamination.

Problem 14.54

Fluorine-18 for PET scans is made in a cyclotron by bombarding oxygen-18 with protons. (a) Complete the equation and identify the product. (b) In the patient, each fluorine-18 nucleus gives off a positron. What happens to the positron, and why can the PET scanner detect it? (c) A patient needs 7.50 mCi of fluorine-18 at the time of injection, and the dose takes 220 min to reach the clinic and be given. What activity must be shipped? (d) About how many hours after injection is less than 1% of the injected fluorine-18 left?

\( {}^{18}_{8}\text{O} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow \;? + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

Hint

(a) Use the top-and-bottom rule. (c) Count half-lives in 220 min; going back in time, double once per half-life. (d) Halve from 100% until below 1%, then convert to hours.

Answer

(a) Left: A = 18 + 1 = 19, Z = 8 + 1 = 9. Subtract the neutron: A = 18, Z = 9, fluorine-18.

\( {}^{18}_{8}\text{O} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{18}_{9}\text{F} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

(b) The positron travels about a millimeter, meets an electron, and the two annihilate: their mass becomes two gamma rays that fly off in opposite directions. Gamma rays pass out of the body, and the ring of detectors catches both at the same instant.

(c) n = 220 min ÷ 110 min = 2. Going back in time, double twice:

\( {7.50\;\text{mCi}} \times 2^{2} = {30.0\;\text{mCi}} \)

(d) Less than 1% remains after 7 half-lives (1/128 = 0.781%; after 6 it is still 1.56%):

\( 7 \times {110\;\text{min}} = {770\;\text{min}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{h}}{60\;\text{min}} = {13\;\text{h}} \)

(c) 30.0 mCi; (d) about 13 h (770 min, two significant figures like 110 min). Most of the radioactivity is gone the same day, which is why PET centers must use fluorine-18 within hours of making it.

Problem 14.55

Radon-222 in foothill homes forms from uranium-238 in granite and soil through a series of decays: uranium-238 decays to radium-226 by several alpha and beta decays, and then radium-226 alpha decays to radon-222. (a) How many alpha decays and how many beta decays take uranium-238 to radium-226? (b) The first two steps are an alpha decay of uranium-238 and a beta decay of its daughter. Write both equations. (c) A sealed jar of soil gas holds 3.20 ng of radon-222 (half-life 3.8 days). How much remains after 15.2 days, if no new radon forms?

Hint

(a) Only alpha decays change A, by 4 each; find how many, then use beta decays to fix Z. (c) Count half-lives in 15.2 days and halve 3.20 ng.

Answer

(a) Uranium-238 (A = 238, Z = 92) to radium-226 (A = 226, Z = 88). Only alpha decays change the mass number, each by 4: (238 − 226) ÷ 4 = 3 alpha decays. Three alphas lower Z by 3 × 2 = 6, to 92 − 6 = 86, but radium is 88. Each beta decay raises Z by 1, so there must be 88 − 86 = 2 beta decays.

(b) Uranium (92) loses an alpha particle to give thorium-234 (90); thorium-234 beta decays to protactinium-234 (91):

\( {}^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)

\( {}^{234}_{90}\text{Th} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{91}\text{Pa} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \)

(c) \( n = \dfrac{15.2\;\text{days}}{3.8\;\text{days}} = 4 \qquad {3.20\;\text{ng}} \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{4} = {0.200\;\text{ng}} \)

(a) 3 alpha, 2 beta; (c) 0.200 ng. Radon's short half-life means a home's radon comes from radium decaying in the ground beneath it right now, which is why it can keep seeping in for as long as the house stands.

14.1–14.7 Putting It Together: More Problems

These problems practice the skills added to Sections 14.1–14.7: nuclear stability and the neutron-to-proton ratio, chemical versus nuclear reactions, counting protons and neutrons, bombardment word problems, half-life setups and percent maps, choosing a medical tracer, and contrast agents.

Problem 14.56

The only stable isotope of iodine is iodine-127 (53 protons, 74 neutrons). Iodine-131, used to treat thyroid disease, has 53 protons and 78 neutrons. Which kind of decay would you predict for iodine-131?

A. Alpha decay, because iodine-131 is heavier than iodine-127.
B. Beta decay, because iodine-131 has too many neutrons.
C. Positron emission, because iodine-131 has too many neutrons.
D. Electron capture, because iodine-131 has too few protons to be stable.

Hint

Compare iodine-131's neutron count with the stable iodine-127. Which decay turns a neutron into a proton?

Answer

B. Iodine-131 has 4 more neutrons than the stable isotope, so it lies above the band of stability. Beta decay turns a neutron into a proton, lowering the neutron-to-proton ratio. That is exactly how iodine-131 decays, to xenon-131 (Section 14.2, Example 2).

  • A: alpha decay is typical of very large nuclei, beyond about element 83. Iodine (53) is not in that range; "heavier" here means extra neutrons, not too big.
  • C: positron emission turns a proton into a neutron, which would make the neutron excess worse. It is the fix for nuclei with too few neutrons.
  • D: electron capture also turns a proton into a neutron, the wrong direction for a nucleus with extra neutrons.

Problem 14.57

Which statement correctly compares a chemical reaction with a nuclear reaction?

A. In both, the elements on the left are the same as the elements on the right.
B. In a chemical reaction electrons are rearranged and the elements do not change; in a nuclear reaction the nucleus changes and a new element can form.
C. Both can be sped up by heating or by adding a catalyst.
D. Nuclear equations are balanced by making the charges of the ions equal on both sides.

Hint

For each choice, ask what part of the atom changes in a chemical reaction and in a nuclear reaction. Can heating or a catalyst change a nucleus?

Answer

B. Chemical reactions involve only electrons, so an oxygen atom stays an oxygen atom. Nuclear reactions change the number of protons or neutrons, and a change in the number of protons makes a new element.

  • A is true only for chemical reactions. In alpha decay, for example, radium becomes radon.
  • C is true only for chemical reactions (Chapter 13). Radioactive decay runs at the same rate whatever the temperature, and no catalyst changes it.
  • D confuses the two kinds of balancing. Nuclear equations balance mass numbers and atomic numbers; ion charges are not used.

Problem 14.58

Write a balanced equation for each bombardment reaction. (a) In 1969, scientists at Berkeley bombarded californium-249 with carbon-12 nuclei, producing four neutrons and a new element. Identify it. (b) Molybdenum-98 captures a neutron, producing the molybdenum isotope that is the parent of technetium-99m. (c) Bismuth-209 is struck by a particle, producing astatine-211 (used in experimental cancer treatment) and two neutrons. What was the particle?

Hint

Bombardment: the particle that strikes goes on the left. Total the top and bottom numbers on each side, and remember a coefficient multiplies both numbers of a particle.

Answer

(a) \( {}^{249}_{98}\text{Cf} + {}^{12}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{257}_{104}\text{Rf} + 4\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

(a) Left: 249 + 12 = 261 and 98 + 6 = 104. Four neutrons carry 4 mass units and no charge, so the new nucleus has A = 261 − 4 = 257 and Z = 104: rutherfordium-257.

(b) \( {}^{98}_{42}\text{Mo} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{99}_{42}\text{Mo} \)

(b) Capturing a neutron adds 1 to A and nothing to Z, so the element stays molybdenum.

(c) \( {}^{209}_{83}\text{Bi} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow {}^{211}_{85}\text{At} + 2\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \)

(c) Right: 211 + 2 = 213 and 85 + 0 = 85. Left: 209 + ? = 213, so A = 4; 83 + ? = 85, so Z = 2. The particle is an alpha particle. In each part, the words "bombarded," "captures," or "struck by" put the particle on the reactant side, and a coefficient in front of a particle multiplies both of its numbers.

Problem 14.59

Thallium-201, once widely used in heart scans, decays to mercury-201. Count the protons and neutrons in each nucleus. What happened?

A. The nucleus lost 2 protons and 2 neutrons: alpha decay.
B. A neutron became a proton: beta decay.
C. A proton became a neutron: electron capture or positron emission.
D. Nothing changed in the nucleus: gamma emission.

Hint

Protons = Z and neutrons = A − Z for both nuclei. A did not change; did a proton become a neutron or the reverse?

Answer

C. Thallium-201 has 81 protons and 201 − 81 = 120 neutrons. Mercury-201 has 80 protons and 121 neutrons. One proton became one neutron, and A did not change. (Thallium-201 actually decays by electron capture: \( {}^{201}_{81}\text{Tl} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} \rightarrow {}^{201}_{80}\text{Hg} \).)

  • A would drop A by 4 (to 197) and Z by 2 (to 79, gold).
  • B would raise Z by 1, to 82, lead.
  • D cannot be right, because the element changed from thallium to mercury.

Problem 14.60

Cesium-137 (half-life 30.2 years) is a fission product found in the soil around damaged nuclear plants. (a) Using the half-life as a conversion factor, how many half-lives pass in 90.6 years? (b) What percent of the cesium-137 is left after 90.6 years? Use a percent map. (c) If a soil sample held 1.20 g of cesium-137, what mass is left after 90.6 years?

Hint

(a) Write 1 half-life = 30.2 years as a conversion factor. (b) Arrow map from 100%, one arrow per half-life. (c) Multiply 1.20 g by that fraction.

Answer

(a) \( {90.6\;\text{years}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{half-life}}{30.2\;\text{years}} = {3\;\text{half-lives}} \)

(b) 100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% (3 arrows).

(c) 1.20 g × (1/2)3 = 1.20 g × 0.125 = 0.150 g. Even after almost a century, one-eighth remains, which is why long-lived fission products keep land contaminated for generations.

Problem 14.61

Copper-64 (half-life 12.7 hours) is used as a PET tracer. The decay path that makes it useful for PET is positron emission. (a) Write the balanced equation for that decay. (b) A dose has an activity of 4.00 mCi at 8:00 a.m. Monday. What is its activity 38.1 hours later? (c) Why is a positron emitter, rather than an alpha emitter, chosen for imaging?

Hint

(a) A positron is 0+1e, so Z drops by 1. (b) Count half-lives in 38.1 h and halve 4.00 mCi. (c) Which radiation can leave the body to reach a detector?

Answer

(a) \( {}^{64}_{29}\text{Cu} \rightarrow {}^{64}_{28}\text{Ni} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} \)

(b) 38.1 h ÷ 12.7 h = 3 half-lives. 4.00 → 2.00 → 1.00 → 0.500 mCi.

Problem 14.62

In the 1920s, dial painters who shaped their brushes with their lips swallowed small amounts of radium-226, an alpha emitter. Why was this so harmful, when alpha particles cannot even pass through a sheet of paper?

A. Inside the body, radium gives off gamma rays instead of alpha particles.
B. Radium behaves like calcium and settles in bone, where its alpha particles deliver all of their very high ionizing power to the bone and marrow cells next to them, for years.
C. Alpha particles are the most penetrating radiation, so they passed through the whole body.
D. Swallowed radium makes the stomach acid radioactive, which then spreads through the blood.

Hint

Alpha particles are stopped by dead skin outside the body. Radium is in Group 2 like calcium; where does it settle once swallowed?

Answer

B. Outside the body, the dead outer layer of skin stops alpha particles. Swallowed, radium (Group 2, like calcium) is built into bone, and every alpha particle dumps its energy into living cells within a tiny distance. Radium-226 has a half-life of about 1600 years, so the exposure continued for the rest of the workers' lives.

  • A is wrong: the type of decay is a property of the nucleus and does not change with location.
  • C reverses the trend. Alpha is the least penetrating and most ionizing radiation.
  • D is wrong: radiation does not make stomach acid radioactive; the harm comes from the radium atoms themselves, wherever they settle.

Problem 14.63

Before a barium swallow, a nervous patient asks a radiologic technology student, "Will that drink make me radioactive?" Which answer is correct?

A. Yes, slightly; barium is a radioactive element, which is why it shows up on the image.
B. No; barium sulfate is not radioactive. Its heavy barium atoms absorb X-rays, so the lining of the digestive tract shows up, and because it is insoluble, almost no barium ions dissolve into the body.
C. No, because the barium is dissolved as Ba2+ ions, which the body uses like calcium.
D. Yes, but only until the X-ray machine is turned off.

Hint

Contrast agents block X-rays; is the barium in BaSO4 radioactive? Use the green sheet: is BaSO4 soluble?

Answer

B. Contrast agents work by blocking X-rays, not by giving off radiation. The barium in BaSO4 is ordinary, stable barium. BaSO4 is chosen because it is insoluble (green sheet), so the toxic Ba2+ ion stays locked in the solid. (Context only.)

  • A is wrong: natural barium is not radioactive. It shows up because heavy atoms absorb X-rays strongly.
  • C is wrong and dangerous: dissolved Ba2+ is toxic, which is exactly why a soluble barium compound is never used.
  • D is wrong: X-rays deposit energy but do not change any nuclei, so nothing becomes radioactive, during or after the image.
