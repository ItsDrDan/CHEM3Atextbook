The CHEM 3A final exam is a standardized multiple-choice exam written by the American Chemical Society (ACS). It covers the whole course, and every CHEM 3A section gives it. This page is a plan for the last weeks of the term: what to review, in what order, and which practice in this book to use.

How the ACS Final Is Different

  • It is cumulative. Chapter 1 skills (significant figures, unit conversions) and Chapter 5 skills (moles) show up inside problems from every later chapter.
  • It is all multiple choice. The wrong choices are usually the answers you would get by making a common mistake, such as forgetting to convert mL to L, flipping a ratio, or using a coefficient as a subscript. Work the problem first, then look at the choices.
  • It is timed. Practice under time pressure before the exam, not just untimed.

Ask your instructor which reference materials and calculator you may use, and practice with exactly those.

Chapter Checklist

For each chapter, make sure you can do every item without looking anything up except your reference sheets. Each chapter name links to its Chapter Guide.

Chapter 1: Measurement, Matter, and the Scientific Method

  • Count significant figures and round a calculated answer (fewest significant figures for × and ÷; fewest decimal places for + and −).
  • Write and use scientific notation and metric prefixes.
  • Convert units by dimensional analysis, including multistep, squared and cubed units, dosage, and percent problems.
  • Tell accuracy from precision, classify matter, and tell physical from chemical properties and changes.
  • Use density, d = m/V, as a conversion factor between mass and volume.

Chapter 2: Atoms, Ions, and Isotopes

  • Find protons, neutrons, and electrons from a symbol, mass number, and charge.
  • Write isotope symbols, and predict common ion charges from the periodic table.
  • Calculate an average atomic mass from isotope masses and abundances.
  • Explain what the Dalton, Thomson, and Rutherford experiments showed.

Chapter 3: Light, Electrons, and the Periodic Table

  • Use c = λν and E = hν = hc/λ (convert nm to m first).
  • Explain line spectra with electron energy levels.
  • Write orbital diagrams and full and noble-gas electron configurations, for atoms and ions, including Cr and Cu.
  • Count valence electrons, draw Lewis symbols, and rank atomic radius, ionization energy, and ion size.

Chapter 4: Naming Compounds

  • Write ionic formulas from ion charges, including polyatomic ions from the green sheet.
  • Name ionic compounds, using Roman numerals for metals with more than one charge.
  • Name molecular compounds with prefixes, and name acids (-ide → hydro-…-ic, -ate → -ic, -ite → -ous).
  • Decide which naming system a compound needs.

Chapter 5: The Mole

  • Calculate a molar mass from the yellow sheet.
  • Convert among grams, moles, and particles (6.0221 × 1023); the goal is the mole.
  • Use subscripts as mole ratios inside a formula.

Chapter 6: Chemical Bonds, Shapes, and Composition

  • Tell ionic from covalent bonding, and draw Lewis structures.
  • Predict shapes and bond angles for up to four electron groups (VSEPR).
  • Classify bonds by ΔEN and decide whether a molecule is polar (SNAP).
  • Calculate mass percent, and find empirical and molecular formulas.

Chapter 7: Chemical Reactions

  • Balance equations and classify reactions (combination, decomposition, single and double displacement, combustion).
  • Use the solubility rules to predict precipitates, and write net ionic equations.
  • Recognize acid–base and gas-forming reactions.
  • Assign oxidation numbers, find what is oxidized and reduced, and use the activity series.

Chapter 8: Stoichiometry

  • Use coefficients as mole ratios: grams → moles → moles → grams.
  • Start from a solution volume (mol = M × L) or a gas volume at STP (22.4 L/mol).
  • Find the limiting reactant, the leftover, the theoretical yield, and the percent yield.
  • Use ΔH as a conversion factor, and tell exothermic from endothermic.

Chapter 9: Energy, Solids, and Liquids

  • Convert among °F, °C, and K, and between calories and joules.
  • Use q = mcΔT (including calorimetry) and q = nΔH on heating and cooling curves.
  • Identify intermolecular forces and rank boiling points and vapor pressures.
  • Classify solids and relate forces to properties.

Chapter 10: Gases

  • Convert pressure units, and use the gas laws with temperatures in kelvins.
  • Use PV = nRT, including molar mass of a gas and gas stoichiometry.
  • Use Dalton’s law, including a gas collected over water.
  • Explain the gas laws with kinetic molecular theory.

Chapter 11: Solutions

  • Predict solubility (like dissolves like) and the effects of temperature and pressure.
  • Calculate %(m/m), %(m/v), ppm, and molarity, and do dilutions (M1V1 = M2V2).
  • Do solution stoichiometry and titration problems.
  • Calculate molality and ΔTf or ΔTb with i, and predict the direction of osmosis.

Chapter 12: Acids and Bases

  • Identify Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases and conjugate pairs.
  • Know the strong acids and strong bases (green sheet).
  • Use Kw, and calculate pH, pOH, [H3O+], and [OH−] for strong acids and bases (decimal places in pH = significant figures in the concentration).
  • Recognize a buffer and explain how it works.

Chapter 13: Chemical Equilibrium

  • Explain how concentration, temperature, surface area, and a catalyst change a reaction rate.
  • Describe dynamic equilibrium, and write K expressions (leave out pure solids and liquids).
  • Interpret the size of K, and K for the reverse reaction.
  • Predict Le Chatelier shifts for concentration, temperature, and volume changes (a catalyst causes no shift; only temperature changes K).

Chapter 14: Radioactivity and Nuclear Chemistry

  • Write nuclear equations for alpha, beta, positron, gamma, and electron capture, balancing mass and atomic numbers.
  • Compare penetrating power and shielding.
  • Solve half-life problems with whole numbers of half-lives, including carbon-14 dating.
  • Describe fission, fusion, and the main uses of radiation in medicine.

On Exam Day

  • Read each question to the end before calculating; underline what it asks for and the units it wants.
  • For a calculation, estimate the size of the answer first. If your result is far from the estimate, look for a missed conversion.
  • For a concept question, cross out the choices you know are wrong; two are usually easy to eliminate.
  • Do not get stuck. Mark a hard question, move on, and come back with the time you have left.
