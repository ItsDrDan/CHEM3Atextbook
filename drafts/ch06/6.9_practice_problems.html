6.1 Ionic Bonding

Problem 6.1

A fluoride treatment at the dentist can leave a thin layer of calcium fluoride on tooth enamel. Which statement correctly describes how calcium and fluorine atoms form this compound?

A. Each Ca atom gives one electron to one F atom, forming CaF.
B. Each Ca atom gives one electron to each of two F atoms, forming Ca2+ and two F− ions: CaF2.
C. Each F atom gives one electron to a Ca atom, forming Ca− and F+ ions.
D. Ca and F share a pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

Hint

Calcium is in group 2 and fluorine in group 17. How many electrons must Ca lose, and how many can one F accept? Metals lose, nonmetals gain.

Answer

B. Calcium (group 2) has 2 valence electrons and loses both to reach the configuration of argon; each fluorine (group 17) has 7 and needs 1. One Ca atom therefore gives one electron to each of two F atoms. Electrons lost (2) = electrons gained (2 × 1), and the charges cancel: (2+) + 2(1−) = 0, so the formula is CaF2.

  • A leaves calcium with one valence electron, which is not a noble-gas configuration, and Ca+ with F− is not the ion pair that forms. Calcium always forms Ca2+.
  • C moves electrons the wrong way. Metals lose electrons and nonmetals gain them; fluorine, the most electronegative element, never gives up an electron to a metal.
  • D describes a covalent bond, which forms between two nonmetals. Calcium is a metal and fluorine is a nonmetal, so electrons transfer (the electronegativity difference, 4.0 − 1.0 = 3.0, is far above 2.0; Section 6.5).

Problem 6.2

The dull, protective coating that forms on aluminum irrigation pipe is aluminum oxide. Use electron-dot symbols to show how aluminum and oxygen atoms form this ionic compound. (a) How many valence electrons does each atom have? (b) How many Al and O atoms are needed so that electrons lost equal electrons gained? (c) What is the formula?

Hint

(a) Use group numbers. (b) Find the smallest number both 3 (lost per Al) and 2 (gained per O) divide into. (c) Write the formula from those counts.

Answer

(a) Al (group 13) has 3 valence electrons (3 dots) and loses all three to form Al3+. O (group 16) has 6 valence electrons (6 dots) and gains 2 to form O2− with an octet.

(b) The smallest number that both 3 and 2 divide into is 6. Two Al atoms lose 2 × 3 = 6 electrons, and three O atoms gain 3 × 2 = 6 electrons:

2 Al (3 dots each) + 3 O (6 dots each) → 2 Al3+ (no dots) + 3 O2− (8 dots each)

(c) Al2O3. Check: 2(3+) + 3(2−) = 6+ − 6 = 0, the same formula swap-drop gives (Section 4.2).

Problem 6.4

Electrolytes are substances whose dissolved ions conduct electricity. Which of these conducts electricity well?

A. A crystal of solid sodium chloride
B. Potassium chloride dissolved in water, as in an IV electrolyte solution
C. Glucose dissolved in water, as in a 5% dextrose IV solution
D. Pure, distilled water

Hint

A current needs charged particles that can move. Which choice has ions, and are they free to move?

Answer

B. KCl is ionic. When it dissolves, the K+ and Cl− ions separate and are free to move, so they carry an electric current.

  • A contains ions, but in a solid crystal lattice they are locked in place and cannot move, so solid NaCl does not conduct.
  • C: glucose, C6H12O6, is a molecular compound of nonmetals. It dissolves as whole, neutral molecules, not ions, so the solution does not conduct (glucose is a nonelectrolyte).
  • D: pure water is made of neutral molecules and contains almost no ions, so it conducts very poorly. Tap water conducts a little only because of dissolved salts.

Problem 6.5

Tooth enamel is mostly hydroxyapatite, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2. (a) Show that the charges of the ions in one formula unit add to zero. (b) Fluoride from toothpaste can replace the hydroxide ions, forming fluorapatite, Ca10(PO4)6F2. Why can each F− replace one OH− without changing the rest of the formula? (c) How many ions are in one formula unit of hydroxyapatite?

Hint

(a) Multiply each ion's charge by how many there are, then add. (b) Compare the charges of F− and OH−. (c) Count each polyatomic ion as one ion.

Answer

(a) 10 Ca2+ give 10(2+) = 20+. 6 PO43− give 6(3−) = 18−. 2 OH− give 2(1−) = 2−. Total: 20 − 18 − 2 = 0, so the compound is neutral.

(b) F− and OH− have the same charge (1−) and similar sizes, so a one-for-one swap keeps the total charge at zero.

(c) 10 + 6 + 2 = 18 ions. Each polyatomic ion (PO43− or OH−) counts as one ion; the atoms inside it are held together by covalent bonds.

Problem 6.6

In Experiment 5a, magnesium burns in air to form magnesium oxide, MgO. Which statement about the ions in MgO is true?

A. Mg2+ and O2− both have the electron configuration of neon.
B. Mg2+ has the configuration of argon, and O2− has the configuration of neon.
C. Mg2+ has the configuration of neon, and O2− has the configuration of helium.
D. Neither ion has a noble-gas configuration, because the electrons are shared.

Hint

Count electrons: Mg has 12 and O has 8. How many does each ion have after losing or gaining 2? Which noble gas has that many?

Answer

A. Mg (12 electrons) loses 2 to become Mg2+ with 10 electrons. O (8 electrons) gains 2 to become O2− with 10 electrons. Both have 10 electrons, 1s2 2s2 2p6, the configuration of neon. (Ions with the same configuration are called isoelectronic.)

  • B: losing electrons cannot move magnesium up to argon (18 electrons). Mg2+ drops back to the noble gas before it, neon.
  • C: helium has 2 electrons. Oxygen gains electrons, so O2− has more electrons (10) than an O atom, not fewer.
  • D: in an ionic compound electrons are transferred, not shared, and the whole point of the transfer is that each ion reaches a noble-gas configuration.

6.2 Covalent Bonds

Problem 6.7

Nitrogen gas, N2, makes up most of the air you breathe. Which description of its Lewis structure is correct?

A. N–N single bond with three lone pairs on each N
B. N=N double bond with two lone pairs on each N
C. N≡N triple bond with one lone pair on each N
D. N≡N triple bond with no lone pairs

Hint

Count N2's valence electrons, then count the electrons each choice uses. Never add electrons to complete octets; HONC says N forms 3 bonds.

Answer

C. N2 has 2 × 5 = 10 valence electrons. A triple bond (6 electrons) plus one lone pair on each N (4 electrons) uses exactly 10, and each N has 6 + 2 = 8 electrons. Each N forms 3 bonds, as HONC predicts.

  • A uses 2 + 12 = 14 electrons, 4 more than N2 has. Never add electrons to complete octets.
  • B uses 4 + 8 = 12 electrons, still 2 too many, and each N would have only 2 bonds instead of 3.
  • D uses only 6 electrons, and each N would have just 6 electrons around it, not an octet.

Problem 6.8

Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, the 3% antiseptic in many first-aid kits, has the skeleton H–O–O–H. (a) How many valence electrons does it have? (b) How many bonding pairs and how many lone pairs are in its Lewis structure? (c) Show that each atom has a full valence shell.

Hint

(a) Add group-number electrons. (b) The three single bonds use some; the rest go as lone pairs. (c) Count electrons around each O and each H.

Answer

(a) 2(1) + 2(6) = 14 valence electrons.

(b) The three single bonds (H–O, O–O, O–H) are 3 bonding pairs (6 electrons). The remaining 8 electrons are 4 lone pairs, two on each O.

(c) Each O: 2 bonds (4 electrons) + 2 lone pairs (4 electrons) = 8. Each H: 1 bond = 2 electrons (a duet). Check: 6 + 8 = 14 electrons used, and each O forms 2 bonds, each H 1 bond, as HONC predicts.

Problem 6.9

Which carbon–carbon bond is the longest?

A. C≡C (triple)
B. C=C (double)
C. C–C (single)
D. All three are the same length, because they join the same two kinds of atoms.

Hint

Count the shared electron pairs in each bond. Does sharing more pairs hold the two carbons closer together or farther apart?

Answer

C. The more pairs two atoms share, the more tightly they are pulled together. A single bond (one shared pair) is the longest and weakest of the three; a triple bond is the shortest and strongest.

  • A is backward: the triple bond is the shortest, because three shared pairs hold the atoms closest together.
  • B is in the middle: shorter than a single bond, longer than a triple bond.
  • D ignores bond order. Bond length depends on the number of shared pairs as well as on which atoms are bonded.

Problem 6.10

Chlorine gas, Cl2, is used to disinfect drinking water, and hydrogen chloride, HCl, dissolved in water is the hydrochloric acid in your stomach. For each molecule, give (a) the number of valence electrons, (b) the number of bonding pairs, and (c) the number of lone pairs.

Hint

For each molecule, count valence electrons, connect the atoms, and place the rest as lone pairs. Remember H gets a duet, not an octet.

Answer

Cl2 (Cl–Cl): (a) 2(7) = 14 electrons; (b) 1 bonding pair; (c) 6 lone pairs, three on each Cl. Check: 2 + 12 = 14, and each Cl has 2 + 6 = 8.

HCl (H–Cl): (a) 1 + 7 = 8 electrons; (b) 1 bonding pair; (c) 3 lone pairs, all on Cl. Hydrogen has no lone pairs: its single bond already gives it a duet. Check: 2 + 6 = 8.

Problem 6.11

Ethylene gas is the plant hormone that ripens fruit; packers use it in ripening rooms for bananas and tomatoes. Which molecule contains a double bond?

A. Methanol, CH3OH
B. Ethylene, C2H4
C. Ethane, C2H6
D. Ammonia, NH3

Hint

Use HONC (carbon forms 4 bonds). In which molecule can't every atom reach its usual bond count with single bonds alone?

Answer

B. In C2H4, each carbon is bonded to two H atoms. That is only 3 bonds per carbon if the C–C bond is single, but carbon forms 4 bonds (HONC), so the two carbons share two pairs: H2C=CH2.

  • A: in methanol, C already has 4 single bonds (three H and the O), and O has 2 (C and H). Every atom is satisfied with single bonds.
  • C: in ethane, each carbon is bonded to three H atoms and the other carbon, 4 single bonds each, so no double bond is needed.
  • D: N forms 3 single bonds to H and keeps one lone pair; no double bond.

Problem 6.12

Use the usual number of bonds for each element (Section 6.2) to predict the formula of the simplest neutral compound of hydrogen with each element below, and give the number of lone pairs on that element: (a) silicon; (b) phosphorus; (c) sulfur; (d) bromine.

Hint

Use the group number: each bond to H gives the element one more electron toward an octet. Leftover valence electrons pair up as lone pairs.

Answer

An atom usually forms as many bonds as the electrons it needs to reach an octet, and each bond here goes to one H atom.

  • (a) Si (group 14, 4 valence electrons) forms 4 bonds: SiH4, 0 lone pairs.
  • (b) P (group 15, 5) forms 3 bonds: PH3, 1 lone pair.
  • (c) S (group 16, 6) forms 2 bonds: H2S, 2 lone pairs.
  • (d) Br (group 17, 7) forms 1 bond: HBr, 3 lone pairs.

Check: in each case bonds × 2 + lone pairs × 2 = 8 around the central element. These follow the same pattern as CH4, NH3, H2O, and HF one row up.

Find the total number of valence electrons in each: (a) methanol, CH3OH; (b) the phosphate ion, PO43−, found in bone and in ATP; (c) the hydronium ion, H3O+; (d) the sulfate ion, SO42−; (e) hydrogen cyanide, HCN.

Problem 6.14

Chloromethane, CH3Cl, has 14 valence electrons. Which description of its Lewis structure is correct?

A. C in the center, single-bonded to three H atoms and to Cl; three lone pairs on Cl; no lone pairs on C
B. Cl in the center, single-bonded to C and to the three H atoms; no lone pairs on Cl
C. C in the center, single-bonded to three H atoms and to Cl; three lone pairs on Cl and one lone pair on C
D. C in the center, single-bonded to three H atoms and double-bonded to Cl; two lone pairs on Cl

Hint

Check each choice: total electrons used must be 14, carbon makes 4 bonds (HONC), Cl usually 1, and no atom other than H has more or less than 8.

Answer

A. Carbon is central (H is never central, and halogens are usually on the outside). Four single bonds use 8 electrons; the other 6 go on Cl as three lone pairs. Check: C has 4 bonds (8 electrons), Cl has 1 bond + 3 lone pairs (8), each H has 2, and 8 + 6 = 14 electrons are used.

  • B gives chlorine 4 bonds and carbon only 1. Chlorine usually forms 1 bond and carbon 4 (HONC), and carbon would have only 2 electrons.
  • C uses 16 electrons, 2 more than the molecule has, and gives carbon 10 electrons, more than an octet.
  • D uses 14 electrons, but carbon now has 5 bonds (10 electrons). Carbon never exceeds an octet, and chlorine usually forms only 1 bond.

Problem 6.15

Draw the Lewis structure of phosphorus trichloride, PCl3. (a) How many valence electrons does it have? (b) Which atom is central, and why? (c) How many bonding pairs, lone pairs on the central atom, and lone pairs in total are there?

Hint

(a) Add valence electrons. (b) Which atom appears once and is less electronegative? (c) Connect with single bonds, fill outer atoms first, and put any leftovers on the center.

Answer

(a) 5 + 3(7) = 26 valence electrons.

(b) P is central: it appears once, and it is less electronegative than Cl (2.1 versus 3.0). Halogens usually go on the outside.

(c) Three P–Cl single bonds use 6 electrons. Each Cl gets three lone pairs (18 electrons), leaving 26 − 6 − 18 = 2 electrons, which go on P as one lone pair. So: 3 bonding pairs, 1 lone pair on P, and 10 lone pairs in total (9 on the Cl atoms + 1 on P).

Check: 13 pairs × 2 = 26 electrons. P has 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 8 electrons, and P forms 3 bonds, like N in NH3.

Problem 6.16

Ammonium sulfate fertilizer supplies the ammonium ion, NH4+, and milk of magnesia supplies the hydroxide ion, OH−. Describe the Lewis structure of each ion, including the electron count and how the charge is shown.

Hint

Count each ion's electrons (adjust for the charge), place bonds and lone pairs, then put the structure in brackets with the charge outside.

Answer

NH4+: 5 + 4(1) − 1 = 8 electrons. N is central with four N–H single bonds (8 electrons) and no lone pairs. The whole structure goes in square brackets with the charge outside: [NH4]+. N has 8 electrons and each H has 2.

OH−: 6 + 1 + 1 = 8 electrons. One O–H bond (2 electrons) and three lone pairs on O (6 electrons), in brackets: [O–H]−. O has 8 electrons and H has 2.

Notice that N in NH4+ has 4 bonds, not the usual 3, and O in OH− has 1 bond, not the usual 2. HONC counts are for neutral molecules; ions can differ, which is why the electron count, not HONC, is the final check.

Problem 6.17

Methanol, CH3OH (wood alcohol, in windshield-washer fluid), is toxic if swallowed. (a) Use the formula and HONC to describe its skeleton. (b) How many bonding pairs and lone pairs does its Lewis structure have? (c) Why can the H written last in "CH3OH" not sit between C and O?

Hint

(a) Read the formula as a CH3 group joined to an OH group. (b) Count electrons and subtract the bonding pairs. (c) How many bonds can H form?

Answer

(a) The formula is written as a CH3 group joined to an OH group: C is bonded to three H atoms and to O, and O is bonded to C and to the last H. C has 4 bonds and O has 2, as HONC predicts.

(b) Valence electrons: 4 + 4(1) + 6 = 14. Five single bonds (three C–H, one C–O, one O–H) are 5 bonding pairs (10 electrons). The other 4 electrons are 2 lone pairs on O. C has 8, O has 4 + 4 = 8, each H has 2.

(c) Hydrogen forms only one bond, so it can never connect two other atoms. It must be at the end of a chain, never in the middle.

Problem 6.18

Ethylene, C2H4, has 12 valence electrons. Which description of its Lewis structure is correct?

A. The two carbons are joined by a double bond, each carbon is single-bonded to two H atoms, and there are no lone pairs.
B. The two carbons are joined by a single bond, each carbon is single-bonded to two H atoms, and each carbon has one lone pair.
C. The two carbons are joined by a single bond, each carbon is single-bonded to two H atoms, and there are no lone pairs.
D. The two carbons are joined by a triple bond, and each carbon is single-bonded to two H atoms.

Hint

Count the electrons each description uses (it must be 12) and check that every carbon has 4 bonds and 8 electrons.

Answer

A. H2C=CH2: four C–H bonds (8 electrons) + one C=C double bond (4 electrons) = 12. Each carbon has 2 + 2 + 4 = 8 electrons and 4 bonds.

  • B gives each carbon an octet, but it uses 10 + 4 = 14 electrons, 2 more than ethylene has. When you run out of electrons before the octets are full, make a multiple bond instead of adding electrons.
  • C uses only 10 electrons, leaving 2 unused, and each carbon has only 6 electrons around it.
  • D gives each carbon 5 bonds (10 electrons, more than an octet) and uses 14 electrons. A C≡C triple bond appears in acetylene, C2H2, where each carbon has only one H.

Problem 6.19

White blood cells called neutrophils kill bacteria partly by making hypochlorous acid, HOCl, and some wound-cleansing solutions contain it as well. Its skeleton is H–O–Cl. (a) Why is O, not H or Cl, the central atom? (b) How many valence electrons does HOCl have? (c) Describe the finished Lewis structure, including all lone pairs.

Hint

(a) Which atoms can form only one bond? (b) Add group-number electrons. (c) Connect with single bonds, then fill octets with lone pairs.

Answer

(a) H forms only one bond and Cl usually forms one bond, so neither can connect two atoms. O usually forms two bonds, so it sits in the middle: H–O–Cl (the formula is written HOCl to show this order).

(b) 1 + 6 + 7 = 14 valence electrons.

(c) Two single bonds use 4 electrons. Cl gets three lone pairs (6) and O gets two lone pairs (4), so 4 + 6 + 4 = 14. Final structure: H–O–Cl with two lone pairs on O and three lone pairs on Cl. O has 8 electrons, Cl has 8, H has 2. (Like water, the O has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs, so the molecule is bent; Section 6.4.)

Problem 6.21

Water's shape is why it dissolves the salts and sugars carried in blood. What is the molecular shape of H2O?

A. Linear, 180°
B. Bent, about 109.5°
C. Trigonal planar, 120°
D. Tetrahedral, 109.5°

Hint

Count electron groups on O, including lone pairs. Then name the shape from the atoms only, not from the flat Lewis drawing.

Answer

B. O has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs: 4 electron groups, so the electron geometry is tetrahedral. Only the 3 atoms are named in the shape, so the molecule is bent, with an angle of about 109.5° (measured: 104.5°, squeezed by the lone pairs).

  • A comes from reading the flat Lewis drawing H–O–H as the real shape and ignoring the two lone pairs on O.
  • C would need 3 electron groups; O in water has 4.
  • D is the electron-group geometry, which counts the lone pairs. The molecular shape names only where the atoms are.

Problem 6.22

Every breath you exhale carries carbon dioxide, O=C=O with two lone pairs on each O. How many electron groups surround the carbon, and what is the shape of CO2?

A. 4 groups; tetrahedral
B. 2 groups; linear
C. 2 groups; bent
D. 4 groups; bent

Hint

A double bond counts as one electron group, and only lone pairs on the central atom count. How many groups does carbon have?

Answer

B. Each double bond counts as one electron group, and carbon has no lone pairs, so C has 2 groups. Two groups point in opposite directions: linear, 180°.

  • A counts each double bond as two groups. A double bond is one region of electrons between the same two atoms, so it is one group.
  • C counts the lone pairs on the oxygen atoms. Only lone pairs on the central atom change the shape, and carbon has none.
  • D makes both mistakes: double bonds counted twice and outer lone pairs treated as if they were on carbon.

Problem 6.23

Methanol, CH3OH, has two atoms that each act as a center: the carbon and the oxygen (its Lewis structure is described in Section 6.3 above). Give the number of electron groups, the shape, and the approximate bond angle around (a) the carbon and (b) the oxygen.

Hint

Treat each center separately. Count bonds and lone pairs on C, then on O, and pop the lone pairs to name each shape.

Answer

(a) Carbon: four single bonds (three H, one O) and no lone pairs = 4 groups, 0 lone pairs: tetrahedral, 109.5°.

(b) Oxygen: two single bonds (C and H) and two lone pairs = 4 groups, 2 lone pairs: bent, about 109.5°, just like the oxygen in water.

Larger molecules are described one center at a time this way.

Problem 6.24

Acetylene, C2H2, is the fuel in the oxyacetylene torches used in farm and auto-body shops. Its Lewis structure is H–C≡C–H with no lone pairs. (a) Confirm the electron count. (b) What is the shape around each carbon? (c) What is the shape of the whole molecule?

Hint

(a) Count valence electrons and compare with the bonds drawn. (b) Count groups on each carbon (the triple bond is one group). (c) Put the two centers together.

Answer

(a) 2(4) + 2(1) = 10 valence electrons. Two C–H bonds (4) + one triple bond (6) = 10. Each carbon has 2 + 6 = 8 electrons and 4 bonds.

(b) Each carbon has 2 electron groups (one single bond and one triple bond) and no lone pairs: linear, 180°.

(c) Because both carbons are linear, all four atoms lie on one straight line: the whole molecule is linear.

Problem 6.25

Which pair of molecules or ions has the same molecular shape?

A. H2O and CO2
B. NH3 and CH2O
C. CH4 and NH4+
D. H2S and HCN

Hint

For each molecule or ion, count electron groups and lone pairs on the central atom. The same counts give the same shape, whatever the charge.

Answer

C. In both CH4 and NH4+, the central atom has four single bonds and no lone pairs (8 electrons each): 4 groups, 0 lone pairs, tetrahedral, 109.5°. The charge on NH4+ does not change the shape.

  • A: H2O is bent (4 groups, 2 lone pairs on O); CO2 is linear (2 groups, 0 lone pairs on C).
  • B: NH3 is trigonal pyramidal (4 groups, 1 lone pair); CH2O is trigonal planar (3 groups, 0 lone pairs).
  • D: H2S is bent (like water); HCN is linear (2 groups on C: a single bond and a triple bond).

Problem 6.26

In Experiment 6a, suppose the oxygen pieces in your model kit have four holes pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. You build water by placing two hydrogen pieces in two of the holes. (a) What do the two empty holes stand for? (b) What shape do the three atoms make? (c) A classmate builds ammonia with a four-hole nitrogen piece. How many holes stay empty, and what shape results?

Problem 6.28

Which bond is the most polar?

A. C–H
B. N–H
C. O–H
D. C–Cl

Hint

Compute ΔEN for each bond (larger minus smaller). The largest difference is the most polar bond.

Problem 6.30

Chloroform, CHCl3, was one of the first inhaled anesthetics (it is no longer used, because it damages the liver and heart). Carbon tetrachloride, CCl4, and methane, CH4, have the same tetrahedral shape. Which of these molecules is polar?

A. CCl4
B. CO2
C. CH4
D. CHCl3

Hint

Each has a basic shape with no lone pairs on carbon, so check the outer atoms (SNAP). Which molecule has outer atoms that are not all the same?

Answer

D. In CHCl3, carbon has four single bonds and no lone pairs (26 electrons in all): tetrahedral. But the four outer atoms are not identical (one H, three Cl). The three polar C–Cl bonds (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 0.5) are not balanced by a matching bond on the fourth corner, so the dipoles do not cancel: the molecule is asymmetrical and polar (SNAP).

  • A: CCl4 has four identical polar C–Cl bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. They cancel, so it is nonpolar.
  • B: CO2 has two polar C=O bonds, but it is linear and they point in opposite directions, so they cancel. Nonpolar.
  • C: CH4 has only C–H bonds (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 0.4, nonpolar) in a symmetrical tetrahedron. Nonpolar.

Problem 6.31

Which statement correctly describes the bond in hydrogen chloride, H–Cl? (H 2.1, Cl 3.0)

A. The bond is polar covalent; H is δ+, Cl is δ−, and the dipole arrow points toward Cl.
B. The bond is polar covalent; H is δ−, Cl is δ+, and the dipole arrow points toward H.
C. The bond is nonpolar, because both atoms are nonmetals.
D. The bond is ionic, made of H+ and Cl− ions.

Hint

Find ΔEN to classify the bond. Then decide which atom pulls the shared pair closer, which end gets δ−, and which way the dipole arrow points.

Answer

A. \( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, polar covalent. Chlorine, the more electronegative atom, pulls the shared pair closer, so it carries the partial negative charge, and the dipole arrow points toward it (with the cross on the H end).

  • B reverses the charges. The more electronegative atom is always the δ− end.

Problem 6.32

In the lungs, O2 and CO2 cross the thin, fatty (nonpolar) membranes of the alveoli and capillaries quickly. (a) Classify each of these gases as a polar or nonpolar molecule: O2, N2, CO2, H2O. (b) CO2 has polar bonds. Explain why it is still a nonpolar molecule.

Hint

(a) For each, check bond polarity and then shape. (b) What is the shape of CO2, and which way do its two bond dipoles point?

Answer

(a) O2, N2: nonpolar (identical atoms, \( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 0). CO2: nonpolar. H2O: polar (polar O–H bonds, \( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 1.4, in a bent shape).

Problem 6.33

Valley growers often inject anhydrous ammonia, NH3, into the soil as a nitrogen fertilizer, where it dissolves readily in soil water. (a) Is the N–H bond polar? (N 3.0, H 2.1) (b) What is the shape of NH3? (c) Is NH3 a polar molecule? Explain.

  • NaF: (a) sodium fluoride; (b) ionic (metal + nonmetal); (c) \( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 4.0 − 0.9 = 3.1, ionic.
  • CCl4: (a) carbon tetrachloride; (b) molecular; (c) C–Cl \( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 0.5, polar covalent; (d) 4 groups, 0 lone pairs on C, tetrahedral; four identical dipoles cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar.
  • PCl3: (a) phosphorus trichloride; (b) molecular; (c) P–Cl \( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 0.9, polar covalent; (d) 4 groups, 1 lone pair on P (as found in Section 6.3 above), trigonal pyramidal; the dipoles do not cancel, so the molecule is polar.

CCl4 and PCl3 both have polar bonds; only shape decides which molecule is polar.

Problem 6.51

Phosphorus is a key element in fertilizers, bones, and DNA. (a) Write its full and noble-gas electron configurations. (b) How many valence electrons does it have? (c) What ion does it form, what is the ion's name, and which noble gas does it match? (d) How many bonds and lone pairs does P have in PH3?

Hint

Count 15 electrons into orbitals in order. The valence electrons are the outermost s and p. How many more does P need to match the next noble gas?

Answer

(a) P has 15 electrons: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p3, or [Ne] 3s2 3p3.

(b) 5 valence electrons (3s2 3p3; group 15).

(c) It gains 3 electrons to reach an octet: P3−, phosphide, with 18 electrons, the configuration of argon.

(d) The same "needs 3 more" reasoning gives 3 bonds and 1 lone pair: in PH3, P shares 3 electrons instead of gaining them, and keeps its other 2 as a lone pair.

Problem 6.52

Potassium nitrate is the active ingredient in many toothpastes for sensitive teeth. (a) Write its formula (green sheet). (b) It contains both ionic and covalent bonds. Where is each kind? (c) Find its molar mass and percent potassium by mass. (d) How many moles of K+ ions are in 5.00 g of it? (N 14.007, O 15.999, K 39.10; EN N 3.0, O 3.5)

Hint

(a) Use the green sheet for nitrate. (b) Which pieces are held by attraction between ions, and which by shared electrons? (c) Part ÷ whole. (d) Find moles first; the goal is the mole.

Answer

(a) K+ and the nitrate ion, NO3−, combine 1 : 1: KNO3.

(b) The attraction between K+ and NO3− is ionic. Inside the nitrate ion, N and O (two nonmetals) share electrons in covalent bonds that are polar (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 0.5). Every compound with a polyatomic ion has both kinds.

(c) 39.10 + 14.007 + 3(15.999) = 101.10 g/mol; %K = 39.10 ÷ 101.10 × 100% = 38.67%.

(d) \( 5.00\ \text{g KNO}_3 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol KNO}_3}{101.10\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol K}^+}{1\ \text{mol KNO}_3} = 0.0495\ \text{mol K}^+ \)

(d) 0.0495 mol (4.95 × 10−2 mol) of K+, three significant figures like 5.00 g.

Putting It Together: More Problems (Sections 6.3–6.7)

These problems practice the ideas added to Sections 6.3–6.7: choosing the central atom by bond count, shapes at each center, driving-direction polarity, identity checks, percent as a conversion factor, and empirical formulas from moles. They are part of the main course, not Going Further.

Problem 6.53

Nitrosyl chloride, NOCl, is a toxic gas formed when nitric acid and hydrochloric acid are mixed. Which atom is central, and what is the shape around it?

A. O is central; bent
B. N is central; bent
C. Cl is central; linear
D. There is no central atom; linear

Hint

Use the usual bond counts: which atom forms the most bonds? Draw the structure, then count electron groups (including lone pairs) on that atom.

Answer

B. Usual bond counts: N 3, O 2, Cl 1, so N makes the most bonds and is central. Electrons: 5 + 6 + 7 = 18. Single bonds N–O and N–Cl use 4; Cl and O get three lone pairs each (12), and the last 2 go on N as a lone pair. N then has only 6 electrons and O only 1 bond, so one O lone pair becomes an N=O double bond. N has 3 groups (double bond, single bond, lone pair): bent, about 120°.

  • A puts the most electronegative atom in the middle; O makes only 2 bonds, fewer than N.
  • C puts a one-bond atom in the center; Cl, like H, belongs on the outside.
  • D is true only for two-atom molecules. Here N bonds to two atoms, so it is a central atom.

Problem 6.54

Which molecule is polar? (EN: H 2.1, C 2.5, F 4.0, Si 1.8, Cl 3.0, O 3.5)

A. CO2
B. CF4
C. CH3F
D. SiCl4

Hint

A molecule is polar if its bond arrows do not cancel. For each, check which bonds are polar (ΔEN above 0.4) and whether the outer atoms are all the same.

Answer

C. CH3F is tetrahedral, but only one bond is polar (C–F, 1.5); the three C–H bonds (0.4) are nonpolar. Following the bond arrows like driving directions, there is just one arrow, toward F, so you end up somewhere new: a net dipole.

  • A: two equal C=O arrows (1.0 each) point opposite ways on a linear molecule and cancel.
  • B: four equal C–F arrows point to the corners of a tetrahedron and cancel.
  • D: Si–Cl bonds are polar (1.2), but four identical bonds in a tetrahedron cancel, just like CF4.

Problem 6.55

Ethanol (CH3CH2OH) and dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3) have the same molecular formula, C2H6O. (a) For dimethyl ether, give the shape around each carbon and around the oxygen. (b) Is dimethyl ether polar? (c) What does this pair show about molecular formulas?

Hint

(a) Treat each carbon and the oxygen as separate centers and count their groups. (b) Are the two C–O dipoles pointing in opposite directions? (c) Compare the two structures.

Answer

(a) Electrons: 2(4) + 6(1) + 6 = 20. Each C has 4 single bonds (three C–H and one C–O): tetrahedral. The O has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs: bent, like water.

(b) Polar. The two C–O bonds are polar (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 1.0) and meet at a bent oxygen, so their arrows do not cancel. The C–H bonds are nonpolar.

(c) The same molecular formula can belong to different molecules with different structures and properties (as with glucose and fructose, Section 6.7). Ethanol has an O–H group; dimethyl ether does not, so the two behave differently (Chapter 9).

Problem 6.56

A bottle is labeled "iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3." A 2.000 g sample of the black powder inside contains 1.554 g of iron. (a) Is the sample Fe2O3? (b) Is it consistent with iron(II) oxide, FeO? (Fe 55.85, O 15.999)

Hint

Find the measured %Fe from the sample, then the %Fe expected from each formula. A pure compound must match its formula's percent.

Answer

Measured: 1.554 ÷ 2.000 × 100% = 77.70% Fe.

(a) Fe2O3: 2(55.85) = 111.70; 111.70 + 3(15.999) = 159.70 g/mol; %Fe = 111.70 ÷ 159.70 × 100% = 69.94%. The measured 77.70% does not match, so the sample fails the law of constant composition for Fe2O3; it is not what the label says.

(b) FeO: 55.85 + 15.999 = 71.85 g/mol; %Fe = 55.85 ÷ 71.85 × 100% = 77.73%. This matches 77.70% within rounding, so the sample is consistent with FeO. (Red-brown Fe2O3 and black FeO even look different.)

Problem 6.57

Urea, CO(NH2)2, is one of the most widely used nitrogen fertilizers on Valley farms. (a) Find its percent nitrogen by mass. (b) Use the percent as a conversion factor to find the mass of nitrogen in a 2.50 kg bag of pure urea. (C 12.011, H 1.008, N 14.007, O 15.999)

Hint

(a) Count the N atoms (the 2 outside the parentheses doubles NH2), find the molar mass, then part ÷ whole. (b) Use the percent as kg N per 100 kg urea.

Answer

(a) Molar mass: 12.011 + 15.999 + 2(14.007) + 4(1.008) = 60.056 g/mol. %N = 28.014 ÷ 60.056 × 100% = 46.65%.

(b) \( 2.50\ \text{kg urea} \times \dfrac{46.65\ \text{kg N}}{100\ \text{kg urea}} = 1.17\ \text{kg N} \)

Three significant figures, set by 2.50 kg. Almost half of urea's mass is nitrogen, which is why it is such an efficient fertilizer to ship.

Problem 6.58

An elemental analysis of a compound made by working muscles finds 0.400 mol C, 0.800 mol H, and 0.400 mol O. Its molar mass is 90.08 g/mol. What is its molecular formula? (C 12.011, H 1.008, O 15.999)

A. CH2O
B. C3H6O3
C. C2H4O2
D. C6H12O6

Hint

The data are already in moles: divide by the smallest to get the empirical formula, then n = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass.

Answer

B. Divide each mole value by the smallest (0.400): C 1, H 2, O 1, so the empirical formula is CH2O (30.026 g/mol). n = 90.08 ÷ 30.026 = 3.000, so the molecular formula is C3H6O3 (lactic acid). Check: 3(12.011) + 6(1.008) + 3(15.999) = 90.078 g/mol.

  • A is only the empirical formula. The analysis alone cannot give more, but the molar mass shows the molecule is 3 times bigger.
  • C would have a molar mass of 60.05 g/mol (n = 2), not 90.08.
  • D would have a molar mass of 180.16 g/mol (n = 6). It is glucose, which shares the empirical formula CH2O.

Problem 6.59

(Going Further, not on CHEM 3A exams unless assigned) Ground-level ozone, O3, is the Valley's summer smog pollutant. (a) How many valence electrons does O3 have? (b) Describe its two resonance structures. (c) What is its shape? (d) What does experiment show about the two O–O bonds?

Hint

(a) Add group-number electrons. (b) Try placing the double bond on each side of the central O. (c) Count groups on the central O. (d) Think about the resonance hybrid.

Answer

(a) 3(6) = 18 electrons.

(b) The central O is bonded to both outer O atoms; one bond is double and one is single. In each structure the central O has one lone pair, the double-bonded outer O has two lone pairs, and the single-bonded outer O has three: O=O–O ↔ O–O=O. Check: 3 bonding pairs + 6 lone pairs = 9 pairs = 18 electrons.

(c) The central O has 3 electron groups (one double bond, one single bond, one lone pair): bent, about 120° (measured, about 117°). The shape is the same in both resonance structures.

(d) Both bonds are the same length, between a single and a double bond. The real molecule is one resonance hybrid, not a molecule that switches back and forth.

Problem 6.60

(Going Further, not on CHEM 3A exams unless assigned) Nitrogen dioxide, NO2, is the brown gas in vehicle exhaust smog. (a) How many valence electrons does it have? (b) Why can its Lewis structure not give every atom an octet? (c) Which kind of octet exception is it, and what does that have to do with smog?

Hint

(a) Add group-number electrons. (b) Is the total odd or even? Bonds and lone pairs hold electrons in pairs. (c) What do we call a molecule with an unpaired electron?

Answer

(a) 5 + 2(6) = 17 electrons.

(b) 17 is an odd number. Bonds and lone pairs each hold 2 electrons, so one electron is always left unpaired, and one atom (N) ends up with only 7 electrons.

(c) It is an odd-electron molecule (a radical). The unpaired electron makes NO2 very reactive. In sunlight, NO2 splits into NO and a single O atom, and that O atom combines with O2 to form O3, ground-level ozone. That is why NO2 from traffic and engines drives the Valley's summer ozone.

Problem 6.61

(Going Further, not on CHEM 3A exams unless assigned) Nitrate, NO3−, from fertilizer can leach into groundwater, and it is tested for in many Valley drinking-water wells. (a) How many valence electrons does NO3− have? (b) How many resonance structures does it have, and what does each look like? (c) What is its shape and bond angle?

Hint

(a) Add one electron for the 1− charge. (b) How many different O atoms could hold the double bond? (c) Count groups on N; it has no lone pairs.

Answer

(a) 5 + 3(6) + 1 = 24 electrons.

(b) Three. N is central with one N=O double bond and two N–O single bonds and no lone pairs; the double-bonded O has two lone pairs and each single-bonded O has three. Any of the three O atoms can be the double-bonded one. Each structure is in brackets with a 1− charge. Check: 4 bonding pairs + 8 lone pairs = 12 pairs = 24 electrons. All three real N–O bonds are identical, each about one and one-third bonds.

(c) N has 3 electron groups and no lone pairs: trigonal planar, 120°, in every resonance structure.
