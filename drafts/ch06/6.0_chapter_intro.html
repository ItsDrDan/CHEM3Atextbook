Chemistry in the Community: Ozone Over the Valley

On a still July afternoon in Fresno, Bakersfield, or Visalia, the air can look clean and still be unhealthy to breathe. The culprit is ozone, O3, a molecule made of three oxygen atoms. High in the stratosphere, ozone shields the Earth from ultraviolet light (Chapter 3). At ground level it is a pollutant: it forms when nitrogen oxides from vehicles, trucks, and engines react with vapors from fuels, solvents, and plants in strong sunlight, and the Valley's mountains, heat, and stagnant summer air trap it. Several San Joaquin Valley metropolitan areas consistently rank among the most ozone-polluted in the United States in the American Lung Association's State of the Air reports, and the San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District issues daily air-quality forecasts so schools and outdoor workers can plan.

Why is O3 so harmful when O2, the oxygen we need, is not? The answer is in how the atoms are bonded and what shape the molecule takes. Ozone's bonds and bent shape make it reactive enough to damage the lining of the airways, which is why ozone days trigger asthma attacks and coughing even in healthy people working outdoors. In this chapter you will learn to draw molecules like O2, O3, H2O, and CO2, predict their shapes, and decide whether they are polar, and you will learn how chemists work out a compound's formula from the masses of its elements.

Sources: American Lung Association, State of the Air (lung.org/research/sota); San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District (valleyair.org); U.S. EPA, Ground-level Ozone Basics.

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Describe ionic bonding and the three kinds of ionic compounds in this course: salts, bases, and oxides (Section 6.1).
  • Describe covalent bonds and predict how many bonds an atom usually forms (Section 6.2).
  • Draw Lewis structures for molecules with one central atom, simple organic molecules, and polyatomic ions (Section 6.3).
  • Predict electron-group and molecular shapes with VSEPR theory for up to four electron groups (Section 6.4).
  • Use electronegativity to classify bonds, decide whether a molecule is polar, and work the full chain from formula to shape and polarity for larger molecules (Section 6.5).
  • Calculate percent composition by mass from a formula or from measured masses, and use it to check a compound's identity and as a conversion factor (Section 6.6).
  • Find empirical and molecular formulas from mass or percent data (Section 6.7).

Why This Chapter Matters

Shape and polarity explain a remarkable amount of everyday chemistry and biology: why water dissolves salt but not oil, why oxygen and carbon dioxide slip through cell membranes while ions need channels, why soap works, and why some drugs reach the brain and others do not. Chapter 9 builds on this chapter's polarity to explain intermolecular forces, boiling points, and solubility, and Chapter 7 uses formulas to balance reactions.
