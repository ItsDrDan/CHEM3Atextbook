Learning Objectives

  • Define electronegativity and describe its periodic trend.
  • Classify a bond as nonpolar covalent, polar covalent, or ionic from the electronegativity difference.
  • Show bond polarity with partial charges and dipole arrows.
  • Use shape to decide whether a molecule is polar or nonpolar, following bond dipoles like driving directions.
  • Explain why polarity decides whether substances mix (miscibility).
  • Work the full chain for a larger molecule: Lewis structure, shape at each center, and polar and nonpolar regions.

Not all sharing is equal. When two different atoms share electrons, one usually pulls the shared pair closer. That uneven pull, multiplied over a whole molecule and its shape, decides whether a substance mixes with water, how high it boils, and whether it can cross a cell membrane.

Electronegativity

Electronegativity (EN) is an atom's ability to attract the shared electrons in a bond toward itself. The values on your yellow sheet are on the Pauling scale, from about 0.7 to 4.0. Electronegativity follows the same trend as ionization energy (Section 3.6): it increases across a period and decreases down a group. Fluorine (4.0) is the most electronegative element, followed by oxygen (3.5), then nitrogen and chlorine (3.0). Metals on the left have low values (Na 0.9, K 0.8).

The reason is the same as for ionization energy: an atom up and to the right holds its valence electrons closer to a more strongly attracting nucleus, so it is "greedy" for electrons in a bond as well. Fluorine, one electron short of an octet, pulls hardest of all. Some exams do not print electronegativity values, so learn the trend, not just the numbers: up and to the right is more electronegative.

Bond Polarity

The electronegativity difference, \( \Delta \text{EN} \), between two bonded atoms tells you what kind of bond they form:

  • \( \Delta \text{EN} \) of 0 to 0.4: nonpolar covalent. The electrons are shared (almost) equally: H–H, Cl–Cl, and C–H (2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4).
  • \( \Delta \text{EN} \) of more than 0.4 up to 2.0: polar covalent. The more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other a partial positive charge (δ+): O–H (1.4), N–H (0.9), C–O (1.0), C–Cl (0.5), H–F (1.9).
  • \( \Delta \text{EN} \) greater than 2.0: ionic. The electron has essentially transferred: Na–Cl (2.1), K–F (3.2).

A polar bond is shown with δ+ and δ− labels or with a dipole arrow that points toward the more electronegative atom, with a small cross on the positive end. Bond types form a continuum, so these cutoffs are guidelines. A few metal–nonmetal compounds such as AlCl3 (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 1.5) fall in the polar-covalent range; in this course you still name them as ionic compounds (Chapter 4).

Picturing unequal sharing

A covalent bond is like two friends splitting an order of fries. Between two identical atoms (C–C, Cl–Cl) the fries are cut right down the middle: a nonpolar bond, with the electron cloud spread evenly between the atoms. In a C–O bond, oxygen is the friend who sneaks a few extra fries when you are not looking: the bond is polar, and the electron cloud is shaped like a teardrop, fatter on the oxygen end. In a C–F bond it is like sharing a milkshake with a little kid, who takes most of it: very polar. You can also picture a seesaw or a tug of war, tipped toward the atom with the higher electronegativity. A bond really is a region where the electrons are more likely to be found near the more electronegative atom.

Some bonds come up so often that it pays to simply memorize them:

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Bonds worth memorizing (yellow-sheet values)
BondΔEN (electronegativity difference)Type
X–X (two identical atoms, such as C–C)0nonpolar
C–H0.4nonpolar (an extremely small dipole)
C–N, H–N0.5, 0.9polar
C–O, H–O1.0, 1.4polar
C–F, H–F1.5, 1.9polar
C–Cl, H–Cl0.5, 0.9polar

In short: carbon and hydrogen bonded to each other are nonpolar; carbon or hydrogen bonded to N, O, F, or Cl is polar.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Classify bonds

Problem. Use the yellow-sheet values (H 2.1, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, Mg 1.2, Cl 3.0, S 2.5) to classify (a) C–S; (b) N–H; (c) Mg–F; (d) H–Cl.

Solution. (a) 2.5 − 2.5 = 0.0, nonpolar covalent. (b) 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, polar covalent, N is δ−. (c) 4.0 − 1.2 = 2.8, ionic. (d) 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, polar covalent, Cl is δ−.

Check. Subtract the smaller value from the larger, so \( \Delta \text{EN} \) is never negative.

Polar and Nonpolar Molecules

A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel, leaving one side of the molecule slightly negative and the other slightly positive (a net dipole). Whether they cancel depends on shape:

  • A molecule with no polar bonds is nonpolar: H2, O2, N2, Cl2, and hydrocarbons such as CH4.
  • A symmetrical molecule (linear, trigonal planar, or tetrahedral with all identical outer atoms and no lone pairs on the center) is nonpolar even if its bonds are polar, because the dipoles cancel: CO2, CCl4.
  • An asymmetrical molecule, one with lone pairs on the central atom (bent, trigonal pyramidal) or with different outer atoms, is usually polar: H2O, NH3, CH3Cl, HCN.

A memory aid from the CHEM 3A lectures: SNAP, Symmetrical is Nonpolar, Asymmetrical is Polar.

Put another way, a molecule is polar when its polar bonds work together to pile electrons up on one side. The "basic" shapes (linear, trigonal planar, and tetrahedral) can be nonpolar, but only when the central atom has no lone pairs and the same atom sits on every outer position. Pulling in opposite directions with equal strength is like playing tug of war against your twin: both sides pull hard, and nobody moves. Swap just one outer atom for a different one and the balance is broken. A linear B–A–B molecule can be nonpolar, but B–A–C is usually polar. A few substances are nonpolar every time: CO2, the noble gases, and the seven diatomic elements (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2: "Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Beer").

Arrows as driving directions

Here is a reliable way to decide. Draw an arrow for each polar bond, pointing toward the more electronegative atom, and make bigger \( \Delta \text{EN} \) values longer arrows. Now treat the arrows as driving directions: start at the central atom, follow one arrow, then the next, and so on, keeping each arrow's direction and length. If you end up back where you started, the dipoles cancel and the molecule is nonpolar. If you end up somewhere new, there is a net dipole and the molecule is polar. A bond with \( \Delta \text{EN} \) of 0.4 or less (such as C–H) gets no arrow.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Polar or nonpolar?

Problem. Decide whether each molecule is polar: (a) CCl4; (b) CH2Cl2; (c) NH3; (d) CO2.

Solution.

  • (a) Tetrahedral, four identical polar C–Cl bonds pointing symmetrically: dipoles cancel, nonpolar.
  • (b) Tetrahedral, but two C–H and two C–Cl bonds: not symmetrical, polar.
  • (c) Trigonal pyramidal, with a lone pair: polar.
  • (d) Linear, two identical C=O dipoles pointing opposite ways: nonpolar.

Check. Polar bonds are necessary for a polar molecule, but not enough; the shape must keep them from canceling.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): The power of one atom

Problem. CCl4 (carbon tetrachloride) and CHCl3 (chloroform) are both tetrahedral. Use bond arrows as driving directions to decide which is polar.

Solution. C–Cl: 3.0 − 2.5 = 0.5, polar, so each C–Cl bond gets an arrow pointing toward Cl. C–H: 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4, nonpolar, so no arrow. In CCl4, four equal arrows point to the four corners of a tetrahedron; following them in turn brings you back to the start, so the dipoles cancel: nonpolar. In CHCl3, one corner holds an H instead of a Cl, so there are only three arrows, all pointing toward the Cl side. Following them leaves you on the Cl side of the molecule: a net dipole, polar.

Check. Replacing a single atom turned a nonpolar molecule into a polar one. The same thing happens with CH4 (nonpolar, no polar bonds) and CH3Cl (one polar bond, so polar). Changing one atom changes the molecule's properties, including whether it dissolves in water.

Chemists also map polarity with an electrostatic potential map, a picture of the molecule's electron cloud shaded by electron density. By the usual convention, red marks regions of high electron density (partial negative) and blue marks regions of low electron density (partial positive). For water, the region around the oxygen atom is red (δ−) and the regions around the two hydrogen atoms are blue (δ+), which is the bent dipole you predicted from the shape.

Why Polarity Matters: Mixing

Polarity is not just a label; it predicts behavior. Polar molecules have partially charged ends, and the δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of its neighbor. That is why polar substances mix with polar substances, forming homogeneous mixtures; the ability of two substances to mix is called miscibility. A nonpolar substance cannot mix in, because it has no partial charges with which to pull the polar molecules apart. Oil (nonpolar) and water (polar) separate for exactly this reason.

  • Acetone, (CH3)2CO, the solvent in many nail polish removers, has a polar C=O group and mixes with water in any amount.
  • Acetic acid, CH3COOH, has polar C=O and O–H bonds and mixes with water too; vinegar is a solution of it in water.
  • Carbon dioxide has polar bonds but is a nonpolar molecule (linear), so it dissolves in water only slightly; that is why carbonated drinks have to be bottled under pressure.

You will meet the attractions between molecules by name in Chapter 9 (intermolecular forces, Section 9.6), and the rule "like dissolves like" in Chapter 11 (Section 11.1).

Putting It Together: Bigger Molecules, Shapes Around Each Center, and Polar Regions

Everything in Sections 6.2–6.5 links into one chain, and exam questions often ask for the whole chain at once:

formula → Lewis structure → electron groups and shape at each center → bond polarity → molecular polarity

Small molecules have one central atom, but most molecules in biology have several. The rule is the same at every center: any atom bonded to two or more other atoms is a center, and its shape comes from its own electron groups. You can be asked for "the geometry around this carbon" or "the shape around the oxygen" in a larger molecule, so treat each center separately. Bigger molecules can also have a polar region and a nonpolar region in the same molecule, which turns out to matter a great deal in the body.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Ethanol, CH3CH2OH

Problem. Ethanol is the alcohol in hand sanitizers and in alcoholic drinks. (a) Draw its Lewis structure. (b) Give the shape around each carbon and around the oxygen. (c) Identify its polar and nonpolar regions, and decide whether ethanol is a polar molecule.

Solution. (a) The formula shows the connections: CH3–CH2–O–H. Valence electrons: 2(4) + 6(1) + 6 = 20. Eight single bonds (5 C–H, 1 C–C, 1 C–O, 1 O–H) use 16, and the last 4 go on O as two lone pairs. Bond counts check: each C has 4, O has 2, each H has 1.

(b) Each carbon has 4 bonding groups and no lone pairs: tetrahedral, 109.5°. The oxygen has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs: bent, like water.

(c) C–C (0) and C–H (0.4) are nonpolar, so the CH3CH2– end is a nonpolar region. C–O (1.0) and O–H (1.4) are polar, and they meet at a bent oxygen, so the –OH end is a polar region. The dipoles at the OH end cannot be canceled by anything at the other end, so ethanol is a polar molecule.

Check. Ethanol mixes with water in any amount, as a polar molecule should, but its nonpolar end also lets it dissolve oils, which is part of why alcohol-based sanitizers can disrupt the fatty outer envelope of some viruses.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Acetic acid, CH3COOH

Problem. Acetic acid (vinegar, Section 4.5) has the connections CH3–C(=O)–O–H. Give the shape around the CH3 carbon, the second carbon, and the O of the O–H group.

Solution. Valence electrons: 2(4) + 4(1) + 2(6) = 24. With single bonds everywhere (7 bonds, 14 electrons) and the remaining 10 as lone pairs (three on the end O, two on the O of O–H), the second carbon has only 3 bonds. One lone pair from the end O becomes a C=O double bond. Now:

  • CH3 carbon: 4 bonding groups, no lone pairs: tetrahedral, 109.5°.
  • Second carbon: 3 groups (C–C, C=O, C–O; the double bond counts once), no lone pairs: trigonal planar, 120°.
  • O of the O–H group: 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs: bent.

Check. Electrons: 8 bonding pairs (16 electrons, counting C=O as two pairs) + 4 lone pairs (8 electrons) = 24. The polar C=O and O–H bonds make the COOH end a polar region, so acetic acid is polar and mixes with water.

Biology Connection: Polar Heads, Nonpolar Tails

Molecules with both a polar region and a nonpolar region are everywhere in your body. The phospholipids that make up every cell membrane have a polar "head" that faces the watery fluid inside and outside the cell and two long nonpolar hydrocarbon "tails" that hide from water in the middle of the membrane. The result is a thin, oily barrier: small nonpolar molecules such as O2 and CO2 slip across it, while ions and large polar molecules such as glucose need protein channels or carriers. Many drugs are designed with the right balance of polar and nonpolar regions so that they dissolve in blood but can still cross membranes to reach their targets. Soaps and bile salts work the same way, with one end that likes water and one end that likes fat.

Professor Tip

Work in order: Lewis structure, then shape, then polarity. Exam questions on this chapter usually come down to four things: bond counts, a simple Lewis structure, the shape, and polarity. Students who try to judge polarity from the formula alone get CO2 versus SO2 wrong every time. CO2 is linear (no lone pairs on C) and nonpolar; SO2 has a lone pair on S, is bent, and is polar. Memorize the bond facts from lecture too: C–H is essentially nonpolar, and bonds from C or H to N, O, F, or Cl are polar.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

The gases a respiratory therapist works with show every case in this section. O2 and N2 have identical atoms and are nonpolar. CO2 has polar C=O bonds but is linear, so it is nonpolar. Water vapor, which humidifiers add to dry medical gases, is bent and polar. Nitrous oxide, N2O (N–N–O, linear but with different atoms on each end), is slightly polar. Small nonpolar gases such as O2 and CO2 move easily across the thin, fatty (nonpolar) membranes of the alveoli and capillaries, which is why gas exchange in the lungs is so fast, while ions like Na+ and Cl− cannot cross those membranes without protein channels.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing a polar bond with a polar molecule. CO2 has polar bonds but is a nonpolar molecule.
  • Skipping the shape. Polarity depends on geometry, so draw the Lewis structure and find the shape first.
  • Pointing the dipole arrow the wrong way. It points toward the more electronegative atom (the δ− end).
  • Calling a "basic" shape nonpolar without checking the outer atoms. Tetrahedral CCl4 is nonpolar, but tetrahedral CHCl3 is polar: one different atom breaks the symmetry.
  • Giving a big molecule one shape. In ethanol, each C is tetrahedral and the O is bent; name the shape at the center you are asked about.
  • Thinking a molecule with a nonpolar region must be nonpolar. Ethanol has a nonpolar CH3CH2– region, but its polar OH end makes the whole molecule polar.

Summary

  • Electronegativity increases across a period and up a group; F is highest.
  • \( \Delta \text{EN} \): 0–0.4 nonpolar covalent; above 0.4 to 2.0 polar covalent; above 2.0 ionic.
  • A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel: symmetrical shapes with identical outer atoms and no lone pairs on the center are nonpolar; bent and trigonal pyramidal molecules are polar. Follow the bond arrows like driving directions: back to the start means nonpolar.
  • One atom can change everything: CCl4 is nonpolar, CHCl3 is polar.
  • Polar mixes with polar; nonpolar cannot pull polar molecules apart (miscibility).
  • In a larger molecule, find the shape at each center and look for polar and nonpolar regions (ethanol: tetrahedral carbons, bent O, polar OH end).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Rank these bonds from least to most polar: C–O, C–H, H–F, N–H.

Answer

C–H (0.4) < N–H (0.9) < C–O (1.0) < H–F (1.9).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Is PCl3 polar or nonpolar? Explain.

Answer

P has 3 bonds and 1 lone pair, so PCl3 is trigonal pyramidal. Each P–Cl bond is polar (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9), and the pyramid is asymmetrical, so the dipoles do not cancel: polar.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

BF3 is trigonal planar, and NF3 is trigonal pyramidal. Which is polar?

Answer

NF3. In BF3 the three identical B–F dipoles point 120° apart in a plane and cancel. In NF3 the lone pair makes the molecule a pyramid, so the N–F dipoles do not cancel. (BF3's Lewis structure is an octet exception; see Going Further.)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Use bond arrows as driving directions to decide whether each is polar: (a) CH2F2; (b) CF4; (c) OCS (linear, C in the center).

Answer

(a) Polar: tetrahedral, but only two arrows (C–F, 1.5 each), both toward the F side; C–H (0.4) gets none. You do not return to the start. (b) Nonpolar: four equal C–F arrows to the corners of a tetrahedron cancel. (c) Polar: C=O (1.0) and C=S (0.0) are not equal, so the two arrows do not cancel (B–A–C is usually polar).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Acetone, (CH3)2CO, has a central carbon double-bonded to O and single-bonded to two CH3 groups. (a) Give the shape around the central carbon and around each CH3 carbon. (b) Is acetone polar, and does it mix with water?

Answer

(a) Central C: 3 groups (two C–C and one C=O), no lone pairs: trigonal planar, 120°. Each CH3 carbon: 4 groups: tetrahedral, 109.5°. (b) Polar: the C=O bond (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 1.0) has nothing to cancel it, while the C–H and C–C bonds are nonpolar. As a polar molecule, it mixes with water in any amount.
