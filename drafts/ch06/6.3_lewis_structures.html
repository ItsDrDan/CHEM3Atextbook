Learning Objectives

  • Follow a step-by-step method to draw Lewis structures.
  • Choose the central atom and count total valence electrons, including for ions.
  • Draw Lewis structures for molecules with one central atom, simple organic molecules, and polyatomic ions.

Drawing a Lewis structure is a puzzle with simple rules: use exactly the valence electrons available, and give every atom an octet (hydrogen a duet). With a consistent method, you can solve nearly every molecule in this course in under a minute.

The Method

  1. Skeleton. Choose the central atom: usually the atom that appears once, or the one farther down and to the left on the periodic table (the less electronegative atom, Section 6.5). Hydrogen is never central, and halogens are usually on the outside. The formula often hints at the arrangement: CH3OH means a CH3 group bonded to an OH group.
  2. Count the total valence electrons from the group numbers. For an ion, add one electron for each negative charge and subtract one for each positive charge.
  3. Connect the atoms with single bonds (2 electrons each), then place the remaining electrons as lone pairs, outer atoms first (except H), then the central atom.
  4. Check octets. If the central atom has fewer than 8 electrons, move a lone pair from a neighboring atom into a double bond (or two, for a triple bond) until every atom is satisfied. Never add electrons you do not have.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Ammonia, NH3

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of NH3.

Solution. (1) N is central (H is never central). (2) 5 + 3(1) = 8 electrons. (3) Three N–H bonds use 6; the last 2 go on N as a lone pair. (4) N has 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 8 electrons; each H has 2. Done: N bonded to three H atoms with one lone pair (Section 6.2).

Check. N forms 3 bonds, as HONC predicts.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Carbon dioxide, CO2

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of CO2.

Solution. (1) C is central: O–C–O. (2) 4 + 2(6) = 16 electrons. (3) Two single bonds use 4; the other 12 go as three lone pairs on each O. Now C has only 4 electrons. (4) Move one lone pair from each O into a bond: O=C=O, with two lone pairs on each O. C has 8 (two double bonds) and each O has 8.

Check. 4 bonding pairs (8 electrons) + 4 lone pairs (8 electrons) = 16. Carbon has 4 bonds.

Simple organic molecules and ions

Molecules with two "central" atoms, such as methanol (CH3OH) or ethylene (C2H4), follow the same steps: build the skeleton from the formula and HONC, then complete octets. For a polyatomic ion, adjust the electron count for the charge, and draw the finished structure in square brackets with the charge outside.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): The hypochlorite ion, ClO−

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of hypochlorite, ClO−, the active ion in bleach (Section 4.6).

Solution. (2) 7 + 6 + 1 = 14 electrons. (3) One Cl–O bond uses 2; the other 12 make three lone pairs on each atom. (4) Cl has 8 and O has 8. Draw it in brackets with a 1− charge: [Cl–O]−, three lone pairs on each atom.

Check. 2 + 12 = 14 electrons used.

Professor Tip

Keep a running tally in the margin: "16 total, 4 used in bonds, 12 left." Most wrong Lewis structures come from losing count. When you finish, count every dot and line (2 per line) once more and compare with your total. If you are short of octets with no electrons left, that is your signal to make a double or triple bond, never to add extra electrons.

In the Lab: Experiment 6a, Molecular Modeling

In this lab you draw Lewis structures from formulas and then build each molecule with a model kit, using balls for atoms and sticks or springs for bonds (two flexible connectors for a double bond, three for a triple). You also work backward: given a finished model, you write its formula and Lewis structure. Holding the model is the best way to see that a Lewis drawing is flat but the real molecule is not, which leads directly into Section 6.4.

Common Mistakes

  • Putting H in the center. H forms only one bond.
  • Forgetting the ion charge when counting electrons (CN− has 10, not 9).
  • Adding electrons to complete octets instead of making multiple bonds.
  • Leaving lone pairs off outer atoms. Each O in CO2 still has two lone pairs.

Summary

  • Skeleton (least electronegative atom central; H never central) → count valence electrons (adjust for charge) → single bonds and lone pairs → make multiple bonds if octets are short.
  • Every electron must be used exactly once; every atom gets an octet (H: duet).
  • Polyatomic ions are drawn in brackets with the charge outside.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many valence electrons are in (a) CH4; (b) SO2; (c) NH4+; (d) OH−?

Answer

(a) 4 + 4 = 8. (b) 6 + 12 = 18. (c) 5 + 4 − 1 = 8. (d) 6 + 1 + 1 = 8.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Draw the Lewis structure of H2S. How many lone pairs are on S?

Answer

8 electrons: S central, two S–H bonds (4 electrons), two lone pairs on S (4 electrons). Two lone pairs, like water.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Draw the Lewis structure of HCN and explain why it needs a triple bond.

Answer

10 electrons. With H–C–N single bonds (4 electrons) and the remaining 6 on N, C has only 4 electrons. Moving two lone pairs from N into the C–N bond gives H–C≡N with one lone pair on N: C has 8, N has 8, H has 2.
