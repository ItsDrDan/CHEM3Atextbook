Learning Objectives

  • Follow a step-by-step method to draw Lewis structures.
  • Choose the central atom from the number of bonds each atom forms, and count total valence electrons, including for ions.
  • Use usual bond counts (HONC) to find the arrangement of atoms in molecules with several outer atoms, such as CHOCl.
  • Draw Lewis structures for molecules with one central atom, simple organic molecules, and polyatomic ions.

Drawing a Lewis structure is a puzzle with simple rules: use exactly the valence electrons available, and give every atom an octet (hydrogen a duet). With a consistent method, you can solve nearly every molecule in this course in under a minute.

The Method

  1. Skeleton. Choose the central atom: the atom that can bond to the most things, the "center of attention." Use the usual bond counts from Section 6.2: C makes 4 bonds, N 3, O 2, and H and the halogens 1, so carbon is the center whenever it is present. When two atoms tie, the one farther down and to the left on the periodic table (the less electronegative atom, Section 6.5) goes in the middle. Hydrogen is never central, and halogens are usually on the outside. The formula often hints at the arrangement: CH3OH means a CH3 group bonded to an OH group.
  2. Count the total valence electrons from the group numbers. For an ion, add one electron for each negative charge and subtract one for each positive charge.
  3. Connect the atoms with single bonds (2 electrons each), then place the remaining electrons as lone pairs, outer atoms first (except H), then the central atom.
  4. Check octets. If the central atom has fewer than 8 electrons, move a lone pair from a neighboring atom into a double bond (or two, for a triple bond) until every atom is satisfied. Never add electrons you do not have.

A central atom has to bond to two or more other atoms. A molecule with only two atoms, such as O2, N2, or HCl, has no central atom at all: just connect the two atoms and complete their octets.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Ammonia, NH3

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of NH3.

Solution. (1) N is central (H is never central). (2) 5 + 3(1) = 8 electrons. (3) Three N–H bonds use 6; the last 2 go on N as a lone pair. (4) N has 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 8 electrons; each H has 2. Done: N bonded to three H atoms with one lone pair (Section 6.2).

Check. N forms 3 bonds, as HONC predicts.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Carbon dioxide, CO2

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of CO2.

Solution. (1) C is central: O–C–O. (2) 4 + 2(6) = 16 electrons. (3) Two single bonds use 4; the other 12 go as three lone pairs on each O. Now C has only 4 electrons. (4) Move one lone pair from each O into a bond: O=C=O, with two lone pairs on each O. C has 8 (two double bonds) and each O has 8.

Check. 4 bonding pairs (8 electrons) + 4 lone pairs (8 electrons) = 16. Carbon has 4 bonds.

Simple organic molecules and ions

Molecules with two "central" atoms, such as methanol (CH3OH) or ethylene (C2H4), follow the same steps: build the skeleton from the formula and HONC, then complete octets. For a polyatomic ion, adjust the electron count for the charge, and draw the finished structure in square brackets with the charge outside.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): The hypochlorite ion, ClO−

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of hypochlorite, ClO−, the active ion in bleach (Section 4.6).

Solution. (2) 7 + 6 + 1 = 14 electrons. (3) One Cl–O bond uses 2; the other 12 make three lone pairs on each atom. (4) Cl has 8 and O has 8. Draw it in brackets with a 1− charge: [Cl–O]−, three lone pairs on each atom.

Check. 2 + 12 = 14 electrons used.

Let the bond counts build the skeleton

For a molecule with several different outer atoms, you do not need trial and error. Label each atom with the number of bonds it usually makes, put the atom with the highest number in the middle, attach the one-bond atoms (H and halogens) with single bonds, and let any atom that still "wants" more bonds form a double or triple bond. The formula's order often hints at the connections too.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): CHOCl from bond counts

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of CHOCl (formyl chloride). At first glance, it is hard to tell how these four atoms connect.

Solution. Label the usual bond counts: C 4, H 1, O 2, Cl 1. (1) Carbon makes the most bonds, so C is central, with H, O, and Cl around it. (2) Valence electrons: 4 + 1 + 6 + 7 = 18. (3) Single bonds from C to H, O, and Cl use 6 electrons. H is done. Cl gets three lone pairs (6 electrons), and O gets three lone pairs (6 electrons): 6 + 6 + 6 = 18, all used. (4) Carbon has only 3 bonds (6 electrons), and oxygen has only 1 bond, but O "wants" 2. Move one lone pair from O into the C–O bond: C=O. The result: C bonded to H (single), to Cl (single, Cl with three lone pairs), and to O (double, O with two lone pairs).

Check. Bond counts now match HONC: C 4, H 1, O 2, Cl 1. Electrons: 3 bonds = 4 bonding pairs (2 + 2 + 4 = 8 electrons) + 5 lone pairs (10 electrons) = 18. Every atom has an octet, and H has a duet.

Professor Tip: Line Up the Single Electrons

When you draw each atom's dot symbol before connecting them, put the unpaired dots facing each other. Each pair of facing single electrons becomes one single bond, so the skeleton almost draws itself and you erase far less. If octets come up short after all the electrons are placed, the fix is always the same: erase a lone pair and reuse it as a double bond (or two, for a triple bond).

Professor Tip

Keep a running tally in the margin: "16 total, 4 used in bonds, 12 left." Most wrong Lewis structures come from losing count. When you finish, count every dot and line (2 per line) once more and compare with your total. If you are short of octets with no electrons left, that is your signal to make a double or triple bond, never to add extra electrons.

In the Lab: Experiment 6a, Molecular Modeling

In this lab you draw Lewis structures from formulas and then build each molecule with a model kit, using balls for atoms and sticks or springs for bonds (two flexible connectors for a double bond, three for a triple). You also work backward: given a finished model, you write its formula and Lewis structure. Holding the model is the best way to see that a Lewis drawing is flat but the real molecule is not, which leads directly into Section 6.4.

Common Mistakes

  • Putting H in the center. H forms only one bond.
  • Forgetting the ion charge when counting electrons (CN− has 10, not 9).
  • Adding electrons to complete octets instead of making multiple bonds.
  • Leaving lone pairs off outer atoms. Each O in CO2 still has two lone pairs.
  • Choosing the central atom by guesswork. Pick the atom that makes the most bonds (C before N before O); H and halogens, with one bond each, go on the outside.
  • Treating a molecule, an ion, and an ionic compound as the same thing. ClO2 (chlorine dioxide, a molecule), ClO2− (chlorite, a polyatomic ion), and NaClO2 (sodium chlorite, an ionic compound) have different electron counts and different names (Section 4.6). Only the ion's Lewis structure goes in brackets.

Summary

  • Skeleton (the atom that makes the most bonds is central, then least electronegative; H never central; a central atom bonds to 2 or more atoms) → count valence electrons (adjust for charge) → single bonds and lone pairs → make multiple bonds if octets are short.
  • Every electron must be used exactly once; every atom gets an octet (H: duet).
  • Polyatomic ions are drawn in brackets with the charge outside.
  • For molecules like CHOCl, label usual bond counts (C 4, N 3, O 2, H and halogens 1); the atom still short of bonds forms the double or triple bond.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many valence electrons are in (a) CH4; (b) SO2; (c) NH4+; (d) OH−?

Hint

Add the group-number valence electrons of every atom. For an ion, add one electron per negative charge and subtract one per positive charge.

Answer

(a) 4 + 4 = 8. (b) 6 + 12 = 18. (c) 5 + 4 − 1 = 8. (d) 6 + 1 + 1 = 8.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Draw the Lewis structure of H2S. How many lone pairs are on S?

Hint

Count valence electrons, put S in the center (H is never central), connect with single bonds, then place what is left as lone pairs. Compare with water.

Answer

8 electrons: S central, two S–H bonds (4 electrons), two lone pairs on S (4 electrons). Two lone pairs, like water.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Draw the Lewis structure of HCN and explain why it needs a triple bond.

Hint

Count 10 electrons and connect H–C–N with single bonds. Check octets: if C is short, move lone pairs from N into the C–N bond.

Answer

10 electrons. With H–C–N single bonds (4 electrons) and the remaining 6 on N, C has only 4 electrons. Moving two lone pairs from N into the C–N bond gives H–C≡N with one lone pair on N: C has 8, N has 8, H has 2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Use bond counts to draw the Lewis structure of COCl2 (phosgene, a toxic industrial gas). Which bond is a double bond?

Hint

Let the bond counts build the skeleton: which atom bonds to the most things? After single bonds and lone pairs, check octets and erase a lone pair to make a double bond where needed.

Answer

C 4, O 2, Cl 1, so C is central with O and two Cl around it. Electrons: 4 + 6 + 2(7) = 24. Three single bonds use 6; 18 go as lone pairs (three on each Cl, three on O). Carbon has only 6 electrons and O only 1 bond, so one lone pair on O becomes a C=O double bond. Final: C double-bonded to O (two lone pairs) and single-bonded to each Cl (three lone pairs each). 4 bonding pairs (8 electrons) + 8 lone pairs (16) = 24.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

The formula CH3CHO (acetaldehyde) hints at its connections: a CH3 group bonded to a C that also carries an H and an O. Draw its Lewis structure. How many electrons, and where is the double bond?

Hint

Count the electrons, build the skeleton from the formula with single bonds, and place lone pairs. Which atoms are short of their usual bond counts?

Answer

2(4) + 4(1) + 6 = 18 electrons. Skeleton: H3C–C(H)–O with single bonds (6 bonds, 12 electrons). The remaining 6 go on O as three lone pairs. The second carbon has only 3 bonds and O has only 1, so one O lone pair becomes a C=O double bond, leaving O with two lone pairs. Each C now has 4 bonds, O has 2, and each H has 1.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

Draw the Lewis structure of the amide ion, NH2−.

Hint

Count valence electrons for an ion: add one for the 1− charge. Put N in the center, and remember the brackets and the charge outside.

Answer

5 + 2(1) + 1 = 8 electrons. N is central with two N–H bonds (4 electrons) and two lone pairs (4 electrons), drawn in brackets with a 1− charge outside: [H–N–H]− with two lone pairs on N.
