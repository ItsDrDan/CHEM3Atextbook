Learning Objectives

  • Count electron groups around a central atom from a Lewis structure.
  • Predict electron-group geometry and molecular geometry with VSEPR theory (up to four groups).
  • Give ideal bond angles and explain the effect of lone pairs, using the "pop a balloon" picture.
  • Read and draw wedge-and-dash structures.

A Lewis structure is flat, but molecules are three-dimensional, and their shapes decide how they behave: why water is a liquid at room temperature, how an enzyme recognizes its target, and how odor and taste receptors tell molecules apart. A simple idea predicts the shapes of most small molecules.

VSEPR: Electron Groups Spread Out

Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory says that the electron groups around a central atom repel each other and spread out as far apart as possible. Think of balloons tied together at their knots: two balloons point in opposite directions, three spread into a flat triangle, and four point to the corners of a tetrahedron.

An electron group is any region of electrons around the central atom:

  • a single bond, a double bond, or a triple bond (each counts as one group), or
  • a lone pair (one group).

The number of groups sets the electron-group geometry: 2 groups, linear (180°); 3 groups, trigonal planar (120°); 4 groups, tetrahedral (109.5°).

Molecular Geometry: Where the Atoms Are

The molecular geometry (the shape) describes only the positions of the atoms. If all the groups are bonds, the shape matches the electron-group geometry. If some groups are lone pairs, they still push the bonds around, but they are invisible in the shape's name, so the shape changes:

The "pop a balloon" trick

Here is a quick way to get from electron-group geometry to molecular shape without memorizing the table. Picture the electron groups as tied balloons, arranged in their electron-group geometry. For every lone pair, pop one balloon, but leave all the other balloons exactly where they were. Then name the shape that the remaining balloons (the bonded atoms) make:

  • Tetrahedral, pop 1: three balloons are left, pointing down like the legs of a tripod: trigonal pyramidal (NH3).
  • Tetrahedral, pop 2: two balloons are left in a V: bent (H2O).
  • Trigonal planar, pop 1: two balloons are left in a flat V: bent (O3, SO2).

The popped balloon is still there in one sense: the lone pair still takes up space and still pushes on the bonds. It just is not an atom, so it is not part of the shape's name.

Lone pairs spread out a little more than bonding pairs, so they squeeze the bonds slightly closer together. A lone pair is like a friend you like but can only handle in small doses: it stays attached to the central atom, but it pushes everyone else a little farther away, and two lone pairs push even more. The H–N–H angle in NH3 is about 107° and the H–O–H angle in water is about 104.5°; in this course, "about 109.5°" is enough.

Drawing shapes in 3D: wedges and dashes

A Lewis structure is flat, so chemists use three kinds of lines to show a three-dimensional shape on paper:

  • A plain line is a bond in the plane of the page (or your screen).
  • A solid wedge, getting wider toward the outer atom, is a bond coming out of the page toward you.
  • A dashed wedge (a row of short dashes) is a bond going behind the page, away from you.

To draw tetrahedral CH4, draw C with two plain lines to H atoms in the plane (one up, one to the side), then a wedge to a third H and a dash to the fourth, both pointing down. To draw water, start from the same tetrahedral arrangement, put the two lone pairs on top of the O "like little ears," and draw the two H atoms angled downward from it, so the bent shape is plain to see. In the Experiment 6a lab, compare your wedge-and-dash drawings with the ball-and-stick models; they show the same shape.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Predict the shape

Problem. Give the electron-group geometry and molecular shape of (a) PCl3; (b) CO2; (c) H2S.

Solution.

  • (a) P has 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 groups: tetrahedral electron geometry; trigonal pyramidal shape.
  • (b) C has 2 double bonds = 2 groups (each double bond counts once): linear, 180°.
  • (c) S has 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs = 4 groups: tetrahedral electron geometry; bent shape, like water.

Check. Always draw the Lewis structure first; the lone pairs on the central atom decide the shape.

Professor Tip

Two numbers do all the work: total groups on the central atom and how many of those are lone pairs. Write them as "4 groups, 1 lone pair" next to every Lewis structure, then read the shape from the table. Do not count lone pairs on the outer atoms; only the central atom's groups matter. If your instructor gives you a VSEPR reference chart, the "steric number" on it is simply the number of electron groups on the central atom; you still need the Lewis structure to know how many of those groups are lone pairs. In a bigger molecule with more than one central atom, do this at each center separately (Section 6.5, "Putting It Together").

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

Water's bent shape is one of the most important facts in biology. If H2O were linear like CO2, its bond polarities would cancel (Section 6.5), it would not form hydrogen bonds well, and it would be a gas at body temperature. Because it is bent, water is polar, sticks to itself, dissolves ions and sugars, and carries them through blood and lymph. Shape matters for larger molecules too: an enzyme's active site fits only molecules of the right shape, and many drugs work by fitting the same pocket as a natural molecule, like a key in a lock.

Common Mistakes

  • Counting a double bond as two groups. Any bond between two atoms is one group.
  • Naming the shape by electron geometry. NH3 has tetrahedral electron geometry but a trigonal pyramidal shape.
  • Calling water linear because its Lewis structure is drawn in a line. The two lone pairs make it bent.
  • Moving the other balloons after you "pop" one. The remaining bonds stay where the electron-group geometry put them; that is why NH3 is a pyramid, not a flat triangle.
  • Mixing up wedges and dashes. A solid wedge comes toward you; dashes go away from you.

Summary

  • Electron groups (bonds of any order, lone pairs) around a central atom spread apart: 2 linear (180°), 3 trigonal planar (120°), 4 tetrahedral (109.5°).
  • Lone pairs change the molecular shape: 3 groups with 1 lone pair is bent; 4 groups with 1 lone pair is trigonal pyramidal; 4 groups with 2 lone pairs is bent. "Pop a balloon" for each lone pair and name what is left.
  • Wedge = toward you; dash = away from you; plain line = in the plane.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the shape and approximate bond angle of (a) CCl4; (b) HCN; (c) NF3.

Answer

(a) tetrahedral, 109.5°. (b) linear, 180° (C has a single and a triple bond: 2 groups). (c) trigonal pyramidal, about 109.5° (3 bonds + 1 lone pair on N).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Both CH4 and H2O have 4 electron groups on the central atom. Why do they have different shapes?

Answer

All 4 groups on C in CH4 are bonds, so the shape is tetrahedral. In H2O, 2 of the 4 groups on O are lone pairs, which are not part of the shape, so only the bent arrangement of the 3 atoms is seen.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What is the shape around each carbon in ethylene, C2H4?

Answer

Each C has 3 groups (two C–H bonds and one C=C double bond) and no lone pairs: trigonal planar, 120°.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Use the "pop a balloon" trick to predict the shape of NCl3, and describe how you would draw it with wedges and dashes.

Answer

N has 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 groups: tetrahedral balloons. Pop one (the lone pair) and the three Cl atoms stay where they were: trigonal pyramidal, about 109.5°. Draw the lone pair on top of N, one N–Cl bond as a plain line in the plane, one as a solid wedge (toward you), and one as a dashed wedge (away from you), all angled downward.
