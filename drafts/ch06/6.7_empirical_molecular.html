Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish an empirical formula from a molecular formula.
  • Find an empirical formula from masses or from percent composition.
  • Find a molecular formula from the empirical formula and the molar mass.
  • Find the number of waters in a hydrate from heating data.

When chemists discover or make a new compound, an instrument called an elemental analyzer can tell them how much of each element it contains, but not how the atoms are arranged or how many are in one molecule. From those masses, they can work out the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. That ratio is the first formula of every new compound.

Empirical and Molecular Formulas

  • A molecular formula gives the actual number of each kind of atom in one molecule: glucose is C6H12O6.
  • An empirical formula gives the smallest whole-number ratio of atoms: glucose's empirical formula is CH2O (divide every subscript by 6).

Different compounds can share an empirical formula: formaldehyde (CH2O), acetic acid (C2H4O2), and glucose (C6H12O6) are all CH2O. For ionic compounds, the formula you write is the empirical formula, because ionic formulas are always reduced (Section 4.2). For many molecules, such as H2O and CO2, the two formulas are the same.

Finding an Empirical Formula

Subscripts are mole ratios (Section 5.4), so the job is to turn masses into moles and then find the simplest ratio of moles.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): From lab masses (magnesium oxide)

Problem. In Section 6.6, 0.486 g of Mg combined with 0.320 g of O. Find the empirical formula.

Solution. Moles: Mg, 0.486 ÷ 24.31 = 0.01999 mol; O, 0.320 ÷ 15.999 = 0.02000 mol. Divide by the smaller (0.01999): Mg 1.000, O 1.000. Empirical formula: MgO.

Check. Mg forms Mg2+ and O forms O2−, so MgO is exactly the formula predicted in Chapter 4.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): From percent composition

Problem. A compound is 69.9% iron and 30.1% oxygen by mass. Find its empirical formula. (Fe 55.85, O 15.999)

Solution. Assume 100 g, so there are 69.9 g Fe and 30.1 g O. Moles: Fe, 69.9 ÷ 55.85 = 1.252 mol; O, 30.1 ÷ 15.999 = 1.881 mol. Divide by the smaller: Fe 1.000, O 1.503. A ratio ending in .5 means multiply both by 2: Fe 2, O 3. Empirical formula: Fe2O3 (iron(III) oxide, rust).

Check. Never round 1.5 to 2; a value close to .5 (or .33, .25) means multiply everything by 2 (or 3, 4).

Professor Tip

Carry at least three or four digits in your mole values and do not round until the division step. After dividing by the smallest, values within about 0.1 of a whole number can be rounded (1.03 → 1, 1.97 → 2). Values near 1.5, 1.33, or 1.25 are not rounding errors; they are telling you to multiply by 2, 3, or 4. Set up a table with columns for grams, moles, and ratio so nothing gets lost.

Finding a Molecular Formula

A molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple, n, of the empirical formula. If you know the molar mass of the compound (from another experiment, such as mass spectrometry), find n:

\( n = \dfrac{\text{molar mass of compound}}{\text{empirical formula mass}} \)

Then multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by n.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Vitamin C

Problem. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is 40.92% C, 4.58% H, and 54.50% O, and its molar mass is 176.12 g/mol. Find its empirical and molecular formulas.

Solution. Per 100 g: C, 40.92 ÷ 12.011 = 3.407 mol; H, 4.58 ÷ 1.008 = 4.544 mol; O, 54.50 ÷ 15.999 = 3.406 mol. Divide by the smallest (3.406): C 1.000, H 1.334, O 1.000. A ratio of 1.33 means multiply by 3: C 3, H 4, O 3. Empirical formula: C3H4O3, mass 3(12.011) + 4(1.008) + 3(15.999) = 88.062 g/mol. Then n = 176.12 ÷ 88.062 = 2.000, so the molecular formula is C6H8O6.

Check. 6(12.011) + 8(1.008) + 6(15.999) = 176.124, matching the given molar mass.

Hydrates

The same mole-ratio idea finds the number of waters in a hydrate (Section 4.3): heat it, weigh the water lost, and compare moles of water to moles of anhydrous salt.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Copper(II) sulfate hydrate

Problem. Heating 2.500 g of blue copper(II) sulfate hydrate, CuSO4·xH2O, leaves 1.598 g of white anhydrous CuSO4 (159.61 g/mol). Find x.

Solution. Water lost: 2.500 − 1.598 = 0.902 g, or 0.902 ÷ 18.015 = 0.05007 mol. CuSO4: 1.598 ÷ 159.61 = 0.01001 mol. Ratio: 0.05007 ÷ 0.01001 = 5.00, so x = 5: CuSO4·5H2O.

Check. This matches the pentahydrate whose molar mass you found in Section 5.2.

In the Lab: Experiment 5a, Empirical Formula

In this experiment you heat a weighed coil of magnesium ribbon in a covered crucible over a Bunsen burner until it has reacted with oxygen from the air, then weigh the white oxide, exactly the calculation in Sections 6.6 and 6.7. Two known errors pull results away from MgO: magnesium that does not fully react (too little oxygen), and magnesium that reacts with nitrogen in the air (about 78% of air) to form some magnesium nitride. Report what you actually measured and explain the difference; honest data with a reasoned error analysis is the goal.

Biology Connection: Biochemistry

The name carbohydrate comes from the empirical formula: many sugars have the formula Cn(H2O)n, which looks like "carbon hydrate," or CH2O for short. Glucose (C6H12O6), fructose (also C6H12O6), and ribose (C5H10O5, part of RNA) all share the empirical formula CH2O. Glucose and fructose even share the same molecular formula, but their atoms are arranged differently, so they are different sugars with different properties. A formula tells you what is there; a structure (Sections 6.3 and 6.4) tells you how it is put together.

Common Mistakes

  • Dividing grams instead of moles. Convert each mass to moles first.
  • Rounding 1.5 to 2 (or 1.33 to 1). Multiply to clear the fraction instead.
  • Using the empirical formula as the molecular formula when a molar mass is given; find n.
  • Forgetting to assume 100 g when the data are percents.

Summary

  • Empirical formula: smallest whole-number ratio. Molecular formula: actual atoms per molecule, n × empirical.
  • Masses (or percents, using 100 g) → moles → divide by smallest → clear fractions → empirical formula.
  • n = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the empirical formula of (a) C2H6; (b) N2O4; (c) C6H6; (d) H2O2.

Answer

(a) CH3. (b) NO2. (c) CH. (d) HO.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A gas is 85.6% C and 14.4% H, and its molar mass is 42.08 g/mol. Find its empirical and molecular formulas.

Answer

C: 85.6 ÷ 12.011 = 7.127 mol; H: 14.4 ÷ 1.008 = 14.29 mol; ratio 1 : 2.00, so CH2 (14.027 g/mol). n = 42.08 ÷ 14.027 = 3.00, so C3H6.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Acetic acid has the empirical formula CH2O and a molar mass of 60.05 g/mol. What is its molecular formula?

Answer

CH2O = 30.026 g/mol; n = 60.05 ÷ 30.026 = 2.000; C2H4O2 (written HC2H3O2 in Section 4.5).
