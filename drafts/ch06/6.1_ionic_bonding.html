Learning Objectives

  • Explain how an ionic bond forms by electron transfer.
  • Show ion formation with electron-dot (Lewis) symbols.
  • Recognize salts, bases (hydroxides), and metal oxides as ionic compounds.
  • Describe typical properties of ionic compounds.

Atoms bond because bonding lowers their energy, usually by giving each atom a filled valence shell, an octet (Section 3.5). There are two main ways to get there. A metal atom with one, two, or three valence electrons can give them to a nonmetal atom that needs one, two, or three more. Or two nonmetal atoms can share electrons. Giving produces ions and ionic bonds; sharing produces molecules and covalent bonds (Section 6.2).

Electron Transfer and the Ionic Bond

When sodium meets chlorine, sodium's single 3s electron moves to chlorine. Sodium becomes Na+, with the electron configuration of neon, and chlorine becomes Cl−, with the configuration of argon. The oppositely charged ions attract each other strongly. This electrostatic attraction between cations and anions is an ionic bond.

Electron-dot symbols (Section 3.5) make the electron bookkeeping visible: draw each atom's valence electrons as dots, move dots from the metal to the nonmetal until every ion has an octet (or no valence electrons left), and write the charges. The number of electrons lost must equal the number gained, which is exactly why the formulas from swap-drop (Section 4.2) come out neutral.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Magnesium and chlorine

Problem. Use electron-dot symbols to show how magnesium and chlorine form an ionic compound, and give its formula.

Solution. Mg has 2 valence electrons; each Cl has 7 and needs 1. One Mg atom gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms:

Mg (2 dots) + 2 Cl (7 dots each) → Mg2+ (no dots) + 2 Cl− (8 dots each) → MgCl2

Check. 2 electrons lost = 2 electrons gained, and (2+) + 2(1−) = 0, the same formula that swap-drop gives.

Three Kinds of Ionic Compounds

  • Salts are ionic compounds made of a cation (other than H+) and an anion (other than OH− or O2−): NaCl, CaCl2, KNO3, (NH4)2SO4.
  • Bases in this course are mostly metal hydroxides, ionic compounds containing OH−: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)2, Mg(OH)2. They release OH− ions in water (Chapter 12).
  • Metal oxides contain the oxide ion, O2−: MgO, CaO (lime), Al2O3, Fe2O3 (rust). Many react with water to form bases: CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2.

Properties of ionic compounds

An ionic solid is not made of separate molecules; it is a huge, repeating three-dimensional array of ions called a crystal lattice, in which each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge (Section 4.1). Because many strong attractions must be broken to melt it, an ionic compound is usually a hard, brittle solid with a high melting point (NaCl melts at 801 °C). Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity, because the ions cannot move, but they do conduct when melted or dissolved in water, because the ions are then free to move. Dissolved ions that conduct electricity are why your body's sodium, potassium, and chloride are called electrolytes.

Professor Tip

Use the periodic table first: metal + nonmetal (or a polyatomic ion) means ionic, so think "transfer"; nonmetal + nonmetal means covalent, so think "share." Then use electron-dot symbols only as a check that electrons lost equals electrons gained. If the ion charges do not cancel, you have the wrong ratio.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth enamel is the hardest substance in the body because it is an ionic crystal: hydroxyapatite, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2, a tightly packed lattice of Ca2+, PO43−, and OH− ions. When fluoride from toothpaste or varnish replaces some of the OH− ions, it forms fluorapatite, which holds together better when plaque acids attack, so enamel resists decay. Pit-and-fissure sealants and composite fillings are the opposite kind of material: covalent polymers, long chains of carbon-based molecules linked by shared electrons (Section 6.2). Hygienists work every day with both: an ionic mineral that must be protected and covalent plastics that bond to it.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking ionic compounds form molecules. They form lattices; the formula is the simplest ratio (a formula unit).
  • Moving electrons the wrong way. Metals lose electrons; nonmetals gain them.
  • Thinking solid salt conducts electricity. Only melted or dissolved ionic compounds conduct.

Summary

  • An ionic bond is the attraction between cations and anions formed by electron transfer from a metal to a nonmetal.
  • Electrons lost = electrons gained, so ionic formulas are neutral.
  • Salts, bases (hydroxides), and metal oxides are ionic. Ionic solids are lattices with high melting points that conduct only when melted or dissolved.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Use electron-dot symbols to show how potassium and oxygen form an ionic compound. What is its formula?

Answer

Each K atom (1 valence electron) gives its electron to O (6 valence electrons, needs 2). Two K atoms are needed: 2 K· + O → 2 K+ + O2−, so K2O.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Classify each as a salt, a base, or a metal oxide: (a) LiOH; (b) Al2O3; (c) CaCO3.

Answer

(a) base (hydroxide). (b) metal oxide. (c) salt.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Why does an IV bag of saline conduct electricity, while a crystal of table salt does not?

Answer

In the solid, Na+ and Cl− are locked in place in the lattice. In solution, the ions are separated and free to move, so they can carry an electric current.
