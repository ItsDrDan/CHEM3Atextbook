Learning Objectives

  • Explain how an ionic bond forms by electron transfer.
  • Show ion formation with electron-dot (Lewis) symbols, with each ion in brackets and its charge outside.
  • Recognize salts, bases (hydroxides), and metal oxides as ionic compounds.
  • Describe typical properties of ionic compounds.

Atoms bond because bonding lowers their energy, usually by giving each atom a filled valence shell, an octet (Section 3.5). In a sense, every atom "wants to be like a noble gas." There are two main ways to get there. A metal atom with one, two, or three valence electrons can give them to a nonmetal atom that needs one, two, or three more. Or two nonmetal atoms can share electrons. Giving produces ions and ionic bonds; sharing produces molecules and covalent bonds (Section 6.2).

Electron Transfer and the Ionic Bond

When sodium meets chlorine, sodium's single 3s electron moves to chlorine. Sodium becomes Na+, with the electron configuration of neon, and chlorine becomes Cl−, with the configuration of argon. The oppositely charged ions attract each other strongly. This electrostatic attraction between cations and anions is an ionic bond.

The ionic compound looks nothing like the elements that formed it. Sodium is a soft, shiny metal so reactive that it is stored under oil, and chlorine is a poisonous yellow-green gas, yet together they form ordinary table salt.

Electron-dot symbols (Section 3.5) make the electron bookkeeping visible: draw each atom's valence electrons as dots, move dots from the metal to the nonmetal until every ion has an octet (or no valence electrons left), and write the charges. The number of electrons lost must equal the number gained, which is exactly why the formulas from swap-drop (Section 4.2) come out neutral.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Magnesium and chlorine

Problem. Use electron-dot symbols to show how magnesium and chlorine form an ionic compound, and give its formula.

Solution. Mg has 2 valence electrons; each Cl has 7 and needs 1. One Mg atom gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms:

Mg (2 dots) + 2 Cl (7 dots each) → Mg2+ (no dots) + 2 Cl− (8 dots each) → MgCl2

Check. 2 electrons lost = 2 electrons gained, and (2+) + 2(1−) = 0, the same formula that swap-drop gives.

Drawing ions with dots and brackets

When you draw the ions themselves, follow one convention: every ion, positive or negative, is drawn in brackets, with its charge outside. A cation that has lost all its valence electrons has no dots left to draw, so its brackets are empty of dots: [Na]+ or [Ca]2+, the same form used in Section 3.5. (Some books leave the cation unbracketed and write simply Na+; ask which form your instructor prefers.) An anion shows its full octet of 8 dots inside the brackets. It helps to draw the electrons the anion gained in a different color, or as x's instead of dots, so you can see at a glance that the electrons lost by the metal equal the electrons gained by the nonmetal. For example, O2− is the 6 dots of an oxygen atom plus 2 new ones, and N3− is the 5 dots of a nitrogen atom plus 3 new ones.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Calcium and fluorine, drawn with brackets

Problem. Use electron-dot symbols to show how calcium and fluorine form an ionic compound. Draw the ions in the conventional way, and give the formula.

Solution. Ca (group 2) has 2 valence electrons; each F (group 17) has 7 and needs 1. Calcium gives one electron to each of two fluorine atoms:

Ca (2 dots) + 2 F (7 dots each) → [Ca]2+ + 2 [F, 8 dots]− → CaF2

Calcium is written as [Ca]2+: brackets with no dots inside, because both valence electrons are gone, and the charge outside. Each fluoride ion is drawn as F surrounded by 8 dots (its own 7 plus the 1 it received), inside brackets, with − outside.

Check. 2 electrons lost = 2 × 1 electron gained, and (2+) + 2(1−) = 0. Ca2+ now has the electron arrangement of argon, and each F− has that of neon. A one-to-one case works the same way: Li gives its single electron to F, giving [Li]+ and [F, 8 dots]−, or LiF.

Three Kinds of Ionic Compounds

  • Salts are ionic compounds made of a cation (other than H+) and an anion (other than OH− or O2−): NaCl, CaCl2, KNO3, (NH4)2SO4.
  • Bases in this course are mostly metal hydroxides, ionic compounds containing OH−: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)2, Mg(OH)2. They release OH− ions in water (Chapter 12).
  • Metal oxides contain the oxide ion, O2−: MgO, CaO (lime), Al2O3, Fe2O3 (rust). Many react with water to form bases: CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2.

Properties of ionic compounds

An ionic solid is not made of separate molecules; it is a huge, repeating three-dimensional array of ions called a crystal lattice, in which each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge (Section 4.1). Because many strong attractions must be broken to melt it, an ionic compound is usually a hard, brittle solid with a high melting point (NaCl melts at 801 °C). Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity, because the ions cannot move, but they do conduct when melted or dissolved in water, because the ions are then free to move. Dissolved ions that conduct electricity are why your body's sodium, potassium, and chloride are called electrolytes.

A third kind of bond, the metallic bond, holds metals such as iron or copper together: each metal atom releases some valence electrons into a shared "sea of electrons" that flows through the whole piece of metal. It occurs only between metal atoms, and you will not need to draw it in this course; the two bonds that matter here are ionic and covalent.

Professor Tip

Use the periodic table first: metal + nonmetal (or a polyatomic ion) means ionic, so think "transfer"; nonmetal + nonmetal means covalent, so think "share." Then use electron-dot symbols only as a check that electrons lost equals electrons gained. If the ion charges do not cancel, you have the wrong ratio.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth enamel is the hardest substance in the body because it is an ionic crystal: hydroxyapatite, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2, a tightly packed lattice of Ca2+, PO43−, and OH− ions. When fluoride from toothpaste or varnish replaces some of the OH− ions, it forms fluorapatite, which holds together better when plaque acids attack, so enamel resists decay. Pit-and-fissure sealants and composite fillings are the opposite kind of material: covalent polymers, long chains of carbon-based molecules linked by shared electrons (Section 6.2). Hygienists work every day with both: an ionic mineral that must be protected and covalent plastics that bond to it.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking ionic compounds form molecules. They form lattices; the formula is the simplest ratio (a formula unit).
  • Moving electrons the wrong way. Metals lose electrons; nonmetals gain them.
  • Thinking solid salt conducts electricity. Only melted or dissolved ionic compounds conduct.
  • Drawing the anion without its octet or its charge. F− has 8 dots, in brackets, with − outside; the cation has no valence dots left, so its brackets are empty: [Ca]2+.
  • Giving all of a metal's electrons to one nonmetal atom. Calcium gives one electron to each of two F atoms, not two electrons to one F.

Summary

  • An ionic bond is the attraction between cations and anions formed by electron transfer from a metal to a nonmetal.
  • Electrons lost = electrons gained, so ionic formulas are neutral. In dot drawings, each ion is bracketed with its charge outside; the cation’s brackets are empty of dots.
  • Salts, bases (hydroxides), and metal oxides are ionic. Ionic solids are lattices with high melting points that conduct only when melted or dissolved.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Use electron-dot symbols to show how potassium and oxygen form an ionic compound. What is its formula?

Hint

Start with valence electrons: K is in group 1, O in group 16. How many electrons does O need, and how many K atoms does it take to supply them?

Answer

Each K atom (1 valence electron) gives its electron to O (6 valence electrons, needs 2). Two K atoms are needed: 2 K· + O → 2 K+ + O2−, so K2O.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Classify each as a salt, a base, or a metal oxide: (a) LiOH; (b) Al2O3; (c) CaCO3.

Hint

Look for the ion that decides: does the formula contain OH− (base), the oxide ion O2− with a metal (metal oxide), or neither (salt)?

Answer

(a) base (hydroxide). (b) metal oxide. (c) salt.

LiOH contains the hydroxide ion, Al2O3 is a metal with the oxide ion, and CaCO3 is a metal cation with a polyatomic anion that is neither OH− nor O2−.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Why does an IV bag of saline conduct electricity, while a crystal of table salt does not?

Hint

To carry a current, charged particles must be able to move. Ask where the ions are free to move: in the crystal lattice or in water?

Answer

In the solid, Na+ and Cl− are locked in place in the lattice. In solution, the ions are separated and free to move, so they can carry an electric current.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Describe the electron-dot picture for aluminum and fluorine forming an ionic compound. How many electrons move, how is each ion drawn, and what is the formula?

Hint

Al is in group 13 and F in group 17. Each F needs only one electron, so do not give all of Al's electrons to one F. Electrons lost must equal electrons gained.

Answer

Al has 3 valence electrons; each F has 7 and needs 1. Al gives one electron to each of three F atoms, so 3 electrons move: [Al]3+ (empty brackets, no dots) and three [F, 8 dots]− (7 of its own plus 1 gained, in brackets). 3 lost = 3 × 1 gained, so the formula is AlF3. (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 4.0 − 1.5 = 2.5, above 2.0, so the bond is ionic.)
