Learning Objectives

  • Describe ionic bonding and recognize salts, bases, and metal oxides.
  • Describe covalent bonds; draw Lewis structures for molecules with one central atom, simple organic molecules, and polyatomic ions.
  • Predict shapes with VSEPR for up to four electron groups.
  • Classify bonds with electronegativity, decide whether a molecule is polar, and find the shape at each center and the polar regions of a larger molecule.
  • Calculate percent composition, use it to check identity and as a conversion factor, and find empirical and molecular formulas.

Key Ideas

1. Ionic bonds: transfer

Atoms "want to be like a noble gas": they give, take, or share electrons. Metals give electrons to nonmetals; the ions attract. Electrons lost = electrons gained. In dot drawings each ion is bracketed with its charge outside (CaF2: [Ca]2+ with no dots and two [F]−, each F with 8 dots). Salts, bases (hydroxides), and metal oxides are ionic: crystal lattices, high melting points, conduct only when melted or dissolved. (Section 6.1)

2. Covalent bonds: sharing

A shared pair is a bond (a line, 2 electrons); unshared pairs are lone pairs. Single, double, and triple bonds share 1, 2, or 3 pairs; more pairs = shorter, stronger. HONC 1-2-3-4. (Section 6.2)

3. Lewis structures in four steps

Skeleton (the atom that makes the most bonds is central, the "center of attention"; then least electronegative; H never central; a central atom bonds to 2 or more atoms) → for molecules like CHOCl, label usual bond counts and let the atom still short of bonds form the double bond → count valence electrons (± charge) → single bonds, then lone pairs → make multiple bonds if the center is short of an octet. Ions in brackets. (Section 6.3)

4. Shape from groups and lone pairs

Count groups on the central atom (any bond = 1; lone pair = 1). 2 linear 180°; 3 trigonal planar 120° (1 LP: bent); 4 tetrahedral 109.5° (1 LP: trigonal pyramidal; 2 LP: bent). "Pop a balloon" for each lone pair and name what is left. Wedge = toward you, dash = away. (Section 6.4)

5. Polarity: bonds plus shape

\( \Delta \text{EN} \) 0–0.4 nonpolar; above 0.4 to 2.0 polar covalent; above 2.0 ionic. A molecule is polar if its dipoles do not cancel: SNAP, Symmetrical Nonpolar, Asymmetrical Polar. CO2 and CCl4 are nonpolar; H2O and NH3 are polar. Follow the bond arrows like driving directions: back to the start = nonpolar. One atom can flip it (CCl4 nonpolar, CHCl3 polar). Polar mixes with polar. In a larger molecule, find the shape at each center and the polar and nonpolar regions (ethanol). (Section 6.5)

6. Formulas from masses

Mass percent = part ÷ whole × 100%. Empirical formula: grams (or percents with 100 g) → moles → divide by smallest → clear .5 or .33. Molecular formula = n × empirical, n = molar mass ÷ empirical mass (a whole number; n can be 1). A measured percent that does not match the formula means the sample is not that compound; a percent is also a conversion factor (69.94 g Fe per 100 g Fe2O3). (Sections 6.6 and 6.7)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): The full chain for H2O and CO2

Problem. For water and carbon dioxide, give the Lewis structure, the shape, and whether each is polar.

Solution. H2O: 8 electrons; H–O–H with 2 lone pairs on O; 4 groups, 2 lone pairs: bent; O–H bonds polar (\( \Delta \text{EN} \) = 1.4) and the shape is asymmetrical: polar. CO2: 16 electrons; O=C=O with 2 lone pairs on each O; 2 groups on C: linear; C=O bonds polar (1.0) but they point opposite ways: nonpolar.

Check. Same kind of polar bonds, different shapes, opposite results: exactly why you must find the shape first.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Ammonia, step by step

Problem. For NH3, give the number of electron groups and lone pairs on N, the electron geometry, the shape, the approximate bond angle, and the polarity.

Solution. 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 groups, 1 lone pair; electron geometry tetrahedral; shape trigonal pyramidal; about 109.5° (actually 107°); N–H bonds are polar (0.9) and the pyramid is asymmetrical: polar.

Check. Ammonia's polarity is why it dissolves so readily in water (Chapter 9).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): From percents to a molecular formula

Problem. A compound used in antifreeze is 38.70% C, 9.74% H, and 51.56% O, with a molar mass of 62.07 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

Solution. Per 100 g: C 38.70 ÷ 12.011 = 3.222 mol; H 9.74 ÷ 1.008 = 9.663 mol; O 51.56 ÷ 15.999 = 3.223 mol. Divide by 3.222: C 1.000, H 2.999, O 1.000: empirical formula CH3O (31.034 g/mol). n = 62.07 ÷ 31.034 = 2.000: molecular formula C2H6O2 (ethylene glycol).

Check. 2(12.011) + 6(1.008) + 2(15.999) = 62.068 g/mol. Ethylene glycol is toxic if swallowed, a reason antifreeze must be stored away from children and pets.

Common Mistakes

  • H is never central; count each double or triple bond as one electron group.
  • Add electrons for negative ions and subtract for positive ions.
  • Name the shape from atom positions only (NH3 is trigonal pyramidal, not tetrahedral).
  • Polar bonds do not guarantee a polar molecule; check symmetry, and check that every outer atom is the same (CHCl3 is polar).
  • Choose the central atom by bond count (C before N before O); in a big molecule, give the shape at the center you are asked about.
  • n = molar mass ÷ empirical mass (not the reverse); n can be 1.
  • Convert grams to moles before finding ratios; never round 1.5 to 2.
