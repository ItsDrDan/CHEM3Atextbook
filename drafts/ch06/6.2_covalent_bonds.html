Learning Objectives

  • Describe a covalent bond as a shared pair of electrons.
  • Distinguish bonding pairs from lone pairs, and single, double, and triple bonds.
  • Predict the usual number of bonds formed by H, C, N, O, and the halogens.
  • Relate bond order to bond length and bond strength.

Two nonmetal atoms both need electrons, so neither can simply give electrons away. Instead they share them. A pair of electrons shared between two atoms is a covalent bond, and a group of atoms held together by covalent bonds is a molecule.

Shared Pairs and Lone Pairs

In a Lewis structure, each shared pair is drawn as a line (one line = two electrons), and electrons that belong to one atom only are drawn as pairs of dots called lone pairs (or nonbonding pairs). Each atom counts all the electrons in its bonds plus its lone pairs. A good Lewis structure gives every atom an octet, except hydrogen, which needs only two electrons, a duet (like helium).

Water is the simplest example. Oxygen has 6 valence electrons and needs 2 more; each hydrogen has 1 and needs 1 more. Oxygen shares one pair with each H: H–O–H. Oxygen now has 2 bonds (4 electrons) plus 2 lone pairs (4 electrons) = 8, and each H has one bond (2 electrons).

Single, Double, and Triple Bonds

Sometimes one shared pair is not enough to complete the octets. In O2, each O has 6 valence electrons; sharing two pairs, a double bond (O=O), gives each O an octet. In N2, each N has 5 and needs 3, so the atoms share three pairs, a triple bond (N≡N).

The more pairs two atoms share, the shorter and stronger the bond. A triple bond is shorter and stronger than a double bond, which is shorter and stronger than a single bond. The N≡N triple bond is so strong that the N2 making up 78% of the air is very unreactive; plants cannot use it directly and depend on nitrogen-fixing bacteria or fertilizer (Section 5.4).

How Many Bonds Does an Atom Form?

In neutral molecules, an atom usually forms as many bonds as the number of electrons it needs to reach an octet (or duet). That number matches the size of its predictable ion charge from Section 3.5:

A quick memory aid is HONC 1-2-3-4: hydrogen 1 bond, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4. A double bond counts as two bonds and a triple bond as three, so carbon in CO2 (two double bonds) still has four bonds.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Count bonds and lone pairs

Problem. In HCN (H–C≡N with one lone pair on N), how many bonds does each atom have, and does each atom have a full valence shell?

Solution. H: 1 bond = 2 electrons (duet). C: 1 single + 1 triple = 4 bonds = 8 electrons. N: 1 triple bond (6 electrons) + 1 lone pair (2) = 8 electrons. Every atom is satisfied, and each matches HONC.

Check. Total electrons drawn: 4 bonds × 2 + 1 lone pair × 2 = 10, equal to the valence electrons 1 + 4 + 5 = 10.

Professor Tip

When a structure "looks wrong," check the bond count against HONC first. A carbon with three bonds or a hydrogen with two is almost always a sign that a double bond belongs somewhere else or that the skeleton is wrong. Most of the Lewis structures in this course can be checked in five seconds this way.

Common Mistakes

  • Counting a bond line as one electron. Each line is a pair: 2 electrons.
  • Giving hydrogen an octet. H forms one bond and has no lone pairs.
  • Forgetting that a double bond counts as two bonds toward HONC.

Summary

  • A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons; lone pairs belong to one atom.
  • Atoms share 1, 2, or 3 pairs (single, double, triple bonds) to reach an octet (H: duet). More shared pairs = shorter, stronger bond.
  • Usual bonds: H 1, O 2, N 3, C 4; halogens 1.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many bonds and lone pairs does each atom usually have: (a) Cl; (b) S; (c) P?

Hint

Use the group number: an atom usually forms as many bonds as the electrons it needs to reach an octet, and its leftover valence electrons pair up as lone pairs.

Answer

(a) 1 bond, 3 lone pairs. (b) 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs. (c) 3 bonds, 1 lone pair.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which is the strongest bond: C–O, C=O, or C≡O?

Hint

Think about how many pairs are shared. The more shared pairs between two atoms, the shorter and stronger the bond.

Answer

C≡O, the triple bond (shortest and strongest), as in carbon monoxide.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Why must the two oxygen atoms in O2 share two pairs rather than one?

Hint

Count O2's valence electrons, then try a single bond. Does each O end up with 8 electrons using only that many?

Answer

O2 has 12 valence electrons. With a single bond (2 electrons) and the other 10 as lone pairs, one O can have an octet but the other has only 6 electrons. Sharing a second pair (a double bond, 4 electrons, plus 2 lone pairs on each O) gives both atoms 8 electrons and uses exactly 12.
