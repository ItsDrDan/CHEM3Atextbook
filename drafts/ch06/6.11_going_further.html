Resonance

Ozone, the Valley pollutant from this chapter's opener, has 18 valence electrons (3 × 6). Following Section 6.3, the central O ends up with a double bond to one outer O and a single bond to the other: O=O–O. But you could just as well have put the double bond on the other side: O–O=O. Which is right? Experiments say neither: both O–O bonds in ozone are the same length, in between a single and a double bond.

When two or more valid Lewis structures differ only in where the electrons (not the atoms) are placed, they are called resonance structures, drawn with a double-headed arrow between them:

O=O–O  ↔  O–O=O

The real molecule is a single resonance hybrid, a blend of the structures, much as green paint is a blend of blue and yellow, not a mixture that switches between them. Other common examples are sulfur dioxide (SO2, two structures), the nitrate ion (NO3−, three), and the carbonate ion (CO32−, three). In carbonate, all three C–O bonds are identical, each about one and one-third bonds.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): The carbonate ion

Problem. How many resonance structures does CO32− have, and what do they look like?

Solution. 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24 electrons. With C central and three single bonds (6 electrons) plus lone pairs completing each O (18 electrons), C has only 6. One O must share a lone pair to form C=O. Any of the three O atoms can be the double-bonded one, so there are three resonance structures, each with one C=O and two C–O bonds, in brackets with a 2− charge.

Check. The shape (trigonal planar, 3 groups on C) is the same in every resonance structure; only electrons move.

Exceptions to the Octet Rule

  • Odd-electron molecules (radicals). A molecule with an odd number of valence electrons cannot give every atom an octet. Nitric oxide, NO (5 + 6 = 11 electrons), and nitrogen dioxide, NO2 (17 electrons), each have one unpaired electron. Radicals are usually very reactive, one reason NO2 is an air pollutant and a building block of ozone smog.
  • Incomplete octets. Boron and beryllium compounds are often stable with fewer than 8 electrons around the central atom: BF3 has only 6 electrons around B.
  • Expanded octets. Elements in period 3 and below (P, S, Cl, and heavier) can have more than 8 electrons around them: PCl5 has 10 around P, and SF6 has 12 around S. These molecules also have shapes with five and six electron groups, which are covered in CHEM 1A.

Professor Tip

For CHEM 3A, you will not be asked to draw resonance structures or octet exceptions on an exam unless your instructor assigns this page. The skills on Sections 6.3 to 6.5 are the ones to master: they cover every molecule with one central atom and up to four electron groups.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many resonance structures does SO2 have? What is its shape?

Answer

Two (O=S–O and O–S=O, with one lone pair on S); 3 electron groups with 1 lone pair: bent, about 120°.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Classify each as a radical, an incomplete octet, or an expanded octet: (a) BH3; (b) SF6; (c) NO.

Answer

(a) incomplete octet (6 electrons around B). (b) expanded octet (12 around S). (c) radical (11 valence electrons).
