Learning Objectives

  • Decide whether a formula or name belongs to an ionic compound, a molecular compound, or an acid.
  • Apply the right set of naming rules to any compound in this course, following the questions on the blue naming sheet.
  • Tell apart a molecule, a polyatomic ion, an ionic compound, and an acid that contain the same group of atoms.

You now have three sets of rules. The hard part on an exam is not any single rule; it is choosing the right one quickly. This section puts everything in one flowchart, walks through the course's blue naming sheet question by question, and then works through the cases that cause the most confusion.

The Decision Flowchart

Ionic compounds come in two kinds. A binary ionic compound has just two elements (NaCl, Fe2O3); a ternary ionic compound has three or more elements because it contains a polyatomic ion (Na2SO4, NH4Cl). Both follow the same ionic rules. When you start from a name, the clues are in the words: a metal name or "ammonium" first means ionic; Greek prefixes mean molecular; the word "acid" means an acid; a Roman numeral means a variable-charge metal.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): A mixed set

Problem. Name each compound: (a) Ca(ClO)2; (b) Cl2O; (c) HClO(aq); (d) CuCl2; (e) NH4HCO3; (f) N2H4.

Solution.

  • (a) Metal first, fixed charge: ionic. Ca2+ and hypochlorite: calcium hypochlorite (used to chlorinate pools).
  • (b) Two nonmetals: molecular. dichlorine monoxide.
  • (c) Starts with H, aqueous, anion hypochlorite: hypochlorous acid.
  • (d) Variable-charge metal: 2 Cl− = 2−, so Cu2+: copper(II) chloride.
  • (e) Starts with ammonium: ionic. ammonium bicarbonate (ammonium hydrogen carbonate).
  • (f) Two nonmetals: molecular. dinitrogen tetrahydride (its common name is hydrazine).

Check. Prefixes appear only in (b) and (f), the two molecular compounds; the Roman numeral appears only on copper, the only variable-charge metal.

Following the Blue Naming Sheet

CHEM 3A students get a one-page naming flowchart, the blue naming sheet, and it is worth keeping next to you while you practice, just like the green sheet of polyatomic ions. Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) follows the same order as the sheet: it starts with "Is there a metal?" Here is its logic, step by step:

  1. Is there a metal in the formula?
    • Yes: it is ionic. Next ask, is the metal a transition metal?
      • No (groups 1 and 2, aluminum): count the elements. Two elements = ionic binary: name the metal, then the nonmetal with its ending changed to -ide (CaCl2, calcium chloride). Three or more elements = ionic ternary: name the metal, then the polyatomic ion, and do not change the ion's ending (Na2SO4, sodium sulfate).
      • Yes: use the same two branches, but first work out the metal's charge from the anion and write it as a Roman numeral (FeCl3, iron(III) chloride; Cu(NO3)2, copper(II) nitrate).
    • No metal: next ask, is the first element hydrogen?
      • Yes: it is an acid. H + one element: hydro- + root + -ic acid (HBr, hydrobromic acid). H + a polyatomic ion: an -ate ion becomes -ic acid (HNO3, nitric acid) and an -ite ion becomes -ous acid (HNO2, nitrous acid). The root is the first part of the element or polyatomic ion name. (H2 by itself is just the element hydrogen, not an acid, and H2O is water.)
      • No: count the elements. One element = a diatomic element (N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2, and H2, hydrogen): just name the element (Br2 is bromine). Two elements = covalent (molecular) binary: prefixes on the first element except mono-, prefixes on the second element including mono-, and change the second element's ending to -ide (CO, carbon monoxide; N2O4, dinitrogen tetroxide).

Two notes keep the sheet's shortcuts accurate:

  • Zinc and silver are transition metals, but their charges are fixed (Zn2+ and Ag+, "the steps" with Al3+, Section 4.2), so they get no Roman numeral: ZnCl2 is zinc chloride. On the other side, tin and lead are not transition metals, but they have more than one charge, so they do get a Roman numeral (SnCl4, tin(IV) chloride). The real question behind "transition metal?" is "can this metal have more than one charge?"
  • Ammonium compounds have no metal, but NH4+ is a cation, so they are ionic: treat NH4+ like a fixed-charge metal (NH4Cl, ammonium chloride; (NH4)2SO4, ammonium sulfate). Also, an H-first formula is named as an acid when it is dissolved in water (aq); pure HCl gas is hydrogen chloride (Section 4.5).

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Following the blue naming sheet

Problem. Use the blue-sheet questions to name (a) K2CO3; (b) CoCl2; (c) HI(aq); (d) I2; (e) P2O5; (f) Ag2S.

Solution.

  • (a) Metal? Yes (K). Transition? No. Three elements: ternary. Metal + polyatomic ion, ending unchanged: potassium carbonate.
  • (b) Metal? Yes (Co). Transition? Yes. Two elements: binary. 2 Cl− = 2−, so Co2+: cobalt(II) chloride.
  • (c) Metal? No. H first? Yes: acid. H + one element: hydro- + iod + -ic: hydroiodic acid.
  • (d) Metal? No. H first? No. One element: a diatomic element, iodine.
  • (e) Metal? No. H first? No. Two elements: covalent binary. 2 P = di-, 5 O = penta- (the a drops before oxide), and the second element ends in -ide: diphosphorus pentoxide.
  • (f) Metal? Yes (Ag), a transition metal, but its charge is fixed at 1+: binary, no Roman numeral: silver sulfide.

Check. Roman numeral only in (b), the one variable-charge metal; prefixes only in (e), the one covalent binary; the ion ending in (a) stayed "-ate."

Same Atoms, Different Species

The same group of atoms can show up as a molecule, an ion, part of an ionic compound, or an acid. The charge and the rest of the formula tell you which, and each gets a different kind of name:

  • ClO2: a neutral molecule of two nonmetals, chlorine dioxide (a water disinfectant).
  • ClO2−: a polyatomic ion, chlorite.
  • NaClO2: an ionic compound, sodium chlorite.
  • HClO2: an acid, chlorous acid.

Likewise, H2SO4 is sulfuric acid, HSO4− is the hydrogen sulfate (bisulfate) ion, and NaHSO4 is sodium hydrogen sulfate (sodium bisulfate), an ingredient in some toilet-bowl cleaners. Hydroxides such as NaOH contain H but are ionic because they start with a metal; they are named like any other ionic compound (sodium hydroxide).

Professor Tip

Say the type out loud before you name: "metal first, ionic"; "two nonmetals, prefixes"; "H in water, acid." It takes one second and prevents the most common errors, such as "dicopper oxide" or "sulfur oxide." For practice, cover the answers in this chapter's examples and name the compounds in a random order, because exams will not group them by type.

Professor Tip: Why Speed Matters

Naming is a language, and like any language it takes time and daily practice to become fluent. It pays off for the rest of the course: questions about reactions, moles, and solutions are often written with names, not formulas ("aqueous lead(II) nitrate is mixed with potassium iodide…"), so you must turn names into formulas before you can even start. Outside class you will read names on reagent bottles, lab labels, and medication orders. A few minutes a day with flashcards or the Chapter 4 games builds that speed far better than one long cram session.

Biology Connection: Microbiology

Labs and clinics disinfect with several compounds whose names sound alike. Sodium hypochlorite, NaClO, is household bleach, an ionic compound; its active form in water is hypochlorous acid, HClO, which kills bacteria and viruses by damaging their proteins. Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, is a molecular compound used to disinfect surfaces and, at low concentration, minor wounds. Chlorine dioxide, ClO2, treats drinking water. These are different compounds with different strengths, contact times, and hazards, and mixing bleach with acids or with ammonia releases toxic gases. Reading the full name on the label is the first safety step in any microbiology lab.

Common Mistakes

  • Naming before classifying. Always decide ionic, molecular, or acid first.
  • Putting a Roman numeral on zinc or silver. They are transition metals, but their charges are fixed: ZnO is zinc oxide, not "zinc(II) oxide."
  • Changing a polyatomic ion's ending in a ternary compound. Na2SO4 is sodium sulfate, not "sodium sulfide."
  • Naming a diatomic element as a compound. Cl2 is chlorine, not "dichlorine" or "chlorine chloride."
  • Treating ammonium compounds as molecular. (NH4)2S is ammonium sulfide, not "diammonium sulfide."
  • Treating hydroxides as acids. KOH is potassium hydroxide, a base (Section 4.5).
  • Confusing a molecule with an ion. ClO2 (chlorine dioxide) is not ClO2− (chlorite).

Summary

  • Classify first: H in water = acid; metal or NH4+ first = ionic (binary, or ternary with a polyatomic ion; metal hydroxides are bases); nonmetals only = molecular.
  • The blue naming sheet asks: metal? → transition (variable-charge) metal? → count elements (2 = binary, -ide; 3+ = ternary, keep the ion's ending). No metal → H first? → acid; otherwise count elements (1 = diatomic element; 2 = covalent binary with prefixes). Zinc and silver get no Roman numeral; ammonium compounds are ionic.
  • Ionic: cation + anion, Roman numerals only for variable-charge metals, no prefixes. Molecular: Greek prefixes. Acids: -ide → hydro-...-ic, -ate → -ic, -ite → -ous.
  • The same atoms can form a molecule, an ion, an ionic compound, or an acid, each with its own name.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Name (a) SO2; (b) K2SO3; (c) H2SO3; (d) Fe2(SO4)3.

Answer

(a) sulfur dioxide (molecular). (b) potassium sulfite (ionic). (c) sulfurous acid (sulfite → -ous). (d) iron(III) sulfate: 3 sulfates = 6− over 2 iron ions.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write formulas for (a) nitrogen trifluoride; (b) magnesium nitride; (c) nitric acid; (d) ammonium nitrite.

Answer

(a) NF3. (b) Mg3N2. (c) HNO3. (d) NH4NO2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Explain why CaCl2 is not named "calcium dichloride" but CCl4 is named "carbon tetrachloride."

Answer

CaCl2 is ionic (calcium is a metal); the fixed charges already fix the ratio, so no prefixes are used. CCl4 is molecular (two nonmetals); there are no charges to fix the ratio, so prefixes give the atom counts.
