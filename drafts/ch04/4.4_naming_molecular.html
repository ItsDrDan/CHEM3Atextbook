Learning Objectives

  • Use Greek prefixes to name binary molecular compounds.
  • Write the formula of a binary molecular compound from its name.
  • Recognize common molecular compounds known by their common names.

Two nonmetals can combine in more than one ratio. Carbon and oxygen form both CO, a deadly gas, and CO2, the gas you exhale. Nitrogen and oxygen form at least six different compounds. Charges cannot help you here, because molecular compounds are not made of ions, so the name must count the atoms directly. That is the job of the Greek prefixes.

Naming Rules

  1. Name the first element in the formula, with a prefix for its number of atoms. Do not use mono- on the first element: CO2 is carbon dioxide, not monocarbon dioxide.
  2. Name the second element with a prefix and the -ide ending, as for anions: keep the root of the name (often the first syllable): fluor-, ox-, chlor-, brom-, iod-, sulf-, nitr-, phosph-, and add -ide (fluorine → fluoride, oxygen → oxide). Mono- is used on the second element: CO is carbon monoxide.
  3. When a prefix ending in a or o comes before "oxide," the vowel is usually dropped: pentoxide (not pentaoxide), monoxide (not monooxide). You may also see tetraoxide written out in full.

The element written first is usually the one farther to the left (or lower) on the periodic table, so the formula starts with the less nonmetallic element: CO2, SF6, N2O5.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Formula to name

Problem. Name (a) N2O5; (b) CCl4; (c) B2Cl4; (d) SCl2; (e) PCl5.

Solution. (a) dinitrogen pentoxide. (b) carbon tetrachloride. (c) diboron tetrachloride. (d) sulfur dichloride. (e) phosphorus pentachloride.

Check. Every name has two words, the first element never starts with mono-, and the second always ends in -ide.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Name to formula

Problem. Write formulas for (a) dinitrogen monoxide; (b) sulfur trioxide; (c) tetraphosphorus decoxide; (d) iodine heptafluoride.

Solution. (a) N2O. (b) SO3. (c) P4O10. (d) IF7.

Check. Molecular formulas are never "reduced": P4O10 is the real molecule, so do not rewrite it as P2O5, and do not swap-drop charges, because there are none.

Professor Tip

The most common naming mistake in this whole chapter is mixing up the two systems. Before you name, classify: a metal (or NH4) in front means ionic, no prefixes; nonmetals only means molecular, use prefixes. AlCl3 is aluminum chloride; PCl3 is phosphorus trichloride. Same chloride count, different rules, because Al is a metal and P is not.

Common names

A few molecular compounds are almost always called by common names. Know these: H2O, water; NH3, ammonia; CH4, methane; H2O2, hydrogen peroxide.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Many dental offices offer nitrous oxide, N2O, often called "laughing gas," to help anxious patients relax. Its systematic name, dinitrogen monoxide, tells you exactly what is in the tank: two nitrogen atoms and one oxygen atom per molecule. It is delivered mixed with oxygen through a nasal mask, and hygienists need extra training and certification before they may give it. Because the gas can build up in the room air, offices use scavenging systems that pull exhaled gas away from the staff. Do not confuse it with nitric oxide, NO (nitrogen monoxide), or nitrogen dioxide, NO2, a brown air pollutant: one letter in a name or one subscript in a formula makes a completely different compound.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

Respiratory therapists in neonatal intensive care units deliver inhaled nitric oxide, NO (nitrogen monoxide), to some newborns with severe lung blood-pressure problems; the gas relaxes blood vessels in the lungs. It is a different compound from nitrous oxide (N2O) and from nitrogen dioxide (NO2), which is toxic and can form when NO meets oxygen, so delivery systems monitor NO2 levels. Carbon monoxide, CO, is another molecule every RT knows: it binds hemoglobin far more tightly than O2 does, and a standard pulse oximeter cannot tell the difference (Chapter 3).

Common Mistakes

  • Using prefixes for ionic compounds (or leaving them off molecular ones).
  • Putting mono- on the first element. CO is carbon monoxide.
  • Reducing a molecular formula. N2O4 and NO2 are different molecules.
  • Forgetting the -ide ending on the second element: sulfur trioxide, not sulfur trioxygen.

Summary

  • Binary molecular compounds (two nonmetals) are named with Greek prefixes: prefix + first element, prefix + second element with -ide.
  • Mono- is never used on the first element; a or o is usually dropped before "oxide."
  • Molecular formulas give the actual atoms in one molecule and are not reduced.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Name (a) SiO2; (b) N2O4; (c) SF6; (d) Cl2O7.

Hint

Both elements are nonmetals, so use Greek prefixes for each atom count. Skip mono- on the first element, and end the second element in -ide.

Answer

(a) silicon dioxide. (b) dinitrogen tetroxide (or tetraoxide). (c) sulfur hexafluoride. (d) dichlorine heptoxide.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write formulas for (a) carbon disulfide; (b) nitrogen triiodide; (c) tetraphosphorus hexoxide.

Hint

The prefixes give the subscripts directly (di- = 2, tri- = 3, tetra- = 4, hexa- = 6), and no prefix on the first element means one atom. No charges, no swapping.

Answer

(a) CS2. (b) NI3. (c) P4O6.

In a molecular compound each prefix is the atom count: carbon has no prefix (1 C) and disulfide means 2 S, triiodide means 3 I, and tetraphosphorus hexoxide means 4 P and 6 O.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A student names MgCl2 "magnesium dichloride" and PF3 "phosphorus fluoride." Correct both names.

Hint

Classify each first: does the formula start with a metal (ionic, no prefixes) or contain only nonmetals (molecular, prefixes required)?

Answer

MgCl2 is ionic (Mg is a metal): magnesium chloride, no prefix. PF3 is molecular: phosphorus trifluoride, prefix required.
