Learning Objectives

  • Name binary ionic compounds of metals that form only one ion.
  • Name compounds of metals that can form more than one ion, using Roman numerals (and recognize the older -ous/-ic names).
  • Name ionic compounds that contain polyatomic ions, and write formulas from names, reading a Roman numeral as a charge, not a subscript.
  • Name and write formulas for simple hydrates.

Ionic compound names have two parts: the cation name followed by the anion name. "Sodium chloride," "calcium carbonate," "iron(III) oxide." The only real decision is whether the cation's name needs to tell you its charge.

Naming the Cation

Metals with a fixed charge

Some metals form only one ion, so the name of the metal is enough. In this course, these fixed-charge metals are:

  • Group 1 (alkali metals): always 1+ (Li+, Na+, K+, ...).
  • Group 2 (alkaline earth metals): always 2+ (Mg2+, Ca2+, Ba2+, ...).
  • Aluminum, Al3+; zinc, Zn2+; and silver, Ag+: "the steps" of Section 4.2, three, two, one.

The ammonium ion, NH4+, is always 1+ and is simply called "ammonium." (These are sometimes called Type I compounds.)

Metals with a variable charge

Most other metals, especially the transition metals, can form more than one ion. Iron, for example, forms both Fe2+ and Fe3+. For these variable-charge metals, the name must say which ion is present. The modern (Stock) system puts the charge in Roman numerals in parentheses right after the metal's name, with no space: iron(II), iron(III). An older system, still seen on labels and in older references, uses the Latin root with -ous for the lower charge and -ic for the higher charge. (These are sometimes called Type II compounds.) The table below lists the most common variable-charge metals. Fourteen rows look like a lot, but they come in just three charge pairs, which is much easier to remember:

  • I and II (1+ or 2+): copper and mercury.
  • II and III (2+ or 3+): chromium, iron, and cobalt.
  • II and IV (2+ or 4+): tin and lead.

Manganese (Mn2+ and other charges) also needs a numeral; find its charge from the anions.

In case your Roman numerals are rusty: I = 1, II = 2, III = 3, IV = 4, V = 5, VI = 6, VII = 7, VIII = 8. In this course the charge on a metal ion rarely goes above 4, and almost never above 6.

Naming the Anion

  • A monatomic anion takes the root of the element's name plus -ide: fluoride, chloride, bromide, iodide, oxide, sulfide, nitride, phosphide. To find it, keep the root of the name (often the first syllable): fluor-, ox-, chlor-, brom-, iod-, sulf-, nitr-, phosph-. Then add -ide: fluorine becomes fluoride, oxygen becomes oxide, sulfur becomes sulfide, and phosphorus becomes phosphide.
  • A polyatomic anion keeps its own name unchanged: sulfate, nitrite, hydroxide, acetate.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Fixed-charge metals

Problem. Name (a) KCl; (b) CaS; (c) AlN; (d) Mg(OH)2; (e) Ag2SO4.

Solution. (a) potassium chloride. (b) calcium sulfide. (c) aluminum nitride. (d) magnesium hydroxide (the active ingredient in milk of magnesia). (e) silver sulfate.

Check. None of these metals needs a Roman numeral: K is group 1, Ca and Mg are group 2, and Al and Ag are on the fixed-charge list. Ionic names never use prefixes such as "di-": Mg(OH)2 is not "magnesium dihydroxide."

Naming Compounds of Variable-Charge Metals

Think of the formula as a balancing scale. The whole compound has zero charge, so the positive side must exactly balance the negative side. The anions are the side you know; the metal must supply whatever positive charge it takes to level the scale. Compare two copper chlorides:

  • CuCl2: two Cl− put 2− on the scale, so the one copper must be 2+. This is copper(II) chloride.
  • CuCl: one Cl− puts 1− on the scale, so the copper must be 1+. This is copper(I) chloride.

Same two elements, two different compounds, and the name "copper chloride" alone could not tell them apart. That is exactly why the Roman numeral is needed. To choose it, work out the metal's charge from the anions, as in Section 4.2:

  1. Find the total negative charge from the anions.
  2. Divide by the number of metal ions to get the charge on each one.
  3. Write that charge as a Roman numeral in parentheses.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Roman numerals

Problem. Name (a) FeCl3; (b) CuO; (c) SnF2; (d) Co2(SO4)3; (e) PbO2.

Solution.

  • (a) 3 Cl− = 3−, so Fe3+: iron(III) chloride (ferric chloride).
  • (b) O2− = 2−, so Cu2+: copper(II) oxide (cupric oxide).
  • (c) 2 F− = 2−, so Sn2+: tin(II) fluoride, sold in toothpaste as stannous fluoride.
  • (d) 3 SO42− = 6−, shared by 2 cobalt ions, so Co3+: cobalt(III) sulfate.
  • (e) 2 O2− = 4−, so Pb4+: lead(IV) oxide (plumbic oxide).

Check. The Roman numeral is the charge of one metal ion, not the number of metal atoms. In (d) there are 2 cobalt ions but the numeral is III.

Mineral sunscreens show both kinds of metal side by side. Zinc oxide, ZnO, needs no Roman numeral, because zinc is always 2+. Titanium is a transition metal that can form more than one ion, so TiO2 needs one: two O2− ions put 4− on the scale, so the titanium is 4+, and the name is titanium(IV) oxide. (On sunscreen labels it is listed by its common name, titanium dioxide.)

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): From name to formula

Problem. Write formulas for (a) iron(II) phosphate; (b) chromium(III) oxide; (c) mercury(I) chloride; (d) ammonium acetate.

Solution. (a) Fe2+ and PO43−: Fe3(PO4)2. (b) Cr3+ and O2−: Cr2O3. (c) Hg22+ and Cl−: Hg2Cl2. (d) NH4+ and C2H3O2−: NH4C2H3O2.

Check. The Roman numeral gives you the cation's charge directly, then swap-drop as usual.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): The Roman numeral is a charge, not a subscript

Problem. Write formulas for (a) copper(I) oxide; (b) tin(IV) sulfide; (c) chromium(III) sulfate.

Solution. Read each Roman numeral as the cation's charge, look up the anion's charge, then swap-drop.

  • (a) Cu+ and O2−: it takes two Cu+ to balance one O2−, so Cu2O, not CuO.
  • (b) Sn4+ and S2−: swap-drop gives Sn2S4, which reduces to SnS2, not SnS4.
  • (c) Cr3+ and SO42−: Cr2(SO4)3.

Check. (a) 2(1+) + (2−) = 0. (b) (4+) + 2(2−) = 0. (c) 2(3+) + 3(2−) = 0. In (a) the numeral is I, yet the subscript on copper is 2; in (b) the numeral is IV, yet tin has no subscript at all. The numeral and the subscript are answers to different questions.

Professor Tip

Ask one question before you name an ionic compound: is the metal on the fixed-charge list (group 1, group 2, and the steps: Al, Zn, Ag)? If yes, no Roman numeral. If no, you must calculate the charge and include it. A Roman numeral on a fixed-charge metal ("sodium(I) chloride") is wrong, and so is leaving it off a variable-charge metal ("iron chloride" could be two different compounds). Read symbols carefully: Mg (magnesium, group 2) never takes a numeral, but Mn (manganese, a transition metal) does. And when you go from a name to a formula, say it to yourself: "the Roman numeral is a charge, not a subscript."

Hydrates

Some ionic compounds trap a fixed number of water molecules in their crystals, with the waters packed around the ions. These are hydrates. The formula shows the water after a raised dot, and the name adds a Greek prefix plus "hydrate" (prefixes are listed in Section 4.4):

  • CuSO4·5H2O: copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (bright blue crystals).
  • MgSO4·7H2O: magnesium sulfate heptahydrate (Epsom salt).
  • CaSO4·2H2O: calcium sulfate dihydrate (gypsum, used in drywall, plaster casts, and as a soil amendment on Valley farms).

Heating a hydrate drives off the water and leaves the anhydrous ("without water") compound. You can watch it happen with copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate: as the blue crystals are heated, they turn white, because losing the waters changes how the ions are arranged in the crystal. Some hydrates lose water even at room temperature when they sit in dry air, a process called efflorescence; the same word is used for the white, powdery crust of salts left on concrete and brick walls when water evaporates from them.

The raised dot in a hydrate formula is not a multiplication sign. CuSO4·5H2O means "one CuSO4 plus five water molecules." When you find a hydrate's molar mass in Section 5.2, you add the mass of the waters; you do not multiply. Hydrates are also everyday chemicals: many supplements and medicines are sold in hydrate form because it is a stable solid that stores and ships well, so look for words like "monohydrate" or "dihydrate" on labels (creatine monohydrate is a common example).

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Hydrates, both directions

Problem. (a) Write the formula of sodium acetate trihydrate. (b) Name FeCl3·6H2O.

Solution. (a) Build the ionic part first: Na+ and C2H3O2− give NaC2H3O2. Tri- means 3 waters: NaC2H3O2·3H2O. (b) Name the ionic part: three Cl− = 3−, so Fe3+, iron(III) chloride. Six waters is hexa-: iron(III) chloride hexahydrate.

Check. The prefix counts only the waters; the ionic part is named with the usual ionic rules, Roman numeral included, and never gets a prefix.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many medications are sold as salts: the drug, which is often a large organic molecule with a positive or negative charge, paired with a simple ion. The salt partner is right there in the name. Albuterol sulfate and morphine sulfate pair the drug with sulfate; ipratropium bromide pairs it with bromide; diphenhydramine hydrochloride was made with hydrochloric acid. Electrolyte replacements are simple inorganic salts whose names you can now decode: potassium chloride (KCl), magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), calcium gluconate, sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), and potassium phosphate. Similar-looking names are a known source of medication errors, and concentrated potassium chloride is on safety lists of high-alert medications, so nurses read the whole name, including the ion, every time. (Context only; this is not dosing or treatment guidance.)

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

For a barium swallow or barium enema, the patient takes a suspension of barium sulfate, BaSO4. Barium has many electrons per atom, so it absorbs X-rays strongly and outlines the esophagus, stomach, or colon on the image. The name matters: barium sulfate is one of the few barium compounds that is almost completely insoluble in water, so very few barium ions (Ba2+) dissolve and enter the blood. Soluble barium compounds, such as barium chloride or barium carbonate (which dissolves in stomach acid), release Ba2+ ions and are poisonous. Radiology staff confirm the exact product name before use.

Common Mistakes

  • Using the subscript as the Roman numeral. In Fe2O3 the iron is iron(III), not iron(II).
  • Adding Roman numerals to fixed-charge metals. CaCl2 is calcium chloride.
  • Using prefixes in ionic names. Prefixes are for molecular compounds and hydrates only; Na2O is sodium oxide, not disodium oxide.
  • Changing a polyatomic ion's ending. Na2SO4 is sodium sulfate, not sodium sulfide.
  • Writing HgCl for mercury(I) chloride. The mercury(I) ion is Hg22+.
  • Reading the Roman numeral as a subscript (name to formula). Copper(I) oxide is Cu2O, not CuO: the I is copper's charge, 1+, and it takes two Cu+ to balance one O2−. Always swap-drop after you read the charge.
  • Confusing magnesium and manganese. MgCl2 is magnesium chloride (Mg is group 2, fixed charge). MnCl2 is manganese(II) chloride (Mn is a transition metal and needs a numeral).
  • Multiplying through a hydrate dot. The dot in CuSO4·5H2O means "plus five waters," not "times."

Summary

  • Ionic name = cation name + anion name. Monatomic anions end in -ide; polyatomic anions keep their names.
  • Fixed-charge metals (groups 1 and 2, Al, Zn, Ag) and ammonium use just their names.
  • Variable-charge metals need a Roman numeral equal to the charge of one metal ion, found by balancing the anions' charge. They come in three pairs: I/II (Cu, Hg), II/III (Cr, Fe, Co), II/IV (Sn, Pb). Older names use -ous (lower charge) and -ic (higher charge).
  • From a name, the Roman numeral is the charge, not a subscript: copper(I) oxide is Cu2O.
  • Hydrates add a prefix + "hydrate": CuSO4·5H2O is copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate. The dot means "plus waters"; heating leaves the anhydrous compound.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Name (a) ZnBr2; (b) Cu2S; (c) Fe(NO3)2; (d) (NH4)3PO4.

Hint

First ask: is the metal on the fixed-charge list (group 1, group 2, Al, Zn, Ag)? If not, find its charge from the total anion charge and write it as a Roman numeral.

Answer

(a) zinc bromide (no numeral: Zn is fixed at 2+). (b) copper(I) sulfide: one S2− shared by two Cu, so each is 1+. (c) iron(II) nitrate: 2 NO3− = 2−. (d) ammonium phosphate.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write formulas for (a) lead(II) nitrate; (b) tin(IV) chloride; (c) cobalt(II) hydroxide; (d) ferric oxide.

Hint

Read each Roman numeral as the cation's charge, not a subscript; ferric is the higher iron charge. Then look up the anion, swap-drop, and reduce if you can.

Answer

(a) Pb(NO3)2. (b) SnCl4. (c) Co(OH)2. (d) Fe2O3 (ferric = iron(III)).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Name BaCl2·2H2O, and write the formula for sodium carbonate decahydrate.

Hint

Name the salt first, then add a Greek prefix plus "hydrate" for the waters. Going the other way, the number of waters goes in front of H2O after a raised dot.

Answer

Barium chloride dihydrate. Na2CO3·10H2O.

Barium is a group 2 fixed-charge metal (no numeral) and di- means 2 waters; deca- means 10 waters, written after Na2CO3, where two Na+ balance one CO32−.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Give the symbol and charge of each ion: (a) cobaltous; (b) stannic; (c) cuprous; (d) ferric; (e) lead(IV); (f) mercuric.

Hint

Use the charge pairs: copper and mercury are I/II; chromium, iron, and cobalt are II/III; tin and lead are II/IV. The -ous name is the lower charge, -ic the higher.

Answer

(a) Co2+. (b) Sn4+. (c) Cu+. (d) Fe3+. (e) Pb4+. (f) Hg2+. In each pair, -ous is the lower charge and -ic the higher.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Write formulas for (a) iron(III) sulfide; (b) lead(IV) oxide; (c) copper(I) nitrate; (d) manganese(II) phosphate.

Hint

Turn each Roman numeral into the cation's charge, get the anion's charge (periodic table or green sheet), then swap-drop and reduce.

Answer

(a) Fe3+ and S2−: Fe2S3. (b) Pb4+ and O2−: Pb2O4 reduces to PbO2. (c) Cu+ and NO3−: CuNO3. (d) Mn2+ and PO43−: Mn3(PO4)2.
