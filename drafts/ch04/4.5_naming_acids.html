Learning Objectives

  • Recognize an acid from its formula.
  • Name binary acids (hydro-...-ic acid) and write their formulas.
  • Name oxyacids from their -ate and -ite anions and write their formulas.
  • Name and write formulas for acetic acid and oxalic acid.
  • Name bases that are metal hydroxides and write their formulas.

Vinegar, stomach fluid, car batteries, soft drinks, and the fizz in sparkling water all contain acids. In Chapter 12 you will learn what acids do; here you will learn what they are called. An acid in this chapter is a compound whose formula starts with H and that releases hydrogen ions (H+) when it dissolves in water. You can think of every acid as H+ ions joined to an anion, so the acid's name comes straight from the anion's name. At the end of the section you will name the common bases, which are even easier.

Binary Acids

A binary acid is hydrogen combined with one other nonmetal, a halogen or sulfur, dissolved in water. Its anion is an -ide ion. To name it:

hydro- + root of the nonmetal + -ic acid

  • HF(aq): fluoride → hydrofluoric acid
  • HCl(aq): chloride → hydrochloric acid
  • HBr(aq): bromide → hydrobromic acid
  • HI(aq): iodide → hydroiodic acid
  • H2S(aq): sulfide → hydrosulfuric acid (two H+ to balance S2−; the root becomes "sulfur")

The (aq) matters. Pure HCl is a gas called hydrogen chloride; only its water solution is hydrochloric acid.

Oxyacids

An oxyacid contains hydrogen, oxygen, and another element; its anion is an oxyanion (Section 4.2). Add enough H+ to cancel the anion's charge, then change the ending. There is no "hydro-" on an oxyacid.

  • An -ate ion becomes an -ic acid.
  • An -ite ion becomes an -ous acid.
  • Prefixes (per-, hypo-) stay in the name.

Three memory aids from the CHEM 3A lectures: "my ride has hydrolics" (-ide → hydro-...-ic), "I hate this mnemonic" (-ate → -ic), and "Sprite is delicious" (-ite → -ous).

The table below lists the common oxyacids. For sulfur and phosphorus, the root is lengthened to make the name easier to say: sulfuric, phosphoric.

sulfate, SO42−H2SO4sulfuric acid
sulfite, SO32−H2SO3sulfurous acid
nitrate, NO3−HNO3nitric acid
nitrite, NO2−HNO2nitrous acid

carbonate, CO32−H2CO3carbonic acid

Acetic acid is the acid in vinegar (about 5% in water). Oxalic acid occurs naturally in spinach and rhubarb leaves, and calcium oxalate (CaC2O4) is the most common compound in kidney stones.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Name the acid

Problem. Name (a) HBr(aq); (b) HNO2; (c) H3PO4; (d) HClO; (e) HBrO4.

Solution.

  • (a) Anion Br−, bromide: hydrobromic acid.
  • (b) Anion NO2−, nitrite: nitrous acid.
  • (c) Anion PO43−, phosphate: phosphoric acid.
  • (d) Anion ClO−, hypochlorite: hypochlorous acid.
  • (e) Anion BrO4−, perbromate (one more O than bromate): perbromic acid.

Check. Only (a) has no oxygen, so only (a) gets "hydro-."

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Write the formula

Problem. Write formulas for (a) sulfurous acid; (b) chloric acid; (c) hydroiodic acid; (d) oxalic acid.

Solution.

  • (a) -ous comes from -ite: sulfite, SO32−. Two H+ cancel 2−: H2SO3.
  • (b) -ic from -ate: chlorate, ClO3−: HClO3.
  • (c) hydro-...-ic from -ide: iodide, I−: HI(aq).
  • (d) oxalate, C2O42−: H2C2O4.

Check. The number of H atoms always equals the size of the anion's negative charge.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): The chlorine ladder

Problem. Chlorine forms four oxyanions: ClO−, ClO2−, ClO3−, and ClO4−. Name each ion, then write and name the acid made from each.

Solution. Each ion is 1−, so each acid needs exactly one H+. Keep any prefix, and swap only the suffix:

Table \(\PageIndex{2}\): Chlorine oxyanions and their acids
IonIon nameAcidAcid name
ClO−hypochloriteHClOhypochlorous acid
ClO2−chloriteHClO2chlorous acid
ClO3−chlorateHClO3chloric acid
ClO4−perchlorateHClO4perchloric acid

Check. The "chlor" root and the prefixes (hypo-, per-) never change; only the ending switches, -ite to -ous and -ate to -ic. Hypochlorite becomes hypochlorous acid (it comes from an -ite ion), not "hypochloric."

Professor Tip

Work through the anion every time. Ask "what anion is hiding after the H?", name that anion, and then switch the ending. Students who try to memorize acid names directly mix up sulfuric and sulfurous, or nitric and nitrous. Students who go through the anion (sulfate or sulfite? nitrate or nitrite?) get them right.

Naming Bases

The common bases in this course are metal hydroxides: a metal cation, most often from group 1 or group 2, combined with the hydroxide ion, OH−. They are ionic compounds, so they follow the rules of Sections 4.2 and 4.3 with nothing new to learn: name the metal, then say "hydroxide." No prefixes, and no change to the ending. The only real work is balancing the charges, and remembering that more than one hydroxide needs parentheses.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Naming bases

Problem. (a) Name NaOH and Ba(OH)2. (b) Write the formulas of potassium hydroxide and calcium hydroxide.

Solution. (a) Na+ and OH−: sodium hydroxide. Ba2+ and two OH−: barium hydroxide (not "barium dihydroxide"). (b) K+ and OH− combine 1 : 1: KOH. Ca2+ needs two hydroxides: Ca(OH)2.

Check. In Ca(OH)2, the 2 sits outside the parentheses because it counts whole OH− ions; CaOH2 would mean one O and two H. (2+) + 2(1−) = 0.

Professor Tip: Front or Back?

Look at where the H is. Hydrogens in the front indicate acids: HCl(aq), H2SO4, HNO3. Hydroxides in the back indicate bases: NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)2. Acetic acid, HC2H3O2, has H atoms in the middle, but the H written in front is the one it gives up, which is why the formula is written that way. What acids and bases do, and how strong they are, is the subject of Chapter 12.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

Every breath connects to an acid in this section. Carbon dioxide from your cells dissolves in blood and reacts with water to form carbonic acid, H2CO3, which releases H+ and bicarbonate, HCO3−. An arterial blood gas (ABG) report, which respiratory therapists draw and interpret, lists the pH, the partial pressure of CO2, and the bicarbonate level (HCO3−). When a patient like Mateo cannot breathe out CO2 fast enough during a severe asthma attack, more carbonic acid forms and the blood becomes more acidic. You will see this balance again with buffers in Chapter 12.

Biology Connection: Physiology and Microbiology

Cells lining your stomach, called parietal cells, pump H+ and Cl− ions into the stomach, making hydrochloric acid strong enough to unfold food proteins and kill many swallowed bacteria. Bacteria make acids too: in dental plaque, bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans ferment sugars into lactic acid, which dissolves calcium phosphate from tooth enamel and starts a cavity. Muscles also make lactate during hard exercise.

Common Mistakes

  • Adding hydro- to an oxyacid. H2SO4 is sulfuric acid, not hydrosulfuric acid (that is H2S).
  • Swapping -ic and -ous. -ate → -ic; -ite → -ous.
  • Getting the number of H wrong. Use the anion's charge: phosphate is 3−, so phosphoric acid is H3PO4.
  • Calling HCl(g) hydrochloric acid. Without water it is hydrogen chloride.
  • Dropping a prefix, or changing it. Hypochlorite becomes hypochlorous acid, not chlorous or "hypochloric" acid.
  • Leaving out parentheses in a base. Barium hydroxide is Ba(OH)2, not BaOH2; and it is not "barium dihydroxide," because bases are ionic.

Summary

  • Acids start with H and release H+ in water.
  • Binary acids (-ide anion): hydro- + root + -ic acid (HCl(aq), hydrochloric acid).
  • Oxyacids: -ate → -ic acid; -ite → -ous acid; prefixes stay; no hydro-.
  • The number of H atoms equals the anion's charge. Acetic acid is HC2H3O2; oxalic acid is H2C2O4.
  • Bases in this course are metal hydroxides, named like any ionic compound: metal + "hydroxide" (Ba(OH)2, barium hydroxide). H in front means acid; OH in back means base.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Name (a) HF(aq); (b) H2CO3; (c) HClO2; (d) HNO3.

Answer

(a) hydrofluoric acid. (b) carbonic acid. (c) chlorous acid (chlorite → -ous). (d) nitric acid.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write formulas for (a) perchloric acid; (b) phosphorous acid (from phosphite, PO33−); (c) acetic acid.

Answer

(a) HClO4. (b) H3PO3. (c) HC2H3O2 (also written CH3COOH).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What is the difference between hydrosulfuric acid and sulfuric acid?

Answer

Hydrosulfuric acid is H2S(aq), a binary acid from sulfide (no oxygen). Sulfuric acid is H2SO4, an oxyacid from sulfate.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Classify each as an acid or a base, and name it: (a) LiOH; (b) HBrO2; (c) Sr(OH)2; (d) HIO4.

Answer

(a) Base, lithium hydroxide. (b) Acid, bromous acid (bromite, BrO2− → -ous). (c) Base, strontium hydroxide. (d) Acid, periodic acid (periodate, IO4− → per-...-ic).
