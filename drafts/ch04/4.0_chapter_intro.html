Patient Case: Mateo's Medication List

Later in the emergency department, the nurse reviews what Mateo Herrera (Chapters 2 and 3) has received for his asthma attack. The list reads: albuterol sulfate by nebulizer, ipratropium bromide by nebulizer, magnesium sulfate by IV, and prednisone by mouth. Three of those four names end with the name of an ion you already know or will learn in this chapter: sulfate, bromide, sulfate. The drug is the first part of the name; the ion is a partner that makes the medicine a stable, dissolvable salt.

Names like these are not decoration. "Magnesium sulfate" and "manganese sulfate" differ by a few letters and are completely different compounds; "potassium chloride" and "potassium phosphate" are both IV electrolytes with different uses. Every chemical name follows a set of rules, and once you know the rules, a name tells you the formula and a formula tells you the name. That is the skill of this chapter. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Read a chemical formula, count its atoms, and classify the compound as ionic, molecular, or an acid (Section 4.1).
  • Predict ion charges, recall the polyatomic ions with the T43 pattern, write formulas of ionic compounds, including those with polyatomic ions, and deduce ion charges from a formula (Section 4.2).
  • Name ionic compounds of fixed-charge and variable-charge metals and of polyatomic ions, and name simple hydrates (Section 4.3).
  • Name binary molecular compounds with Greek prefixes, and write their formulas (Section 4.4).
  • Name binary acids and oxyacids, including acetic acid and oxalic acid, and name bases that are metal hydroxides (Section 4.5).
  • Decide which set of rules a compound needs and move between names and formulas for any compound in this course (Section 4.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Chemical names are the vocabulary of every lab report, medication label, safety data sheet, and fertilizer bag. In health care, a misread name can mean the wrong drug or the wrong dose; in agriculture, it can mean the wrong amendment on a field. In Chapters 2 and 3 you learned which ions atoms form; this chapter puts those ions together into compounds and gives each compound its name. Chapter 5 then uses formulas to find molar masses, and Chapter 6 looks at how the atoms in molecular compounds are bonded.
