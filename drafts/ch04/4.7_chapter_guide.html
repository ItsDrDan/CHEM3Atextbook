Learning Objectives

  • Read formulas, count atoms, and classify compounds as ionic, molecular, or acids.
  • Predict ion charges (columns and "the steps"), recall the -ate ions with T43, write ionic formulas with swap-drop, including polyatomic ions; deduce ion charges from formulas.
  • Name ionic compounds of fixed- and variable-charge metals (Roman numerals and older -ous/-ic names), polyatomic ions, and hydrates.
  • Name binary molecular compounds with Greek prefixes.
  • Name binary acids and oxyacids, including acetic and oxalic acid, and bases (metal hydroxides).
  • Choose the right rules for any compound and move between names and formulas.

How to Use This Guide

Read the Key Ideas after the sections, use the video moments for anything still unclear, work the examples with the answers covered, and then go to the Practice Problems. Key Ideas

1. Read the formula, then classify

Subscripts count atoms (superscripts are charges); a subscript after parentheses multiplies everything inside, but parentheses are never "multiplied out" in the formula itself: Fe(NO3)2 is one Fe2+ for every two NO3− units. Seven elements are diatomic: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2. Metal or NH4+ first = ionic; nonmetals only = molecular; H first in water = acid. (Section 4.1)

2. Ionic formulas are neutral

Main-group charges follow the columns; "the steps" give Al3+, Zn2+, Ag+. Swap the charge numbers, drop them as subscripts, reduce, check that the total charge is zero. A polyatomic ion is like family: it stays whole, gets parentheses only when there is more than one (Na2CO3 has none), and only the outside subscript can change or be reduced. Work backward from a formula to find an unknown metal charge, starting from the ion you know; un-swapping fails on reduced formulas such as SnO2 (Sn4+). (Section 4.2)

3. Polyatomic ions follow patterns

Learn the -ate ions on the green sheet; the T43 pattern on the periodic table gives their oxygen counts (T: B, C, N, Si have 3 O; 4: P, As, S, Se, Te have 4 O; 3: Cl, Br, I have 3 O), and the charges follow the columns except along the top of the T (nitrate 1−, carbonate 2−, borate 3−). per-...-ate: one more O; -ite: one less O; hypo-...-ite: two less; same charge. Each added H+ lowers the negative charge by 1: HCO3− is hydrogen carbonate (bicarbonate); H2PO4− is dihydrogen phosphate. Ammonium, NH4+, is the common polyatomic cation. (Section 4.2)

4. Ionic names: cation + anion

Fixed-charge metals (groups 1 and 2, Al, Zn, Ag) use just their names. Other metals need a Roman numeral for the charge of one ion (three pairs: I/II Cu, Hg; II/III Cr, Fe, Co; II/IV Sn, Pb), or the older -ous (lower) / -ic (higher) name: FeCl3 is iron(III) chloride (ferric chloride). Monatomic anions end in -ide; polyatomic anions keep their names. No prefixes, except for hydrates (CuSO4·5H2O, copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate; the dot means "plus waters"). Going from a name to a formula, the Roman numeral is a charge, not a subscript: copper(I) oxide is Cu2O. (Section 4.3)

5. Molecular names count atoms

Prefix + first element, prefix + second element with -ide. No mono- on the first element; drop a or o before oxide. N2O5 is dinitrogen pentoxide. Never reduce a molecular formula. (Section 4.4)

6. Acid names come from anion names

-ide → hydro-...-ic acid (HCl(aq), hydrochloric acid); -ate → -ic acid (H2SO4, sulfuric acid); -ite → -ous acid (HNO2, nitrous acid). Number of H = size of the anion charge. Acetic acid, HC2H3O2; oxalic acid, H2C2O4. Bases are metal hydroxides named like any ionic compound: Ba(OH)2, barium hydroxide. H in front = acid; OH in back = base. (Sections 4.5 and 4.6)

7. Name any compound with the blue naming sheet

Ask the blue-sheet questions in order. Metal? Yes → ionic. Transition metal? If yes, find its charge and write a Roman numeral (zinc and silver are the exceptions: fixed charges, no numeral; tin and lead need one). Then count elements: 2 = binary (metal + nonmetal-ide); 3 or more = ternary (metal + polyatomic ion, ending unchanged). No metal? H first → acid (hydro-...-ic for H + one element; -ate → -ic, -ite → -ous). Otherwise count elements: 1 = diatomic element (Br2, I2, N2, Cl2, O2, F2, and H2, hydrogen: just the element's name); 2 = covalent binary (prefixes, no mono- on the first element, -ide). Ammonium compounds have no metal but are ionic. (Section 4.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Mateo's medication list

Problem. Mateo received magnesium sulfate by IV. Write its formula, name its ions, and explain why the name has no Roman numeral.

Solution. Magnesium is in group 2, a fixed-charge metal (Mg2+); sulfate is SO42−. Equal and opposite charges combine 1 : 1: MgSO4. No Roman numeral, because magnesium forms only one ion.

Check. (2+) + (2−) = 0. The same reasoning names his albuterol sulfate and ipratropium bromide partners: SO42− and Br−.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): A variable-charge metal with a polyatomic ion

Problem. Iron supplements often contain ferrous sulfate. Write its Stock name and formula, and give the formula of ferric sulfate.

Solution. Ferrous = iron(II), Fe2+: iron(II) sulfate, FeSO4. Ferric = iron(III), Fe3+, with SO42−: swap-drop gives Fe2(SO4)3, iron(III) sulfate.

Check. 2(3+) + 3(2−) = 0. -ous is the lower charge, -ic the higher.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Three systems, one sulfur

Problem. Name SO3, Na2SO3, and H2SO3.

Solution. SO3: two nonmetals, sulfur trioxide. Na2SO3: metal first, ionic, with sulfite: sodium sulfite. H2SO3: an acid from sulfite: sulfurous acid.

Check. Only the molecular compound uses a prefix; the acid ending follows -ite → -ous.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Name to formula, all types

Problem. Write formulas for (a) tin(IV) oxide; (b) dinitrogen pentoxide; (c) ammonium dichromate; (d) hydrofluoric acid.

Solution. (a) Sn4+, O2−: Sn2O4 reduces to SnO2. (b) N2O5. (c) NH4+, Cr2O72−: (NH4)2Cr2O7. (d) HF(aq).

Check. Ionic formulas are reduced (SnO2); the subscripts inside dichromate are never changed.

Common Mistakes

  • Classify before naming: prefixes are for molecular compounds only.
  • Reduce ionic formulas, but never change subscripts inside a polyatomic ion.
  • Use parentheses when more than one polyatomic ion is needed.
  • The Roman numeral is the charge of one metal ion, not a subscript, in both directions: Fe2O3 is iron(III) oxide, and copper(I) oxide is Cu2O.
  • No parentheses around a single polyatomic ion (Na2CO3), and reduce the outside subscripts (Sn(SO4)2).
  • Mg (magnesium) never takes a Roman numeral; Mn (manganese) does.
  • No Roman numerals on groups 1 and 2, Al, Zn, or Ag.
  • -ate → -ic acid, -ite → -ous acid, -ide → hydro-...-ic acid. No hydro- on oxyacids.
  • Sulfate, sulfite, and sulfide are three different ions.
