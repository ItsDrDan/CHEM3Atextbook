Learning Objectives

  • Predict the charges of common ions from the periodic table, including the fixed charges of Al, Zn, and Ag ("the steps").
  • Write the formula of a binary ionic compound from its ions using the swap-drop method.
  • Recognize common polyatomic ions, recall the -ate ions with the T43 pattern, and use the green sheet to work out related ions.
  • Write formulas of ionic compounds that contain polyatomic ions, using parentheses only when more than one polyatomic ion is needed.
  • Deduce the charge of an ion from the formula of a compound, starting from the ion whose charge you know.

An ionic compound has no overall charge: the positive charges of its cations exactly cancel the negative charges of its anions. That single rule, the total charge must be zero, is all you need to write any ionic formula. In Section 3.5 you learned which charges main-group ions take; now you will combine them.

Charges You Can Predict

Before you can write a formula, you need each ion's charge. For the main-group elements, the charge follows the column of the periodic table, so it is the same every time. Write these charges at the top of the columns on your own blank periodic table:

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Predictable ion charges by column
ColumnIon chargeExamples
Group 1 (alkali metals)1+Li+, Na+, K+
Group 2 (alkaline earth metals)2+Mg2+, Ca2+, Ba2+
Group 15 (nitrogen group)3−N3− (nitride), P3− (phosphide)
Group 16 (oxygen group)2−O2− (oxide), S2− (sulfide)
Group 17 (halogens)1−F−, Cl−, Br−, I−
Group 18 (noble gases)nonealready stable; they do not form ions

Three more metals have charges you can count on, and they sit on a little staircase of their own. Start at aluminum, in group 13, and "walk down the steps": each step goes down one row and one column to the left. Aluminum is Al3+, the next step is zinc, Zn2+, and the last step is silver, Ag+: three, two, one. Most other transition metals can form more than one ion; you will name those in Section 4.3.

Professor Tip: The Steps

Find aluminum, zinc, and silver on your periodic table and draw a staircase through them, labeled 3+, 2+, 1+. Students who mark "the steps" on their table almost never put a Roman numeral on aluminum, zinc, or silver, and they never have to guess their charges. Group 1, group 2, and the steps are the metals whose charges are always the same.

Binary Ionic Compounds: Swap-Drop

A binary compound contains just two elements. To write the formula of a binary ionic compound:

  1. Write the ions with their charges, metal (cation) first.
  2. Swap the numbers of the charges and drop them down as subscripts on the other ion. Leave the signs behind.
  3. Reduce the subscripts to the smallest whole-number ratio, and do not write a subscript of 1.
  4. Check that the total charge is zero.

Aluminum oxide, Al2O3, the first swap-drop example, is a real and useful compound. As the mineral corundum it is one of the hardest natural substances. A trace of iron and titanium ions turns it blue (sapphire), and a trace of chromium(III) ions turns it red (ruby). Clear, lab-grown sapphire is also used to make the nearly invisible ceramic brackets on some braces.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Binary ionic formulas

Problem. Write the formula of the ionic compound formed by (a) sodium and oxygen; (b) magnesium and nitrogen; (c) calcium and sulfur.

Solution.

  • (a) Na+ and O2−: swap-drop gives Na2O. Check: 2(1+) + (2−) = 0.
  • (b) Mg2+ and N3−: swap-drop gives Mg3N2. Check: 3(2+) + 2(3−) = 6+ + 6− = 0.
  • (c) Ca2+ and S2−: swap-drop gives Ca2S2, which reduces to CaS.

Check. Ions with equal and opposite charges (2+ and 2−) always combine 1 : 1.

Polyatomic Ions

A polyatomic ion is a group of atoms, bonded together, that carries an overall charge. It acts as a single unit in a compound and keeps its own name. The table below lists the polyatomic ions used in this course. Most are oxyanions, anions that contain oxygen. These are the ions listed on your green sheet. Other oxyanions (-ite, per-...-ate, hypo-...-ite) and the hydrogen ions follow from the naming rules below.

Naming patterns for oxyanions

You do not have to memorize every oxyanion separately. Start from the "-ate" ion and change the number of oxygen atoms; the charge stays the same:

  • per-...-ate: one more O than the -ate ion (perchlorate, ClO4−).
  • -ite: one less O than the -ate ion (sulfite, SO32−; nitrite, NO2−; chlorite, ClO2−).
  • hypo-...-ite: one less O than the -ite ion (hypochlorite, ClO−, the active ion in bleach).

Adding H+ to an oxyanion does change the charge, by one for each H. Add "hydrogen" (or "dihydrogen" for two) to the front of the name. An older name for an ion with one added H uses the prefix bi-:

  • HCO3−: hydrogen carbonate, also called bicarbonate (the "bicarb" in baking soda and in blood chemistry).
  • HSO4−: hydrogen sulfate, or bisulfate. HSO3−: hydrogen sulfite, or bisulfite.
  • HPO42−: hydrogen phosphate. H2PO4−: dihydrogen phosphate.

Professor Tip

Use your green sheet as a map, not a crutch. Memorize the -ate ions with their charges, then build everything else: one fewer O is -ite, one more is per-...-ate, and each H+ you add lowers the negative charge by one. Practice until you can read SO42− as "sulfate" as fast as you read "Na" as "sodium." Think of each polyatomic ion as one unit that your eyes recognize by shape.

Memorizing the Polyatomic Ions: The T43 Method

Why memorize them at all, if the green sheet lists them? Because speed matters on an exam, and because these ions follow you into every later science course. Phosphate, bicarbonate, sulfate, and nitrate come up constantly in physiology, nutrition, and microbiology, usually with no reference sheet in sight. The good news is that you do not have to memorize the whole sheet. If you know the -ate ions in the "start here" box (the oxyanions with the most common number of oxygens), the naming rules above build nearly everything else.

The -ate ions also hide a pattern on the periodic table. The nonmetals that form them trace out the shapes of a letter T, a number 4, and a number 3, side by side from left to right. Each shape tells you how many oxygen atoms its ions have: the T stands for three O atoms, the 4 for four, and the 3 for three. The green sheet's "start here" box is laid out in the same T, 4, 3 arrangement.

Table \(\PageIndex{3}\): The T43 pattern for the -ate ions
ShapeElementsOxygen atoms-ate ionsCharge rule
TB, C, N across period 2, with Si below C3borate BO33−, carbonate CO32−, nitrate NO3−, silicate SiO32−Runs backward along the top of the T: N is 1−, C is 2−, B is 3−. Silicate matches carbonate, the ion above it.
4P, As (group 15) and S, Se, Te (group 16)4phosphate PO43−, arsenate AsO43−, sulfate SO42−, selenate SeO42−, tellurate TeO42−Same as the column's ion charge: group 15 is 3−, group 16 is 2−.
3Cl, Br, I (group 17)3chlorate ClO3−, bromate BrO3−, iodate IO3−Same as the column's ion charge: 1−.

Notice that the charges mostly follow the same column trend as the simple ions in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\). The one twist is the T, where the trend resets and runs backward: nitrate 1−, carbonate 2−, borate 3−.

Reading the green sheet

Once you know the pattern, the green sheet reads like a map, in this order:

  1. Start here: the -ate ions, laid out as T, 4, 3.
  2. Oxygen modifiers: if the number of O atoms does not match an -ate ion, use per- (one more O), -ite (one less), or hypo-...-ite (two less). The charge stays the same.
  3. Hydrogen modifiers: if the ion has H in front, each H+ lowers the negative charge by one. Say "hydrogen" (or "dihydrogen") in front of the name; the older prefix bi- also means one added H.
  4. Alternate starts: if the ion matches none of the above, it is one of the "other common" ions that you simply learn: hydroxide, cyanide, peroxide, acetate, oxalate, permanganate, chromate, dichromate, and the cations ammonium, hydronium, and mercury(I).

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Using the T43 pattern

Problem. Without the green sheet, give the formula and charge of (a) the selenate ion; (b) the borate ion; (c) the selenite ion. Then write the formula of (d) sodium selenate and (e) magnesium arsenate.

Solution.

  • (a) Se is in the "4" (group 16), so 4 O atoms and the group 16 charge: SeO42−.
  • (b) B is on the T, so 3 O atoms; along the top of the T the charges run backward (N 1−, C 2−, B 3−): BO33−.
  • (c) -ite has one less O than -ate, same charge: SeO32−.
  • (d) Na+ and SeO42−: Na2SeO4.
  • (e) As is in the "4" (group 15): arsenate is AsO43−. With Mg2+, swap-drop gives Mg3(AsO4)2 (parentheses: see below).

Check. Selenium sits right under sulfur, so selenate should look like sulfate (SO42−), and it does. In (e), 3(2+) + 2(3−) = 0.

Formulas with Polyatomic Ions

Swap-drop works the same way with polyatomic ions. Treat the whole polyatomic ion as one unit, and if you need more than one of it, put it in parentheses with the subscript outside. Never change the subscripts inside a polyatomic ion: that would make a different ion.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Formulas with polyatomic ions

Problem. Write the formulas of the compounds formed from (a) calcium and phosphate; (b) aluminum and hydroxide; (c) ammonium and sulfate; (d) sodium and bicarbonate.

Solution.

  • (a) Ca2+ and PO43−: Ca3(PO4)2. Check: 3(2+) + 2(3−) = 0.
  • (b) Al3+ and OH−: Al(OH)3.
  • (c) NH4+ and SO42−: (NH4)2SO4.
  • (d) Na+ and HCO3−: NaHCO3 (no parentheses needed, because there is only one bicarbonate).

Check. In (a), writing Ca3PO42 would be meaningless; the parentheses show that the 2 counts whole phosphate ions.

When to use parentheses, and when not to

Three rules cover every case:

  1. Parentheses are only for polyatomic ions, and only when the subscript is more than 1. Na+ and CO32− swap-drop to Na2CO3: the carbonate gets a 1, and 1 is never written, so there are no parentheses. Writing Na2(CO3) is not chemically wrong, but chemists do not write what they do not need, and auto-graded homework will often mark it incorrect. A single atom never gets parentheses either: two fluoride ions is F2, as in MgF2.
  2. Never change the subscripts inside the parentheses. Carbonate is always CO3, nitrate is always NO3. Only the subscript outside the parentheses can change.
  3. Always reduce the outside subscripts when they share a common factor, just as for binary compounds.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Parentheses and reducing

Problem. Write the formulas of the compounds formed from (a) potassium and sulfate; (b) barium and nitrite; (c) Sn4+ and sulfate.

Solution.

  • (a) K+ and SO42−: swap-drop gives K2(SO4)1. The sulfate subscript is 1, so drop it and the parentheses: K2SO4.
  • (b) Ba2+ and NO2−: two nitrite ions are needed, so Ba(NO2)2.
  • (c) Sn4+ and SO42−: swap-drop gives Sn2(SO4)4. The outside subscripts 2 and 4 share a factor of 2, so reduce them: Sn(SO4)2. The SO4 inside is untouched.

Check. (a) 2(1+) + (2−) = 0. (b) (2+) + 2(1−) = 0. (c) (4+) + 2(2−) = 0.

Professor Tip: A Polyatomic Ion Is Like Family

A polyatomic ion is like family: its atoms stay together, and you do not change the family. You can change how many of them come to the party (the subscript outside the parentheses), and you can reduce that number, but never the numbers inside. If an online homework answer looks right but is marked wrong, check two things first: did you put parentheses around a single polyatomic ion, and did you forget to reduce?

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Polyatomic ions in everyday life

Problem. Write the formulas of (a) sodium acetate, which gives salt-and-vinegar chips their vinegar flavor, and (b) lead(II) acetate. (In lead(II), the Roman numeral gives the charge, 2+; Section 4.3.)

Solution.

  • (a) Na+ and C2H3O2− combine 1 : 1: NaC2H3O2, with no parentheses.
  • (b) Pb2+ needs two acetate ions: Pb(C2H3O2)2.

Check. (1+) + (1−) = 0 and (2+) + 2(1−) = 0. Lead(II) acetate tastes sweet, and it was once called "sugar of lead." Ancient Romans sweetened food and wine with grape syrup boiled down in lead pots, which contained it; they did not realize the danger.

Pigments are another place polyatomic ions show up. Cyanide, CN−, was first made from the deep blue paint pigment Prussian blue, which is built from iron(III) ions and cyanide-containing iron units and appears in many famous paintings. Its real formula, Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3, is more complex than a simple swap-drop formula, so treat it as a story about cyanide rather than a formula to practice.

Working Backward: Charges from a Formula

Because the total charge is zero, you can use a formula to find an unknown charge. This is how you will figure out the charge of a metal that can form more than one ion (Section 4.3).

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Deduce the charge

Problem. What is the charge on the iron ion in Fe2O3? On the lead ion in PbS2?

Solution. Fe2O3: the three oxide ions give 3(2−) = 6−, so the two iron ions carry 6+ together, or 3+ each (Fe3+). PbS2: two sulfide ions give 2(2−) = 4−, so the single lead ion is Pb4+.

Check. 2(3+) + 3(2−) = 0 and (4+) + 2(2−) = 0. Watch out for reduced formulas: PbS2 is not Pb2+, even though the subscript is 2.

Why "reverse swap-drop" can fool you

It is tempting to find charges by un-swapping: move each subscript back up as the other ion's charge. That works only if the formula was never reduced, so it works some of the time and fails without warning:

  • MgCl2: un-swapping gives Mg2+ and Cl−. Correct, because the formula was not reduced.
  • SnO2: un-swapping gives Sn2+ and O−. Wrong. Oxide is always O2−, so two oxide ions carry 4− and the tin must be Sn4+. The formula Sn2O4 was reduced to SnO2, and un-swapping cannot see that.

The safe method is the one in Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): start from the ion whose charge you know (a predictable anion such as Cl−, O2−, or a polyatomic ion from the green sheet), find the total negative charge, and divide it among the metal ions. This matters most for the variable-charge metals of Section 4.3, which are exactly the ones you cannot look up.

Valley Connection: Nitrate in Valley Groundwater

The nitrate ion, NO3−, is one of the San Joaquin Valley's most important water-quality problems. Nitrogen fertilizers (such as ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3, and urea), dairy manure, and septic systems all add nitrogen to soil, and the part that crops do not take up can be changed to nitrate and carried down into groundwater. Because nearly all nitrate compounds dissolve in water, it does not stay in the soil. Many rural communities and private wells in the Valley rely on that groundwater for drinking.

The drinking-water limit in California and nationally is 10 mg/L of nitrate measured as nitrogen (about 45 mg/L measured as the whole nitrate ion). The main health concern is for infants: in a baby's body, nitrate can be converted to nitrite (NO2−), which interferes with hemoglobin's ability to carry oxygen, a condition called methemoglobinemia or "blue baby syndrome." A UC Davis study for the state found that nitrate from farming affects groundwater across the Tulare Lake Basin, which includes Fresno, Kings, Tulare, and Kern counties, and the State Water Board now funds well testing and safe-water projects for affected communities. Notice how much depends on reading the units and the ion correctly: "10 as N" and "45 as NO3−" describe the same water.

Sources: California State Water Resources Control Board, Nitrate in drinking water (waterboards.ca.gov); U.S. EPA National Primary Drinking Water Regulations; Harter, T. et al., Addressing Nitrate in California's Drinking Water, UC Davis Center for Watershed Sciences, report to the State Water Resources Control Board (2012).

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to reduce. Mg2+ and O2− give MgO, not Mg2O2.
  • Reducing inside a polyatomic ion. Never change SO4 to SO2; only the number of whole ions can change.
  • Leaving out parentheses. Two nitrates is (NO3)2, not NO32.
  • Thinking -ite and -ate ions have different charges. Sulfate and sulfite are both 2−.
  • Writing the anion first. The cation always comes first in an ionic formula.
  • Parentheses around a single polyatomic ion. Sodium carbonate is Na2CO3, not Na2(CO3). Parentheses appear only when the subscript is 2 or more.
  • Forgetting to reduce with polyatomic ions. Sn4+ and SO42− give Sn(SO4)2, not Sn2(SO4)4.
  • Un-swapping a reduced formula. SnO2 contains Sn4+, not Sn2+. Start from the ion you know (O2−).
  • Guessing the charges of Al, Zn, and Ag. Walk down "the steps": Al3+, Zn2+, Ag+.

Summary

  • Main-group ion charges follow the columns (1+, 2+, then 3−, 2−, 1− for groups 15–17). "The steps" give Al3+, Zn2+, Ag+.
  • Ionic formulas are neutral: swap the charge numbers, drop them as subscripts, reduce, and check.
  • Polyatomic ions are charged units; use parentheses only when more than one is needed, never change the subscripts inside, and reduce the outside subscripts.
  • The T43 pattern recalls the -ate ions: the T (B, C, N, Si) has 3 O, the 4 (P, As, S, Se, Te) has 4 O, the 3 (Cl, Br, I) has 3 O. Charges follow the columns, except along the top of the T, where they run backward (NO3−, CO32−, BO33−).
  • From the -ate ion: per-...-ate has one more O, -ite one less, hypo-...-ite two less, all with the same charge. Each added H+ lowers the negative charge by 1 (bicarbonate, HCO3−).
  • An unknown charge can be found from a formula because the total charge is zero. Start from the ion you know; un-swapping fails on reduced formulas.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the formulas for the compounds of (a) potassium and sulfur; (b) aluminum and chlorine; (c) barium and nitrogen.

Hint

Get each ion's charge from its group (group 1 is 1+, group 16 is 2−, and so on), then swap-drop, reduce, and check that the total charge is zero.

Answer

(a) K2S. (b) AlCl3. (c) Ba3N2.

Each formula has zero total charge: two K+ balance one S2−, three Cl− balance one Al3+, and three Ba2+ (6+) balance two N3− (6−).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the formulas for (a) magnesium nitrate; (b) sodium phosphate; (c) ammonium carbonate.

Hint

Look up each polyatomic ion on the green sheet, keep it as one unit, and use parentheses only when you need more than one of it.

Answer

(a) Mg(NO3)2. (b) Na3PO4. (c) (NH4)2CO3.

Mg2+ needs two NO3−, three Na+ balance one PO43−, and two NH4+ balance one CO32−.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Give the formula and charge of (a) the sulfite ion; (b) the perbromate ion; (c) the hydrogen sulfate ion.

Hint

Start from each -ate ion (sulfate, bromate). -ite has one less O and per-...-ate one more, with the same charge; each added H+ makes the charge one less negative.

Answer

(a) SO32− (one less O than sulfate). (b) BrO4− (one more O than bromate). (c) HSO4− (adding H+ to SO42− makes the charge one less negative, 1−).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

The chlorine family runs hypochlorite, chlorite, chlorate, perchlorate. Iodine sits two places below chlorine in group 17. Predict the formulas and charges of hypoiodite, iodite, iodate, and periodate.

Hint

Use the chlorine family as a template and change only the element: iodine is in the same column, so its ions have the same number of O atoms and the same charge.

Answer

IO−, IO2−, IO3−, IO4−, all 1−. Iodine is in the "3" of T43, so iodate has 3 O; adding or removing O never changes the charge.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Write the formulas of (a) calcium silicate; (b) potassium tellurate; (c) aluminum borate; (d) Pb4+ with carbonate.

Hint

Use T43: Si and B sit in the T (3 O), Te in the 4 (4 O); compare with carbonate, borate, and sulfate. For (d), swap-drop, then reduce the subscripts.

Answer

(a) CaSiO3 (silicate is SiO32−, like carbonate). (b) K2TeO4. (c) AlBO3 (3+ and 3− combine 1 : 1, no parentheses). (d) Pb2(CO3)4 reduces to Pb(CO3)2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

A student finds the charge on manganese in MnO2 by un-swapping and gets Mn2+. Find the correct charge, and explain what went wrong.

Hint

Start from the ion whose charge you know: oxide is always 2−. How much negative charge do two oxides carry? One Mn must balance it. Was the formula reduced?

Answer

Mn4+. Two O2− ions carry 4−, so the one manganese ion must be 4+. The formula was reduced (Mn2O4 → MnO2), so un-swapping gives the wrong charges, including an impossible O−.
