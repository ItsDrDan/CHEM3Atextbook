Learning Objectives

  • Describe solids, liquids, and gases by their shape and volume (the large-scale view).
  • Describe the spacing and motion of the particles in each state (the particle view).
  • Explain why gases are easy to compress and solids and liquids are not.

Water can be ice in a cold pack, liquid in a cup, or vapor in a humidifier, yet every one of those samples is made of the same H2O molecules. What changes is how close the molecules are and how freely they move. This section connects what you see (the macroscopic view) with what the particles are doing (the particle, or particulate, view).

Three States, Two Views

The three common states of matter (also called phases) are solid, liquid, and gas (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

The table below compares the three states.

Comparing solids, liquids, and gases: the macroscopic view (shape, volume, compressibility) and the particle view (spacing, motion)
PropertySolidLiquidGas
ShapeDefinite (keeps its own shape)Indefinite (takes the shape of the container)Indefinite (fills the container)
VolumeDefiniteDefiniteIndefinite (expands to fill any volume)
Particle spacingTouching, closely packedTouching, but not in fixed positionsFar apart; mostly empty space
Particle motionVibrate in placeSlide past one anotherMove fast in straight lines until they collide
Compressible?NoAlmost notYes, easily

A few pictures help these rows stick. Particles in a solid are packed together and hunkered down, vibrating in place but not trading spots. Particles in a liquid are still touching, but they "slide and slither" past one another, so a liquid goes with the flow. Pour a cup of water onto the counter and it takes the shape of the counter; let it drip onto the floor and it takes the shape of the floor. Its volume never changed, though: mop it all up into a graduated cylinder and you would measure the same number of milliliters. Particles in a gas are like billiard balls flying across a table, or a five-year-old hyped up on sugar at a birthday party: far apart, moving fast in every direction, and bouncing off whatever they hit. The hotter the gas, the faster they move.

You should be able to go both ways: given the state, describe what the particles are doing, and given a description of the particles, name the state.

Solids whose particles sit in a regular, repeating pattern (table salt, ice, quartz) are crystalline. Solids whose particles are jumbled, such as glass and many plastics, are amorphous. You will look at kinds of solids, and at the forces that hold particles together in liquids and solids, in Chapter 9.

Professor Tip

If an exam asks you to "describe what the particles are doing," answer two things: how far apart they are and how they move. "Gas: particles far apart, moving fast and randomly" earns full credit; "gas: it is air" does not.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Why can a gas be compressed?

Problem. An oxygen cylinder holds far more oxygen than its size suggests. Explain, using the particle view, why a gas can be squeezed into a small tank but a liquid cannot be squeezed much at all.

Solution. In a gas the particles are far apart, and most of the volume is empty space. Pushing the particles closer together just removes some of that empty space, so the gas compresses easily. In a liquid the particles are already touching, so there is almost no empty space to remove.

Check. The answer mentions particle spacing for both states, which is the property that controls compressibility.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Match the description to the state

Problem. A sample has a definite volume but takes the shape of whatever container it is poured into. Name the state and describe its particles.

Solution. Definite volume rules out a gas; no definite shape rules out a solid. It is a liquid: the particles touch one another (so the volume stays fixed) but can slide past each other (so it flows and takes the container's shape).

Check. Compare with the table: the liquid column is the only one with "definite" volume and "indefinite" shape.

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

Your body uses all three states. Bone and tooth enamel are solids built around a crystalline mineral (a calcium phosphate called hydroxyapatite), which is why they keep their shape under load. Blood plasma and the fluid inside your cells are liquids, so they flow through vessels and fill whatever space they occupy. In the lungs, oxygen and carbon dioxide are gases: they spread out to fill each alveolus (a tiny air sac) and move in and out with every breath. Gases are compressible, which is part of why your chest can change the volume of your lungs to move air (Chapter 10).

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking liquid particles are far apart. They touch; that is why liquids barely compress.
  • Thinking particles in a solid do not move. They vibrate in place.
  • Mixing up "takes the shape of the container" with "fills the container." A liquid takes the shape of the part it occupies; a gas fills all of it.

Summary

  • Solids have a definite shape and volume; liquids have a definite volume only; gases have neither.
  • Solid particles are packed and vibrate in place; liquid particles touch but slide past one another; gas particles are far apart and move fast.
  • Gases compress easily because they are mostly empty space; solids and liquids do not.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A 250 mL sample of a substance is moved from a 500 mL flask to a 1000 mL flask and now fills the whole 1000 mL flask. What state is it in? Explain with the particle view.

Hint

Its volume changed to fill the new container. Which state does that, and what are its particles doing?

Answer

Gas. Its volume changed to fill the new container, which only a gas does; its particles are far apart and move freely until they hit the walls.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Glass holds its shape like a solid, but its particles are not arranged in a repeating pattern. What kind of solid is glass?

Hint

A solid with a repeating pattern is crystalline. What is a solid with no repeating pattern called?

Answer

An amorphous solid (its particles are disordered, unlike a crystalline solid).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A student spills 75.0 mL of water from a beaker onto a lab bench. It spreads into a thin puddle. Has its shape changed? Has its volume changed? Explain with the particle view.

Hint

Treat shape and volume separately. Liquid particles touch but slide past each other; can they be pushed closer together?

Answer

The shape changed (from the beaker's shape to the puddle's), but the volume did not: it is still 75.0 mL. Liquid particles touch one another, so they cannot be pushed closer or spread farther apart, but they slide past each other, so the liquid takes the shape of whatever holds it.
