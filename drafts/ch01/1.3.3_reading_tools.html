Learning Objectives

  • Use the tick-mark rule to decide how many decimal places a reading should have.
  • Read a ruler, a graduated cylinder (at the bottom of the meniscus), a buret, and a thermometer to one estimated digit.
  • Record every digit a digital balance shows, including trailing zeros.
  • Explain why the estimated digit is a significant figure, and write every reading with a unit.

Every significant figure you counted in Section 1.3.1 started at a measuring tool: a ruler, a graduated cylinder, a thermometer, or a balance. The tool decides how many digits you are allowed to report. This section shows how to read each common tool in the CHEM 3A lab, so that the number you write down says exactly how carefully it was measured, no more and no less. These readings come up in the first lab sessions, on exams as pictures of glassware, and at the stations of the lab practical at the end of the term.

The Tick-Mark Rule

The lines printed on a ruler or a piece of glassware are called graduations, or simply tick marks. They tell you how precise the tool is. The digits you can read directly from the tick marks are certain. Between two marks, you estimate one more digit. The rule is short:

Always estimate one more digit beyond the size of the smallest tick mark.

If the marks are 1 mL apart, you can be certain of the ones place and you estimate the tenths. If the marks are 0.1 cm apart, you are certain of the tenths and you estimate the hundredths. The estimated digit is a judgment call, and that is fine: you are the scientist, and a classmate reading the same cylinder might write 42.6 mL where you wrote 42.5 mL. Both readings are honest, and both have three significant figures. The estimated digit is uncertain, but it is still significant, because you took the time to measure it.

Rulers

Picture the edge of a book lying along a centimeter ruler. Zoom in between the 13 and 14 cm marks and you will see ten smaller marks, each 0.1 cm apart. If the edge of the book is past the 13.5 mark but not yet at 13.6, then "13.5" is certain. Should you stop there and call it 13.5 cm? No. The edge sits a little past the mark, and you are allowed to say how far: about 13.52 or 13.53 cm. A ruler marked every 0.1 cm always gives two decimal places.

If the edge lands exactly on a mark, the estimated digit is a zero, and you must write it: an edge exactly on the 13.5 mark is 13.50 cm. Dropping that zero would tell the reader you measured with a ruler ten times less precise.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Read a ruler

Problem. A centimeter ruler has marks every 0.1 cm. The end of a test strip lies between the 7.6 cm and 7.7 cm marks, a little less than halfway to 7.7. Record the length.

Solution. The marks are 0.1 cm apart, so the tenths place is certain (7.6) and you estimate the hundredths. A little less than halfway is about 4 tenths of the gap: 7.64 cm.

Check. The reading has two decimal places, one more than the 0.1 cm marks. Readings of 7.63 or 7.65 cm would also be acceptable; 7.6 cm (too few digits) and 7.640 cm (a digit nobody could see) would not.

Graduated Cylinders, Burets, and the Meniscus

Water and most water solutions curve upward where they touch glass, so the surface in a narrow cylinder is shaped like a shallow U. This curved surface is the meniscus. Read the volume at the bottom of the meniscus, with your eye level with the liquid; looking down from above or up from below shifts the reading. Then apply the tick-mark rule. A 100 mL cylinder with 1 mL marks gives one decimal place. A 10 mL cylinder marked every 0.1 mL gives two.

A buret (the long tube used in titrations) is numbered from the top down, because it measures how much liquid has been delivered. Its marks are usually 0.1 mL apart, so buret readings have two decimal places. Read it at the bottom of the meniscus, just like a cylinder, but remember the numbers increase as you look down.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Read a small graduated cylinder

Problem. A 10 mL graduated cylinder is marked every 0.1 mL. The bottom of the meniscus sits halfway between the 6.3 mL and 6.4 mL marks. Record the volume. How many significant figures does it have?

Solution. The marks make 6.3 certain; halfway to the next mark is an estimated 5 in the hundredths place. The volume is 6.35 mL, with 3 significant figures.

Check. Two decimal places, one beyond the 0.1 mL marks. If the same liquid were poured into a 100 mL cylinder with 1 mL marks, you could only record about 6.4 mL (one decimal place), which shows why chemists choose the smallest cylinder that holds the volume.

Thermometers

A lab thermometer is read the same way: find the top of the liquid column and apply the tick-mark rule. A thermometer marked every 1 °C gives one decimal place. If the liquid is exactly halfway between the 25 °C and 26 °C marks, the reading is 25.5 °C; if it is exactly on the 22 °C mark, the reading is 22.0 °C, not 22 °C.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Read a thermometer

Problem. A thermometer is marked every 1 °C. The top of the liquid is about one-third of the way from the 21 °C mark to the 22 °C mark. Record the temperature.

Solution. 21 is certain; one-third of a degree is about 0.3. The temperature is 21.3 °C.

Check. One decimal place for marks 1 °C apart. Writing "21 °C" would throw away the estimated digit; writing "21.33 °C" would claim a digit no one can read.

Digital Balances

A digital balance has no tick marks to estimate between: the last digit on the display is already its estimated digit. So the rule changes to record every digit the balance shows, no more and no fewer. If an analytical balance reads 2.0500 g, write 2.0500 g (5 significant figures). The trailing zeros are significant, because the balance measured them. Writing 2.05 g would throw away two digits of precision you paid for. Before weighing, tare (zero) the balance with the empty container on it, so the display shows only the mass of the sample.

Professor Tip

At every measurement station, in lab or on the lab practical, write down the number, the unit, and the right number of digits, and show any calculation. A reading without a unit is not a measurement. Much of the credit at a station comes from showing that you read the tool correctly, even before any math. And if your reading does not match someone else's in the last digit, that is normal: the estimated digit is yours to judge.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

In the clinic, liquid medications are measured with oral syringes, medicine cups, and syringes for injection, each printed with its own graduations. Because the precision of a tool depends on the spacing of its marks, a small syringe with marks every 0.1 mL measures a small volume far more precisely than a 30 mL medicine cup marked every 5 mL. Liquid in a cup is read at the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, exactly as in the chemistry lab. (Context only; in practice, always follow the order, the device instructions, and facility policy.)

Common Mistakes

  • Stopping at the last tick mark. Always add one estimated digit, even when it is a zero (13.50 cm, 22.0 °C).
  • Estimating two digits. Only one digit past the smallest mark is allowed.
  • Reading the top edge of the meniscus, or reading from above or below eye level. Read the bottom of the curve, at eye level.
  • Reading a buret upside down. Its numbers increase going down.
  • Rounding a balance reading. Record every digit shown, including trailing zeros.
  • Leaving off the unit. "42.6" is not a measurement; "42.6 mL" is.

Summary

  • Read every certain digit from the tick marks, then estimate one more digit beyond the smallest mark. The estimated digit is significant.
  • Marks every 1 unit give one decimal place; marks every 0.1 unit give two.
  • Read liquids at the bottom of the meniscus, at eye level. Burets are numbered from the top down.
  • For a digital balance, record every digit shown. Always write the unit.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A buret is marked every 0.1 mL. The bottom of the meniscus is between the 18.3 mL and 18.4 mL marks, about seven-tenths of the way down to 18.4. Record the reading.

Hint

Record every certain digit plus one estimated digit. With 0.1 mL marks, which place do you estimate?

Answer

18.37 mL (18.3 is certain; the 7 in the hundredths place is estimated). Readings of 18.36 or 18.38 mL are also reasonable.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

An analytical balance displays 5.200 g. A student records "5.2 g." What is wrong, and how many significant figures should the recorded mass have?

Hint

A digital balance measured every digit it shows. Are the trailing zeros in 5.200 g significant?

Answer

The balance measured the two trailing zeros, so they are significant. The student threw away two digits of precision. Record 5.200 g, which has 4 significant figures.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A 100 mL graduated cylinder is marked every 1 mL. Which reading could have come from it: 36 mL, 36.4 mL, or 36.42 mL? Explain.

Hint

With 1 mL marks, you are certain of the ones place and estimate one more digit. Which reading has exactly one estimated digit?

Answer

36.4 mL. With 1 mL marks you are certain of the ones place and estimate the tenths. 36 mL leaves out the estimated digit; 36.42 mL claims a second estimated digit that the marks cannot support.
