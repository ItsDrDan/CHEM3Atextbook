Learning Objectives

  • Practice every measurement skill in this chapter: scientific notation, units and prefixes, significant figures, reading tools, conversions, and density as a conversion factor.
  • Apply those skills to the kinds of numbers you will see in health care: lab values, doses, weights, and fluid volumes.

These exercises are grouped by section so you can practice one skill at a time, then mix them. Work each problem on paper before you open the answer. Every answer shows the setup, and every multiple-choice answer explains why each wrong choice is wrong, because the wrong choices are the mistakes students most often make. For a mixed set with more conceptual questions, see 1.11 Practice Problems.

Clinical Connection: Why the Last Digit Matters

A lab report that lists potassium as 4.1 mmol/L, hemoglobin as 13.5 g/dL, and white blood cells as 7.5 × 103/µL is making a promise about precision: each value is reliable to the last digit shown, and no further. Lab analyzers usually compute more digits than they report, and the lab rounds on purpose so that the result never claims more precision than the method has. The same honesty is what significant figures ask of you in this chapter. When you work these problems, treat every digit as a claim you would have to defend. (Context only; reference ranges and reporting rules depend on the lab.)

Scientific Notation (Section 1.2.1)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write each number in scientific notation, keeping its significant figures: (a) 0.000450 g; (b) 6,020,000 cells; (c) 0.0302 m; (d) 93,000,000 mi.

Hint

Move the decimal point to get a coefficient from 1 to 10. Keep only significant digits: zeros after a decimal point count, trailing zeros with no decimal point do not.

Answer

(a) 4.50 × 10−4 g (the trailing zero after the decimal point is significant, so keep it). (b) 6.02 × 106 cells (trailing zeros without a decimal point are not significant). (c) 3.02 × 10−2 m (the sandwiched zero counts). (d) 9.3 × 107 mi. Small numbers get negative exponents; large numbers get positive ones.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write each in standard notation: (a) a white blood cell count of 7.5 × 103 cells/µL; (b) 2.8 × 10−3 g; (c) 1.050 × 105 m. For (c), what is lost when you write it in standard notation?

Hint

A positive exponent moves the decimal point right, a negative one left. For (c), count the significant figures before and after, using this book's trailing-zero rule.

Answer

(a) 7,500 cells/µL. (b) 0.0028 g (a negative exponent moves the decimal point to the left). (c) 105,000 m. In standard notation the last two zeros are not significant under this book's rule, so 105,000 shows only 3 significant figures, while 1.050 × 105 shows 4. Scientific notation is the only clear way to show that the zero after the 5 was measured.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which is the correct scientific notation for 0.0007203 m?

A. 7.203 × 10−4 m
B. 7.203 × 104 m
C. 72.03 × 10−5 m
D. 7.2 × 10−4 m

Hint

The coefficient must be from 1 to 10, the exponent is negative for numbers less than 1, and all significant figures must stay.

Answer

A. Move the decimal point 4 places to the right to get 7.203; a number smaller than 1 gets a negative exponent. All 4 significant figures (7, 2, 0, 3) stay.

  • B: the exponent has the wrong sign; 7.203 × 104 is 72,030, a large number.
  • C: has the right value, but the first part (72.03) is not between 1 and 10, so it is not proper scientific notation.
  • D: drops the significant figures 0 and 3. The sandwiched zero counts, so the number has 4 significant figures, not 2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Use your calculator and give each answer in scientific notation with the correct significant figures: (a) (6.0221 × 1023) × (2.50 × 10−3); (b) (4.8 × 10−5) ÷ (1.6 × 10−2).

Hint

Enter each number with the calculator's ×10n key. Then count significant figures: the value with the fewest decides the answer.

Answer

(a) 1.5055 × 1021 → 1.51 × 1021 (3 significant figures, from 2.50). (b) 3.0 × 10−3 (2 significant figures; the calculator shows 0.003, so add the significant zero). On the TI-36X Pro, enter each number with the ×10n key, not as "× 10 ^," to avoid order-of-operations errors.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

A urine culture grows 1.2 × 105 colony-forming units (CFU) of bacteria per milliliter. How many CFU are in 0.50 mL of that urine?

Hint

Start with the 0.50 mL that stands alone and use the count per milliliter as a conversion factor, with mL on the bottom.

Answer

\( 0.50\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{1.2 \times 10^{5}\ \text{CFU}}{1\ \text{mL}} = \) 6.0 × 104 CFU (2 significant figures). Microbiology labs report counts in scientific notation because the numbers are so large.

Units and Metric Prefixes (Section 1.2.2)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

Give the power of ten each prefix stands for: (a) kilo- (k); (b) centi- (c); (c) milli- (m); (d) micro- (µ, written "mcg" for micrograms in health care); (e) nano- (n).

Hint

Use the yellow sheet's prefix table. Positive powers are for prefixes bigger than the base unit, negative for smaller ones.

Answer

(a) 103; (b) 10−2; (c) 10−3; (d) 10−6; (e) 10−9. So 1 kg = 1000 g, 100 cm = 1 m, 1000 mg = 1 g, 106 mcg = 1 g, and 109 nm = 1 m.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{7}\)

Which mass is the largest?

A. 0.025 kg
B. 3.0 × 104 mg
C. 2.5 × 107 µg
D. 2.0 × 10−5 Mg

Hint

Convert every choice to grams before comparing. Remember the capital M in Mg means mega-, not milli-.

Answer

B. Convert each to grams: 3.0 × 104 mg × (1 g / 1000 mg) = 30. g, the largest.

  • A: 0.025 kg × (1000 g / 1 kg) = 25 g.
  • C: 2.5 × 107 µg × (1 g / 106 µg) = 25 g; a big exponent on a tiny unit does not mean a big mass.
  • D: 2.0 × 10−5 Mg × (106 g / 1 Mg) = 20. g; a megagram is huge, but the number in front is tiny.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{8}\)

Choose a sensible metric unit for each: (a) the mass of a newborn; (b) the amount of vitamin B12 in one tablet; (c) the diameter of a red blood cell; (d) the volume of an IV bag.

Hint

Think about the size of each thing: pick the prefix that gives a number that is not too large or too small (a newborn is a few of which unit?).

Answer

(a) kg (newborns are about 3 to 4 kg). (b) mcg (micrograms; vitamin B12 doses on labels are written in micrograms). (c) µm (micrometers; a red blood cell is about 7 to 8 µm across). (d) mL or L (common bags hold 250 mL to 1 L). A good unit keeps the number between about 0.1 and 1000.

Significant Figures, Accuracy, and Precision (Section 1.3.1)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{9}\)

How many significant figures are in each? (a) 0.00720 g; (b) 4005 mL; (c) 1200 mg; (d) 1200. mg; (e) 6.020 × 10−3 L; (f) 12 patients in a ward.

Hint

Leading zeros never count, zeros between nonzero digits always count, and trailing zeros count only with a decimal point. Counted numbers are exact.

Answer

(a) 3: leading zeros are placeholders; the trailing zero after the decimal point counts. (b) 4: sandwiched zeros count. (c) 2: trailing zeros with no decimal point do not count. (d) 4: the decimal point makes the trailing zeros significant. (e) 4: in scientific notation, every digit in the first part counts. (f) Exact (unlimited): 12 patients is a count, not a measurement.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{10}\)

A clinic scale gives readings of 72.4 kg, 72.5 kg, and 72.4 kg for a patient. On a newly calibrated scale, the same patient weighs 70.1 kg. The clinic scale is:

A. precise but not accurate
B. accurate but not precise
C. both accurate and precise
D. neither accurate nor precise

Hint

Precision is how close the readings are to each other; accuracy is how close they are to the true value. Compare the clinic readings with the calibrated scale.

Answer

A. The three readings agree closely with each other (precise), but all are about 2.3 kg too high (not accurate). This is the pattern of a systematic error, such as a scale that is out of calibration; it can be fixed by recalibrating.

  • B: accurate means close to the true value; 72.4 kg is not close to 70.1 kg.
  • C: the readings are precise, but precision alone does not make them correct.
  • D: the readings are within 0.1 kg of each other, so they are precise.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{11}\)

In lab, a student correctly records a volume as "5.0 mL." Later, on a practice medication order, the student writes "5.0 mg" and ".5 mg." Explain why the lab entry is correct but both order entries should be rewritten.

Hint

Lab notebooks show precision with trailing zeros. Medication orders follow the "Do Not Use" list: why might a trailing zero or a missing leading zero be misread?

Answer

In a lab notebook, the trailing zero in 5.0 mL tells the reader the volume was measured to the tenths place, which is good significant-figure practice. On medication orders, The Joint Commission's "Do Not Use" list forbids trailing zeros ("5.0 mg" can be misread as 50 mg if the decimal point is missed) and requires a leading zero (".5 mg" can be misread as 5 mg). The order should read 5 mg and 0.5 mg. Lab reports and medication orders follow different rules because they guard against different risks: hiding precision in one, a tenfold dosing error in the other.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{12}\)

A lab reports serum potassium as 4.1 mmol/L. How many significant figures does it have, and what does the report claim about the uncertainty? Why would the lab not report 4.137 mmol/L, even if the analyzer displayed it?

Hint

Count significant figures in 4.1; the last digit is the uncertain one. Would extra digits from the analyzer be reliable?

Answer

2 significant figures; the value is reliable to the tenths place (uncertain by about ±0.1 mmol/L). Reporting 4.137 would claim precision to the thousandths place that the method cannot deliver, which would make small, meaningless changes from day to day look like real changes in the patient.

Significant Figures in Calculations (Section 1.3.2)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{13}\)

Calculate and round correctly: (a) 12.45 mL + 3.1 mL + 0.567 mL; (b) 4.50 cm × 0.0320 cm; (c) 98.6 °F − 97.92 °F; (d) 2.0 g ÷ 3.000 mL.

Hint

Addition and subtraction: fewest decimal places. Multiplication and division: fewest significant figures. Multiply the units too.

Answer

(a) 16.117 → 16.1 mL (addition: fewest decimal places, tenths from 3.1). (b) 0.144 → 0.144 cm2 (multiplication: fewest significant figures, 3; the units multiply too). (c) 0.68 → 0.7 °F (subtraction: tenths, from 98.6). (d) 0.6667 → 0.67 g/mL (division: 2 significant figures, from 2.0).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{14}\)

Round 6,451.7 to 2 significant figures.

A. 6.5 × 103
B. 650
C. 6.4 × 103
D. 6,450

Hint

Underline the second digit and look at the digit to its right. Then keep the number's size: the answer must still be about six thousand.

Answer

A. Underline the second digit (6,451.7); the digit to its right is 5, so round the 4 up to 5. Then keep the number's size with placeholder zeros or scientific notation: 6,500 or 6.5 × 103.

  • B: drops a placeholder zero; 650 is ten times too small. If someone owed you $6,500 and paid $650, you would notice.
  • C: cuts off (truncates) instead of rounding; the 5 after the underlined digit means round up.
  • D: keeps 3 significant figures, not 2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{15}\)

Calculate (25.62 − 3.4) × 1.205 ÷ 2.50 to the correct number of significant figures.

Hint

Parentheses first: subtract and underline the last significant digit (decimal-place rule). Keep all digits, then multiply and divide using the significant-figure rule. Round only at the end.

Answer

Work the parentheses first: 25.62 − 3.4 = 22.22. The subtraction is good to the tenths place, so underline the last significant digit: 22.22 (3 significant figures). Keep all the digits and continue: 22.22 × 1.205 ÷ 2.50 = 10.71. The fewest significant figures in the multiplication and division steps is 3 (22.2 and 2.50), so the answer is 10.7. Round only at the end.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{16}\)

A pressure ulcer measures 2.5 cm by 1.25 cm. Estimate its area (length × width) with the correct units and significant figures.

Hint

Area = length × width; cm × cm gives cm2. Which measurement has the fewest significant figures?

Answer

2.5 cm × 1.25 cm = 3.125 cm2 → 3.1 cm2 (2 significant figures, from 2.5 cm). Multiplying cm by cm gives cm2.

Reading Measuring Tools (Section 1.3.3)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{17}\)

(a) A glass thermometer is marked every 1 °C. The liquid ends about four-tenths of the way from 37 to 38. Record the reading. (b) A graduated cylinder is marked every 1 mL. The bottom of the meniscus sits about three-tenths of the way from 23 to 24. Record the volume.

Hint

Estimate one digit past the smallest marking. With 1 unit marks, the ones place is certain and the tenths place is estimated.

Answer

Estimate one digit past the smallest marking. (a) 37.4 °C (3 significant figures; the 4 is estimated). (b) 23.3 mL, read at the bottom of the meniscus. Writing 37 °C or 23 mL would throw away information you measured.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{18}\)

A digital analytical balance displays 2.0500 g. What should you record?

A. 2.0500 g
B. 2.05 g
C. 2.050 g
D. 2 g

Hint

Are the trailing zeros on a digital display measured digits or placeholders? Count the significant figures each choice keeps.

Answer

A. Record every digit a digital balance shows, including trailing zeros; they are measured, so 2.0500 g has 5 significant figures.

  • B: drops two measured zeros and makes the balance look 100 times less precise than it is.
  • C: drops one measured zero.
  • D: throws away almost all of the measurement.

Unit Conversions (Section 1.4.1)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{19}\)

A newborn weighs 7.25 lb. What is the weight in kilograms? (1 kg = 2.2046 lb)

Hint

Use 1 kg = 2.2046 lb with pounds on the bottom. Size check: a kilogram is heavier than a pound, so the number gets smaller.

Answer

\( 7.25\ \text{lb} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{2.2046\ \text{lb}} = 3.289\ \text{kg} \to \) 3.29 kg (3 significant figures). Size check: the number of kilograms is less than half the number of pounds.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{20}\)

(Math practice only.) An order is written as 0.250 mg. How many micrograms (mcg) is this?

A. 2.50 × 102 mcg
B. 2.50 × 10−4 mcg
C. 25.0 mcg
D. 2.50 × 105 mcg

Hint

Go through the base unit: mg → g → mcg. A smaller unit gives a bigger number.

Answer

A. \( 0.250\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{mg}} \times \dfrac{10^{6}\ \text{mcg}}{1\ \text{g}} = 250.\ \text{mcg} = 2.50 \times 10^{2}\ \text{mcg} \). A smaller unit gives a bigger number.

  • B: divides by 1000 instead of multiplying; the factor 1000 mcg = 1 mg was used upside down, and the number got smaller even though the unit got smaller.
  • C: uses 100 mcg = 1 mg, a made-up factor. Go through the base unit and use factors from the sheet.
  • D: multiplies by 106 as if the order were in grams; it skips the mg → g step and is 1000 times too large.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{21}\)

A cup of yogurt is labeled 6.0 oz. What is its mass in grams? (1 g = 0.03527 oz)

Hint

Use 1 g = 0.03527 oz with ounces on the bottom so they cancel. A gram is much smaller than an ounce, so the number of grams must be larger.

Answer

\( 6.0\ \text{oz} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{0.03527\ \text{oz}} = 170.1\ \text{g} \to \) 1.7 × 102 g (2 significant figures, from 6.0 oz; "170 g" also shows 2 under this book's rule).

Multi-step Conversions (Section 1.4.2)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{22}\)

Convert 4.5 × 10−3 kg to milligrams.

Hint

Go through the base unit, grams: kg → g → mg. Each step uses a factor of 1000.

Answer

\( 4.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} = \) 4.5 × 103 mg (through the base unit, grams).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{23}\)

A resting heart rate is 72 beats/min. About how many times does the heart beat in one day?

Hint

Start with the rate and cancel minutes, then hours, one factor at a time. A count of 72 beats limits the significant figures.

Answer

\( \dfrac{72\ \text{beats}}{1\ \text{min}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{min}}{1\ \text{h}} \times \dfrac{24\ \text{h}}{1\ \text{day}} = 103{,}680 \to \) 1.0 × 105 beats/day (2 significant figures, from 72; the time factors are exact).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{24}\)

A blood glucose result is 110 mg/dL. Express it in g/L. (10 dL = 1 L)

Hint

Two units change: mg on top becomes g, and dL on the bottom becomes L. Use one factor for each, arranged so the old unit cancels.

Answer

\( \dfrac{110\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{dL}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{mg}} \times \dfrac{10\ \text{dL}}{1\ \text{L}} = \) 1.1 g/L (110 has 2 significant figures under this book's rule).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{25}\)

The speed limit on a stretch of Highway 99 is 65 mi/h. What is this in m/s? (1 mi = 1.6093 km)

Hint

Convert distance first (mi → km → m), then time (h → min → s). Hours are on the bottom, so put hours on top in the factor.

Answer

\( \dfrac{65\ \text{mi}}{1\ \text{h}} \times \dfrac{1.6093\ \text{km}}{1\ \text{mi}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{60\ \text{min}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{min}}{60\ \text{s}} = 29.06 \to \) 29 m/s (2 significant figures). Distance first, then time.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{26}\)

(Math practice only.) An IV runs continuously at 125 mL/h. How many liters does it deliver per day?

A. 3.00 L/day
B. 3.00 × 103 L/day
C. 5.21 × 10−3 L/day
D. 0.125 L/day

Hint

Use 24 h = 1 day to cancel hours, then 1000 mL = 1 L to cancel milliliters. Which choice is in a sensible range for a daily IV volume?

Answer

A. \( \dfrac{125\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{h}} \times \dfrac{24\ \text{h}}{1\ \text{day}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \text{mL}} = 3.00\ \text{L/day} \) (3 significant figures; 24 h = 1 day is exact).

  • B: 3000 is the number of milliliters per day; the mL → L step was skipped.
  • C: divides by 24 instead of multiplying; "per hour" on the bottom needs 24 h on the top of the next factor to cancel.
  • D: converts mL to L but never converts hours to days, so it is still the volume per hour.

Units Raised to a Power (Section 1.4.3)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{27}\)

Convert 4.0 × 104 cm2 to m2.

A. 4.0 m2
B. 4.0 × 102 m2
C. 4.0 × 108 m2
D. 0.040 m2

Hint

The unit is squared, so square the factor (1 m/100 cm). One square meter is 100 cm by 100 cm.

Answer

A. \( 4.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{2} \times \left(\dfrac{1\ \text{m}}{100\ \text{cm}}\right)^{2} = 4.0 \times 10^{4} \div 10^{4} = 4.0\ \text{m}^{2} \).

  • B: divides by 100, forgetting to square the factor.
  • C: multiplies by 104; the factor is upside down, and a bigger unit must give a smaller number.
  • D: divides by 106, cubing a factor that should be squared (this is an area, not a volume).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{28}\)

A patient room is 12.0 ft long, 10.0 ft wide, and 8.00 ft high. What is its volume in m3? (1 ft = 30.48 cm, exact)

Hint

Find the volume in ft3 first. Then cube each length factor: (30.48 cm/1 ft)3 and (1 m/100 cm)3.

Answer

V = 12.0 × 10.0 × 8.00 = 960. ft3. \( 960.\ \text{ft}^{3} \times \left(\dfrac{30.48\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{ft}}\right)^{3} \times \left(\dfrac{1\ \text{m}}{100\ \text{cm}}\right)^{3} = 27.18\ \text{m}^{3} \to \) 27.2 m3 (3 significant figures).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{29}\)

A bag of IV fluid holds 0.75 L. Express its volume in cm3 and in cc.

Hint

Convert L to mL first, then use 1 mL = 1 cm3 = 1 cc.

Answer

\( 0.75\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{cm}^{3}}{1\ \text{L}} = \) 7.5 × 102 cm3 = 7.5 × 102 cc (750 mL; 1 mL = 1 cm3 = 1 cc). In charting, write mL.

Word Problems and Dosage Calculations (Section 1.4.4)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{30}\)

(Math practice only; not dosing guidance.) A child weighs 55.0 lb. A medication is ordered at 10. mg per kg of body weight per dose. The oral liquid contains 100. mg per 5 mL (5 mL is a defined label volume). What volume is one dose? (1 kg = 2.2046 lb)

Hint

Start with the stand-alone body weight: lb → kg, then mg drug per kg, then the label (mL per mg) with mg on the bottom. Label body and drug.

Answer

\( 55.0\ \text{lb body} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg body}}{2.2046\ \text{lb body}} \times \dfrac{10.\ \text{mg drug}}{1\ \text{kg body}} \times \dfrac{5\ \text{mL}}{100.\ \text{mg drug}} = 12.47\ \text{mL} \to \) 12 mL (2 significant figures, from 10. mg/kg). Check: 55 lb is about 25 kg; 25 × 10 = 250 mg; 250 mg is 2.5 times 100 mg, so 2.5 × 5 mL = 12.5 mL.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{31}\)

A manufacturer has 2.0 kg of a drug. How many 500. mg tablets can it make?

Hint

Go through the base unit: kg → g → mg, then use "1 tablet per 500. mg" with mg on the bottom.

Answer

\( 2.0\ \text{kg} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{tablet}}{500.\ \text{mg}} = \) 4.0 × 103 tablets (2 significant figures, from 2.0 kg).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{32}\)

A nursing student drives from Fresno to a clinical site in Merced, 65 mi away, on a foggy morning at an average of 45 mi/h. How many minutes does the drive take?

A. 87 min
B. 1.4 min
C. 42 min
D. 2.9 × 103 min

Hint

Start with the term that stands alone (the distance), use the speed with miles on the bottom, then convert hours to minutes. The fog is a distractor.

Answer

A. Start with the term that stands alone (65 mi): \( 65\ \text{mi} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{45\ \text{mi}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{min}}{1\ \text{h}} = 86.7 \to 87\ \text{min} \) (2 significant figures). The city names and the fog are distractors.

  • B: 1.4 is the time in hours; the h → min step was skipped.
  • C: uses the speed upside down (45 mi/h × 60 ÷ 65); miles do not cancel.
  • D: multiplies 65 mi by 45 mi/h, which gives mi2/h, not a time.

Percents as Conversion Factors (Section 1.4.5)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{33}\)

Normal saline is 0.90% NaCl, meaning 0.90 g of NaCl per 100 mL of solution. How many grams of NaCl are in a 1.000 L bag?

Hint

Convert 1.000 L to mL first, then use 0.90 g NaCl per 100 mL with mL on the bottom.

Answer

\( 1.000\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{L}} \times \dfrac{0.90\ \text{g NaCl}}{100\ \text{mL}} = \) 9.0 g NaCl (2 significant figures, from 0.90%).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{34}\)

A patient's hematocrit is 42%, meaning red blood cells make up 42% of the blood volume. If the patient has 5.0 L of blood, what volume is red blood cells?

Hint

Write the percent as a conversion factor: 42 L RBC per 100 L blood. Size check: a part is always smaller than the total.

Answer

\( 5.0\ \text{L blood} \times \dfrac{42\ \text{L RBC}}{100\ \text{L blood}} = \) 2.1 L of red blood cells (2 significant figures). Check: a part is always smaller than the total.

Density as a Conversion Factor (Section 1.9.1)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{35}\)

A cough syrup has a density of 1.31 g/mL. What is the mass of 60.0 mL of it?

Hint

Start with the volume that stands alone and use the density with mL on the bottom so mL cancels.

Answer

\( 60.0\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{1.31\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mL}} = \) 78.6 g (3 significant figures). A syrup is denser than water, so 60 mL weighs more than 60 g.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{36}\)

What volume of glycerol (d = 1.26 g/mL) has a mass of 75.6 g?

A. 60.0 mL
B. 95.3 mL
C. 0.0600 mL
D. 0.0167 mL

Hint

Start with the mass and flip the density so grams are on the bottom. A liquid denser than water has fewer mL than g.

Answer

A. \( 75.6\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mL}}{1.26\ \text{g}} = 60.0\ \text{mL} \) (3 significant figures). A liquid denser than water has fewer mL than g.

  • B: multiplies by the density (75.6 × 1.26); grams do not cancel, and the volume came out larger than the mass, which is impossible for a liquid denser than water.
  • C: 0.0600 is the volume in liters, labeled as mL.
  • D: divides the density by the mass (1.26 ÷ 75.6); the units would be 1/mL, not mL.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{37}\)

A patient produces 1.50 L of urine in a day, with a specific gravity of 1.015 (so a density of 1.015 g/mL). What is its mass in kilograms?

Hint

Chain the steps: L → mL, then the density (mL on the bottom) to get grams, then g → kg.

Answer

\( 1.50\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{L}} \times \dfrac{1.015\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \text{g}} = 1.5225\ \text{kg} \to \) 1.52 kg (3 significant figures, from 1.50 L).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{38}\)

After a delivery, soaked pads weigh 410. g; the same pads dry weigh 96 g. (a) Estimate the blood loss in mL with the rule 1 g ≈ 1 mL. (b) Recalculate using a blood density of 1.06 g/mL. (c) Which estimate is larger, and why?

Hint

First find the blood mass: soaked pads minus dry pads. (a) Use 1 g ≈ 1 mL. (b) Use the density flipped, grams on the bottom. (c) Is blood denser than water?

Answer

Blood mass = 410. g − 96 g = 314 g (both masses are known to the ones place, so the difference is too). (a) 314 mL. (b) \( 314\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mL}}{1.06\ \text{g}} = 296.2 \to \) 296 mL. (c) The 1 g ≈ 1 mL rule gives the larger estimate, because blood is slightly denser than water, so each gram of blood is a little less than 1 mL. The difference (about 6%) is small enough that the quick rule is used in practice. (Context only.)
