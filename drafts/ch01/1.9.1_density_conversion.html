Learning Objectives

  • Write a density as a conversion factor between mass and volume, either way up.
  • Use density to find the mass of a measured volume, or the volume of a measured mass.
  • Combine density with metric-prefix factors in a multi-step conversion.
  • Plan a real measurement (for example, measuring out a mass of liquid with only a graduated cylinder).
  • Explain how specific gravity and "1 g ≈ 1 mL" are used in health care.

In Section 1.9 you used density as a formula, d = m/V, to calculate a density and identify a substance. Density has a second, even more useful job. Because it links a mass to a volume, it is a bridge between the two: one of the few conversion factors that lets you go from grams to milliliters and back. In the lab, liquids are easier to measure by volume, but recipes, reactions, and medication strengths are often written by mass. Density lets you cross from one to the other.

Density Is a Conversion Factor

A density of 0.789 g/mL for ethanol says that 0.789 g of ethanol takes up 1 mL. Like every equality, it can be written either way up:

\( {0.789\;\text{g ethanol}} = {1\;\text{mL ethanol}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{0.789\;\text{g}}{1\;\text{mL}} \quad \text{or} \quad \dfrac{1\;\text{mL}}{0.789\;\text{g}} \)

The slash in "g/mL" is a fraction bar, so any time a word problem gives you a quantity with a slash, you are looking at a conversion factor. Use the version that cancels the unit you have:

  • Volume → mass: multiply by \( \tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{mL}} \) (mL cancels).
  • Mass → volume: multiply by \( \tfrac{\text{mL}}{\text{g}} \) (g cancels).

Two more things to remember. A density belongs to one substance at one temperature, so label it (g ethanol, mL ethanol) and use the value for the right substance. And a density is a measured value, so its digits count when you round the answer, unlike the exact factor 1000 mL = 1 L.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Volume to mass

Problem. A beaker holds 230. mL of methanol (d = 0.792 g/mL). What is the mass of the methanol?

Given: 230. mL methanol (stands alone); 0.792 g / 1 mL. Desired: ??? g.

Solution. Milliliters are on top in the given, so the density goes in with mL on the bottom:

\({230.\;\text{mL}}\)\({0.792\;\text{g}}\)
 \({1\;\text{mL}}\)

\(=\; {182.16\;\text{g}} \to {182\;\text{g}} \)

Both the volume and the density have 3 significant figures: 182 g.

Check. Methanol is a little less dense than water, so 230 mL of it should weigh a little less than 230 g, and 182 g does. If you had a beaker of unknown liquid weighing 182 g in 230 mL, this is how you could confirm it is methanol.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Mass to volume, with a prefix

Problem. What volume, in liters, does 100. g of air occupy at room conditions? The density of air is 0.001184 g/mL.

Given: 100. g air (stands alone); 0.001184 g / 1 mL. Desired: ??? L. Strategy: g → mL (density, flipped) → L.

Solution. Grams are on top, so use the density with grams on the bottom:

\( {100.\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mL}}{0.001184\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{L}}{1000\;\text{mL}} = {84.46\;\text{L}} \to {84.5\;\text{L}} \)

The mass (100., with its decimal point) has 3 significant figures and the density has 4, so the answer has 3: 84.5 L.

Check. Air is about a thousand times less dense than water, so 100 g of air should fill about a thousand times more space than 100 mL: roughly 100 L. That is about the volume of a large suitcase. Note that "100 g" without the decimal point would have only 1 significant figure (Section 1.3).

Professor Tip

When a problem gives a mass or a volume and a density, the mass or volume is the term that stands alone; start with it, never with the density. Then write the density both ways up next to your work and pick the one that cancels. If your answer comes out in g2/mL or mL2/g, the density went in upside down.

Planning a Real Measurement

Chemists often need a certain mass of a liquid, but pouring liquids onto a balance is messy, and a large amount may be more than the balance can hold. The solution: use density to find the volume, and measure the volume instead.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Measure out 5.0 kg of ethanol

Problem. A lab needs 5.0 kg of ethanol (d = 0.789 g/mL), but the only measuring tool available is a 1000-mL graduated cylinder. Work out a strategy for measuring the ethanol.

Given: 5.0 kg ethanol (stands alone); 0.789 g / 1 mL; a 1000-mL cylinder. Desired: ??? mL, and a plan. Strategy: kg → g → mL.

Solution. The density is in grams, so go from kilograms to grams first:

\( {5.0\;\text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1000\;\text{g}}{1\;\text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mL}}{0.789\;\text{g}} = {6337\;\text{mL}} \to {6.3 \times 10^{3}\;\text{mL}} \)

Reported to the 2 significant figures of 5.0 kg, the volume is 6.3 × 103 mL, or 6.3 L. The plan: fill the 1000-mL cylinder to the mark six times (6000 mL), then measure about 340 mL more (6337 − 6000, using the unrounded value for planning) for a seventh, partial fill.

Check. Ethanol is less dense than water, so 5.0 kg of it must take up more than the 5.0 L that 5.0 kg of water would, and 6.3 L does. Ethanol is flammable: measure it in a fume hood, away from flames and hot plates.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Glycerin in a pharmacy

Problem. A compounding recipe calls for 250. g of glycerin (glycerol, d = 1.26 g/mL), a thick, sweet liquid used in many oral liquids and skin products. Glycerin is so thick that pouring it onto a balance is slow, so the technician will measure it by volume. What volume is needed? (Context only; a math example.)

Given: 250. g glycerin (stands alone); 1.26 g / 1 mL. Desired: ??? mL.

Solution.

\( {250.\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mL}}{1.26\;\text{g}} = {198.4\;\text{mL}} \to {198\;\text{mL}} \)

Three significant figures from both values: 198 mL.

Check. Glycerin is denser than water, so 250 g of it takes up less than 250 mL. A denser liquid always needs a smaller volume for the same mass.

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

An average adult has about 5.0 L of blood, and whole blood has a density of about 1.06 g/mL. How much does all that blood weigh?

\( {5.0\;\text{L}} \times \dfrac{1000\;\text{mL}}{1\;\text{L}} \times \dfrac{1.06\;\text{g}}{1\;\text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{kg}}{1000\;\text{g}} = {5.3\;\text{kg}} \)

About 5.3 kg, or close to 12 lb, which is roughly 7 to 8% of the body mass of a typical adult. Blood is a little denser than water because of its red blood cells and dissolved proteins.

Clinical Connection: Nursing and Lab Technology

Density turns up in patient care more often than you might expect:

  • Urine specific gravity. Specific gravity is a sample's density divided by the density of water, so it has no units. A specific gravity of 1.020 means the urine is 1.020 times as dense as water, because of dissolved substances such as urea and salts. Labs and bedside dipsticks report urine specific gravity (commonly about 1.005 to 1.030) as one clue to how concentrated the urine is and how well hydrated a patient may be. Because water's density is very close to 1.00 g/mL, a specific gravity of 1.020 corresponds to a density of about 1.020 g/mL.
  • Blood loss by weighing. In childbirth and surgery, teams measure blood loss by weighing soaked pads and sponges and subtracting their dry mass. They convert grams to milliliters with the rule 1 g ≈ 1 mL, which treats blood as having the density of water. Example: soaked pads weigh 642 g and the same pads dry weigh 118 g, so they hold 524 g of blood, about 524 mL by the rule. Using blood's density (1.06 g/mL) gives 494 mL; the 1 g = 1 mL rule is a quick, slightly high estimate.
  • Daily weights. A liter of IV fluid or retained body water has a mass of about 1 kg, so a sudden weight gain of 2 kg can mean about 2 L of retained fluid. Nurses weigh some patients every morning, on the same scale, for this reason.

(Context only; follow facility policy.)

Common Mistakes

  • Using the density upside down. Mass → volume needs mL/g; volume → mass needs g/mL. Check that the unit you started with cancels.
  • Starting with the density instead of the given mass or volume.
  • Skipping the prefix step. If the mass is in kg and the density is in g/mL, convert kg → g before using the density.
  • Using the wrong substance's density. Label every unit with its substance.
  • Treating a density as exact. Its digits count when you round.

Summary

  • A density is a conversion factor between mass and volume for one substance: \( \tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{mL}} \) or \( \tfrac{\text{mL}}{\text{g}} \).
  • Start with the given mass or volume, then choose the version of the density that cancels it.
  • Density lets you measure a needed mass of liquid by volume, and the reverse.
  • In health care, specific gravity (density relative to water) and the rule 1 g ≈ 1 mL for body fluids are everyday uses of density.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What is the mass of 25.0 mL of glycerol (d = 1.26 g/mL)?

Hint

Start with the volume that stands alone and use the density with mL on the bottom so mL cancels.

Answer

\( {25.0\;\text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1.26\;\text{g}}{1\;\text{mL}} = \) 31.5 g (3 significant figures).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

What volume of ethanol (d = 0.789 g/mL) has a mass of 150. g?

A. 190. mL
B. 118 mL
C. 0.190 mL
D. 5.26 × 10−3 mL

Hint

Start with the mass and flip the density so grams are on the bottom. A liquid less dense than water has more mL than g.

Answer

A. \( {150.\;\text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mL}}{0.789\;\text{g}} = {190.1\;\text{mL}} \to {190.\;\text{mL}} \) (3 significant figures; the decimal point shows the zero is significant).

  • B: multiplies the mass by the density (g × g/mL), so grams do not cancel; a liquid less dense than water must have more mL than g.
  • C: is the right number of liters (0.190 L) but labeled mL.
  • D: divides the density by the mass (0.789 ÷ 150); the units would be 1/mL, not mL, so the setup cannot be right.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A urine sample of 250. mL has a specific gravity of 1.020. Taking water's density as 1.000 g/mL, what is the mass of the sample?

Hint

Specific gravity times water's density gives the sample's density. Then use it as a factor with mL on the bottom.

Answer

Density = 1.020 × 1.000 g/mL = 1.020 g/mL. \( {250.\;\text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1.020\;\text{g}}{1\;\text{mL}} = \) 255 g (3 significant figures, from 250. mL).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

You need 1.50 kg of glycerol (d = 1.26 g/mL) and have only a 500-mL graduated cylinder. What volume do you need, and how would you measure it?

Hint

Convert kg → g first, then use the density flipped (grams on the bottom). How many times can you fill a 500-mL cylinder?

Answer

\( {1.50\;\text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1000\;\text{g}}{1\;\text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mL}}{1.26\;\text{g}} = {1190.5\;\text{mL}} \to \) 1.19 × 103 mL. Fill the 500-mL cylinder twice (1000 mL), then measure about 190 mL more.
