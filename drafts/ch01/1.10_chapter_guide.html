Learning Objectives

  • Tell qualitative from quantitative observations and put the steps of the scientific method in order.
  • Name the independent variable, dependent variable, and control in an experiment; tell a law from a theory.
  • Write measurements with units and in scientific notation; use SI units and metric prefixes.
  • Count significant figures, round, and report calculated results with the right precision; tell accuracy from precision.
  • Read a ruler, graduated cylinder, buret, thermometer, and digital balance to the right number of digits.
  • Convert units with dimensional analysis, including multi-step conversions and squared or cubed units.
  • Solve word problems and dosage calculations by starting with the term that stands alone, and use percents as conversion factors.
  • Classify matter as an element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture, from a name, a formula, or a particle diagram.
  • Describe solids, liquids, and gases by shape, volume, particle spacing, and particle motion.
  • Predict whether charged particles attract or repel and how charge size and distance change the force.
  • Classify properties and changes as physical or chemical, and properties as intensive or extensive.
  • Calculate density with d = m/V, predict floating, sinking, and layer order, and use density to convert between mass and volume.

Key Ideas

1. Observe, hypothesize, test

Qualitative observations use words; quantitative observations are numbers with units. A hypothesis is a testable, falsifiable explanation. A fair experiment changes one independent variable, measures a dependent variable, keeps everything else constant, and includes a control. A law describes what always happens; a theory explains why. (Section 1.1)

2. Every measurement is a number and a unit

A measurement without a unit is meaningless. Scientific notation (A × 10n, with 1 ≤ A < 10) handles very large and very small numbers. The SI base units include the meter (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K), and mole (mol); prefixes scale them by powers of ten: kilo- (103), centi- (10−2), milli- (10−3), micro- (10−6), nano- (10−9). (Section 1.2)

3. Report only the digits you measured

Significant figures are the certain digits plus one estimated digit. Nonzero digits and zeros between them count; leading zeros never count; trailing zeros count only with a decimal point (2500 has 2, 2500. has 4, 2.500 × 103 has 4). Multiplying or dividing: keep the fewest significant figures. Adding or subtracting: keep the fewest decimal places. When you read a ruler, graduated cylinder, or buret, record every certain digit plus one estimated digit past the smallest marking (marks every 1 mL give one decimal place; marks every 0.1 mL give two), reading liquids at the bottom of the meniscus; when you read a digital balance, record every digit it shows (Section 1.3.3). Exact numbers (counts, defined conversions) do not limit precision. Accuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is closeness of repeated measurements to each other. (Section 1.3)

4. Let the units do the work

A conversion factor is a ratio equal to 1 (for example, 1000 mL / 1 L). Arrange each factor so the unit you want to cancel is on the opposite side, chain as many factors as you need, and check that only the desired unit is left. Any phrase with "per" or a slash (15 mg/kg, 40.0 mg per 5.0 mL, 39.3% sodium = 39.3 g Na per 100 g NaCl) is a conversion factor you can flip either way. For squared or cubed units, square or cube the whole factor: 1 m3 = (100 cm)3 = 106 cm3; 1 cm3 = 1 mL. In a word problem, write the Givens and the Desired, look for giveaway phrases ("per," "in each," "in every," "is/has/makes," a slash), start with the term that stands alone, and label every unit with its substance (mg drug, kg body) so only matching units cancel. A problem with two stand-alone terms is solved in two setups. A percent is "out of 100": 34.0% N in a fertilizer is 34.0 g N / 100 g fertilizer, usable either way up, and a part is always smaller than its total. (Sections 1.4, 1.4.4, and 1.4.5)

5. One kind of particle or more than one?

A pure substance has one kind of particle and a fixed composition: an element (one kind of atom: He, Fe, O2) or a compound (different elements bonded in a fixed ratio: H2O, NaCl). A mixture has two or more substances not bonded together and can vary in composition: homogeneous (uniform; a solution, such as saline or air) or heterogeneous (not uniform, such as whole blood). Seven elements exist as diatomic molecules: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2. Mixtures separate by physical methods; compounds break down only by chemical change. (Section 1.5)

6. Spacing and motion define the state

Solid: definite shape and volume; particles packed, vibrating in place. Liquid: definite volume, takes the container's shape; particles touching but sliding past each other. Gas: fills its container; particles far apart and fast, so gases compress easily. (Section 1.6)

7. Opposites attract

Like charges repel; opposite charges attract. Bigger charges and shorter distances mean stronger forces. Pulling opposite charges apart takes energy; letting them come together releases energy. (Section 1.7)

8. Is it still the same stuff?

Physical properties (color, melting point, density) are observed without changing a substance; chemical properties (flammability, reactivity) describe how it turns into new substances. Physical changes (cutting, dissolving, every change of state) keep the same particles; chemical changes make new substances, often signaled by gas, color change, a precipitate, heat or light, or a new odor. Intensive properties (density, boiling point) do not depend on amount; extensive properties (mass, volume) do. (Section 1.8)

9. Density = mass ÷ volume

d = m/V, in g/mL (= g/cm3) for liquids and solids and g/L for gases. Less dense floats; more dense sinks; liquid layers stack densest at the bottom. Density is intensive, so it helps identify substances, and it is a conversion factor between mass and volume: multiply volume by density to get mass, or divide mass by density to get volume. For an irregular solid, the volume is the rise in water level, Vfinal − Vinitial, not the final reading. Water (1.00 g/mL) is the benchmark: denser things sink in it. (Sections 1.9 and 1.9.1)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Design check

Problem. A dental hygiene student hypothesizes that rinsing with a fluoride mouthwash for 60 seconds leaves more fluoride on enamel than rinsing for 30 seconds. She soaks identical enamel samples in the same rinse for either 30 s or 60 s and measures the fluoride on each sample. (a) Is the hypothesis falsifiable? (b) Name the independent and dependent variables. (c) Suggest a control.

Solution. (a) Yes: the measurements could show no difference, which would prove it wrong. (b) Independent: rinse time (30 s or 60 s). Dependent: the amount of fluoride measured on the enamel. (c) A control: enamel samples soaked in water with no fluoride for the same times, to show how much fluoride is measured when none was added.

Check. Only rinse time is changed on purpose; the enamel, rinse, and method stay the same.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Significant figures in a calculation

Problem. A lab technician weighs an empty beaker (52.316 g), adds a sample, and weighs it again (55.4 g on a less precise balance). The sample's volume is 2.85 mL. Report the sample's mass and density with the correct significant figures.

Solution. Mass: 55.4 g − 52.316 g = 3.084 g. In subtraction, keep the fewest decimal places (one, from 55.4), so the mass is 3.1 g. Density: 3.084 g ÷ 2.85 mL = 1.082… g/mL; the mass has only 2 significant figures, so report 1.1 g/mL. (Carry the unrounded value through and round only at the end.)

Check. The less precise balance limits everything after it: one decimal place in the subtraction gives 2 significant figures in the mass, and the density can be no more precise than that.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A weight-based dose

Problem. A provider orders 15 mg of a drug per kilogram of body weight. The patient weighs 132 lb. How many milligrams is the dose? Use 1 kg = 2.2046 lb. (Context only: in practice, always follow the order and facility policy.)

Solution.

\( 132\ \text{lb} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{2.2046\ \text{lb}} \times \dfrac{15\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{kg}} = 898\ \text{mg} \to 9.0 \times 10^{2}\ \text{mg} \)

The dose rate 15 mg/kg has 2 significant figures, so the answer has 2: 9.0 × 102 mg.

Check. Pounds cancel, then kilograms cancel, leaving mg. A 132 lb person is about 60 kg, and 60 × 15 = 900, so the answer is reasonable.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): From a dose to a volume

Problem. A patient who weighs 176 lb has an order for 5.0 mg of a drug per kg of body weight. The pharmacy supplies an oral liquid containing 125 mg per 5.0 mL. What volume holds the dose? Use 1 kg = 2.2046 lb. (Context only: in practice, always follow the order and facility policy.)

Solution. Chain three conversion factors so each unit cancels:

\( 176\ \text{lb} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{2.2046\ \text{lb}} \times \dfrac{5.0\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{kg}} \times \dfrac{5.0\ \text{mL}}{125\ \text{mg}} = 16\ \text{mL} \)

The 5.0 mg/kg and 5.0 mL values have 2 significant figures, so the answer has 2: 16 mL (unrounded 15.97 mL).

Check. 176 lb is about 80 kg, and 80 × 5 = 400 mg. The liquid has 25 mg in each mL, and 400 ÷ 25 = 16 mL.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Classify from a particle description

Problem. Box A holds 8 identical particles, each made of one carbon atom bonded to two oxygen atoms. Box B holds 5 particles of two O atoms bonded together and 5 separate argon atoms. Classify each box.

Solution. Box A: one kind of particle, made of two different elements bonded, so it is a pure substance, a compound (CO2). Box B: two kinds of particles (O2 molecules and Ar atoms) not bonded to each other, so it is a mixture; both components are elements.

Check. Count the kinds of particles first (A: one; B: two), then look inside each particle to decide element or compound.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Physical or chemical, property or change

Problem. Label each: (a) magnesium burns with a bright white light, leaving a white powder; (b) magnesium is a shiny, silver-gray metal; (c) a block of magnesium is sawed in half; (d) magnesium reacts with acid.

Solution. (a) chemical change (light given off, a new white substance forms); (b) physical property; (c) physical change (still magnesium); (d) chemical property (a description of how it can react).

Check. Events that happened are changes; descriptions are properties. Then ask whether a new substance forms.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): Density and identity

Problem. A cube of metal from a Valley scrap yard has a mass of 79.1 g and a volume of 10.0 cm3. Is it more likely aluminum (2.70 g/cm3), steel (about 7.9 g/cm3), or lead (11.3 g/cm3)? Will it float in water?

Solution. d = 79.1 g ÷ 10.0 cm3 = 7.91 g/cm3, closest to steel. It is far denser than water (1.00 g/cm3), so it sinks.

Check. The mass is about 8 times the volume, so the density is about 8 g/cm3.

Example \(\PageIndex{8}\): Density as a conversion factor

Problem. Normal saline has a density of about 1.005 g/mL. What is the mass of the saline in a 250. mL IV bag?

Solution.

\( 250.\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{1.005\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mL}} = 251\ \text{g} \)

The volume (250., 3 significant figures) limits the answer to 3 significant figures: 251 g.

Check. mL cancels and g remains. Saline is barely denser than water, so its mass in grams should be just a little more than its volume in milliliters.

Common Mistakes

  • A number without a unit is not a measurement.
  • Trailing zeros with no decimal point are not significant: 500 has one significant figure; 500. has three.
  • Do not round in the middle of a calculation; round once, at the end.
  • When you square or cube a unit, square or cube the number in the conversion factor too (1 m3 = 106 cm3, not 100 cm3).
  • A subscript does not make a compound: O2, N2, and O3 are elements.
  • A clear liquid is not necessarily pure: saline and vinegar are homogeneous mixtures.
  • Bubbles from boiling, and dissolving a solid, are physical changes.
  • Theories do not "graduate" into laws; they answer a different question (why vs what).
  • Density is mass ÷ volume, and it is intensive; a bigger piece is not denser.
  • In a displacement problem, divide by the change in volume, not the final reading.
  • In a word problem, start with the term that stands alone, not with a conversion factor, and label units with the substance.
  • Read every tool to one digit past its smallest mark, and write the zero when the reading falls on a mark (22.0 °C).
  • Two positive ions always repel, even if one has a larger charge.
