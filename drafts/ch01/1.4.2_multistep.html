Learning Objectives

  • Chain two or more conversion factors in one setup so each unit cancels in turn.
  • Convert from one metric prefix to another by going through the base unit.
  • Convert a rate or concentration (mg/dL, m/s, mL/h) by converting its top unit and its bottom unit.
  • Enter a long setup into a calculator one step at a time and round once at the end.

Very few real conversions take one step. A drug label lists micrograms, the order is in milligrams, and the stock bottle is labeled in grams. A lab reports glucose in milligrams per deciliter, and a research paper uses grams per liter. The method from Section 1.4.1 does not change; you just keep adding factors until the only unit left is the one you want. Each factor cancels the unit the previous one created, like stepping stones across a creek.

Go Through the Base Unit

The metric prefix table on your yellow sheet lists each prefix with its power of ten (micro- = 10−6), which compares that prefix to the base unit (gram, meter, liter, second): 1 µg = 10−6 g. It does not tell you directly how many micrograms are in a milligram or how many centimeters are in a kilometer. So when you convert from one prefix to another, take two steps: first go from the starting prefix to the base unit, then from the base unit to the prefix you want.

\( \text{prefix A} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{base unit} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{prefix B} \)

You can skip the middle step once you are confident, but most students make fewer mistakes when they use it, because every factor then comes straight off the sheet.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Milligrams to kilograms

Problem. Convert 3.6 mg to kilograms.

Given: 3.6 mg. Desired: ??? kg. Strategy: mg → g → kg (1000 mg = 1 g; 1000 g = 1 kg).

Solution.

\(3.6\ \text{mg}\)\(1\ \text{g}\)\(1\ \text{kg}\)
 \(1000\ \text{mg}\)\(1000\ \text{g}\)

\(=\; 3.6 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{kg} \)

Milligrams cancel in the first factor, grams in the second. Both factors are exact, so the answer keeps the 2 significant figures of 3.6 mg: 3.6 × 10−6 kg (0.0000036 kg).

Check. A kilogram is a million times larger than a milligram (103 × 103 = 106), so the number must get a million times smaller. Put a check mark next to the final unit to confirm it is the one you wanted.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Milligrams to micrograms

Problem. A medication order is written in milligrams: 0.125 mg. The tablets on hand are labeled in micrograms (mcg, the health-care symbol for µg). How many micrograms is the order? (Context only; a math example, not dosing guidance.)

Given: 0.125 mg. Desired: ??? mcg. Strategy: mg → g → mcg (1000 mg = 1 g; 1 g = 106 mcg).

Solution.

\( 0.125\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{mg}} \times \dfrac{10^{6}\ \text{mcg}}{1\ \text{g}} = 125\ \text{mcg} \)

The answer keeps the 3 significant figures of 0.125 mg: 125 mcg.

Check. A microgram is smaller than a milligram, so the number must get bigger, and 1 mg = 1000 mcg, so 0.125 × 1000 = 125. A nurse who finds that the order and the label use different units does this conversion before preparing anything, then double-checks it.

Professor Tip

When a problem has a long chain of factors, write down every factor you might need before you set anything up, both ways up. Then build the T-table one unit at a time, asking each time, "Which piece has the unit I just made on the bottom?" The pieces fit together only one way. Finally, put a check mark next to the unit at the end; it should be exactly the unit you wrote under Desired.

Converting Rates and Concentrations

Many quantities have a unit on top and a different unit on the bottom: speed (mi/h), flow rate (mL/h), concentration (mg/dL). These are called derived units. To convert one, convert the top unit and the bottom unit separately. Two habits keep this organized:

  • Do one kind of unit at a time. Finish every top-unit (for example, distance) conversion first, then every bottom-unit (time) conversion.
  • Watch where each unit sits. A unit on the bottom of the given needs a factor with that unit on the top to cancel.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A lab value in different units

Problem. A fasting blood glucose result is 95 mg/dL, the unit used by most United States labs. Some labs and research papers use grams per liter. Express the result in g/L. (1 dL = 10−1 L, so 10 dL = 1 L.)

Given: 95 mg/dL. Desired: ??? g/L. Strategy: top: mg → g; bottom: dL → L.

Solution. Milligrams are on top, so the first factor has mg on the bottom. Deciliters are on the bottom, so the second factor has dL on the top:

\( \dfrac{95\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{dL}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{mg}} \times \dfrac{10\ \text{dL}}{1\ \text{L}} = 0.95\ \text{g/L} \)

Two significant figures, from 95 mg/dL: 0.95 g/L.

Check. Going from mg to g divides by 1000; going from "per dL" to "per L" multiplies by 10, because a liter holds ten times as much blood as a deciliter. Overall the number gets 100 times smaller: 95 → 0.95. (Context only; reference ranges depend on the lab.)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Pick the right answer

Problem. Convert 6.4 × 10−3 m/s to m/h. Which answer is right: (A) 0.384 m/h, (B) 23.04 m/h, (C) 23 m/h, (D) 3.84 m/h?

Given: 6.4 × 10−3 m/s. Desired: ??? m/h. Strategy: only the bottom unit changes: s → min → h.

Solution. Seconds are on the bottom, so the next factor needs seconds on top:

\( \dfrac{6.4 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{s}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{s}}{1\ \text{min}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{min}}{1\ \text{h}} = 23.04\ \text{m/h} \to 23\ \text{m/h} \)

The answer is (C), 23 m/h. (B) has the right digits but too many significant figures: 6.4 has 2, and 60 s = 1 min is exact. (A) multiplies by 60 only once (seconds to minutes) and stops. (D) misplaces the decimal point.

Check. There are 3600 seconds in an hour, so the distance per hour must be 3600 times the distance per second: 0.0064 × 3600 ≈ 23.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): A long chain, top and bottom

Problem. Convert 32.5 mi/h to cm/ms. (1 mi = 5280 ft and 1 ft = 12 in, both exact US definitions; 1 in = 2.54 cm, exact.)

Given: 32.5 mi/h. Desired: ??? cm/ms. Strategy: top: mi → ft → in → cm; then bottom: h → min → s → ms.

Solution. Do all the distance factors first, then all the time factors:

\( \dfrac{32.5\ \text{mi}}{1\ \text{h}} \times \dfrac{5280\ \text{ft}}{1\ \text{mi}} \times \dfrac{12\ \text{in}}{1\ \text{ft}} \times \dfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{60\ \text{min}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{min}}{60\ \text{s}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{s}}{1000\ \text{ms}} = 1.453\ \text{cm/ms} \)

Notice that "h" in the given cancels "h" four factors later; units do not have to be next to each other to cancel. Every factor is exact, so the answer keeps the 3 significant figures of 32.5: 1.45 cm/ms.

Check. On a calculator, multiply all the tops first (32.5 × 5280 × 12 × 2.54), press enter, then divide by 60, enter, by 60, enter, and by 1000, enter. Doing the bottoms one at a time keeps you from dividing by only part of the bottom.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

Respiratory therapists describe breathing with rates. Tidal volume is the volume of air moved in one normal breath, often around 400–500 mL in a resting adult, and the respiratory rate is breaths per minute. Multiplying them gives minute ventilation, usually reported in liters per minute. For a tidal volume of 450 mL per breath and a rate of 16 breaths per minute:

\( \dfrac{450\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{breath}} \times \dfrac{16\ \text{breaths}}{1\ \text{min}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \text{mL}} = 7.2\ \text{L/min} \)

Breaths cancel breaths and milliliters cancel milliliters, leaving L/min. A resting adult's minute ventilation is commonly around 5 to 8 L/min. Oxygen flow, ventilator settings, and nebulizer volumes are all rates like this, and they are charted in metric units. (Context only; values vary by patient.)

Common Mistakes

  • Jumping between prefixes in one step and guessing the factor (for example, writing 100 mcg = 1 mg). Go through the base unit and use factors from the sheet.
  • Converting only the top of a rate. In mg/dL → g/L, both mg and dL must change.
  • Putting the bottom unit's factor the wrong way up. A unit on the bottom of the given cancels with a factor that has that unit on top.
  • Rounding after every step. Keep all digits in the calculator and round once at the end.
  • Dividing by only part of the bottom when typing a long chain. Divide by each bottom number one at a time.

Summary

  • A multi-step conversion is a chain of factors; each factor cancels the unit the previous one created.
  • For prefix-to-prefix conversions, go through the base unit: prefix → base → prefix.
  • To convert a rate or concentration, convert the top unit and the bottom unit, one kind at a time.
  • Exact factors (prefixes, 60 s = 1 min, 1 in = 2.54 cm) never limit significant figures; round once, at the end.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Convert 11.84 µs to milliseconds.

Hint

Go through the base unit, seconds: µs → s → ms.

Answer

\( 11.84\ \mu\text{s} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{s}}{10^{6}\ \mu\text{s}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{ms}}{1\ \text{s}} = \) 1.184 × 10−2 ms (0.01184 ms; 4 significant figures).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Convert 3.549 lb to milligrams. (1 lb = 453.59 g)

Hint

Chain two factors: lb → g (453.59 g/1 lb), then g → mg. Use scientific notation to show the significant figures.

Answer

\( 3.549\ \text{lb} \times \dfrac{453.59\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{lb}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} = 1{,}609{,}791\ \text{mg} \to \) 1.610 × 106 mg. The measurement has 4 significant figures and the factor 453.59 has 5, so the answer has 4; scientific notation shows the significant final zero.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A hemoglobin result is 13.5 g/dL. Labs in many other countries report it in g/L. Convert it.

Hint

Only the bottom unit changes. How many deciliters are in one liter? Put dL on top in that factor so dL cancels.

Answer

\( \dfrac{13.5\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{dL}} \times \dfrac{10\ \text{dL}}{1\ \text{L}} = \) 135 g/L (3 significant figures; only the bottom unit changes).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A simple syrup has a sugar concentration of 0.5 g/mL. Express it in kg/dL. (1 dL = 100 mL)

Hint

Two units change: g on top becomes kg, and mL on the bottom becomes dL. For the bottom unit, put mL on top in the factor so it cancels.

Answer

\( \dfrac{0.5\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{100\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{dL}} = \) 0.05 kg/dL (1 significant figure). Answers of 50 kg/dL (forgot g → kg: 0.5 × 100) or 5 × 10−6 kg/dL (put 100 mL on the bottom: 0.5 ÷ 1000 ÷ 100) come from one flipped or missing factor.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

(Math practice only.) An IV pump is set to 125 mL/h. What is this rate in mL/min?

Hint

Hours are on the bottom, so use 1 h = 60 min with hours on top. Size check: per minute should be a smaller number than per hour.

Answer

\( \dfrac{125\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{h}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{60\ \text{min}} = 2.083\ \text{mL/min} \to \) 2.08 mL/min (3 significant figures; 60 min = 1 h is exact).
