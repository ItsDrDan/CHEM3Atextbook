Learning Objectives

  • Tell qualitative observations from quantitative observations.
  • Describe the steps of the scientific method and put a short investigation in order.
  • Write a testable (falsifiable) hypothesis and name the independent variable, dependent variable, and control in an experiment.
  • Explain the difference between a scientific law and a scientific theory.

Before a nurse charts a patient's temperature, before a dental hygienist records pocket depths, and before a respiratory therapist adjusts an oxygen flow, each of them observes, records, and checks. That habit of careful observation and testing is the heart of science. Chemistry is the study of matter and the changes it undergoes, and the way chemists learn about matter is the scientific method: a cycle of observing, explaining, and testing that you will use all semester, in lab and in your future career.

Observations: Qualitative and Quantitative

Every investigation starts with an observation, something you notice with your senses or an instrument. Observations come in two kinds.

  • A qualitative observation describes a quality with words, not numbers: "the solution turned pink," "the patient's skin is pale and clammy," "the gas smells like rotten eggs."
  • A quantitative observation is a measurement: a number with a unit. "The solution has a volume of 25.0 mL," "the patient's temperature is 38.6 °C," "the tank pressure is 1900 psi."

Both matter. A qualitative observation often tells you that something happened; a quantitative one tells you how much. Quantitative observations are where science, medicine, and engineering get their power, and they are only as good as the measurement behind them. That is why the next three sections teach you to record a measurement with the right unit (Section 1.2), the right number of digits (Section 1.3), and to convert it correctly from one unit to another (Section 1.4).

Professor Tip

The quick test: if the observation has a number and a unit, it is quantitative. "Very hot" is qualitative; "92 °C" is quantitative. A number with no unit ("the reading was 4") is an incomplete observation, so always write the unit.

The Steps of the Scientific Method

Scientists do not follow a rigid recipe, but most investigations move through the same steps (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

  1. Observe and record qualitative and quantitative data.
  2. Ask a question about what you observed.
  3. Form a hypothesis: a tentative, testable explanation. A good hypothesis is falsifiable, meaning an experiment could show it is wrong. "Plants grow faster with more nitrogen fertilizer" is falsifiable; "plants are happier with fertilizer" is not.
  4. Experiment: test the hypothesis by changing one thing and measuring the result.
  5. Analyze the data and draw a conclusion. If the data do not support the hypothesis, revise it and test again. If they do, other scientists repeat the work to see whether they get the same result.

Designing a fair experiment

A fair experiment changes only one thing at a time.

  • The independent variable is the one thing you deliberately change.
  • The dependent variable is what you measure; it depends on the change you made.
  • The control is a trial in which you do not apply the change, so you have something to compare against. Everything else (the constants) is kept the same.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Name the variables

Problem. A microbiology student wants to know whether a hand sanitizer reduces bacteria on skin. She presses one clean fingertip onto an agar plate (a dish of nutrient gel), then applies sanitizer to the same hand, waits 30 seconds, and presses a second fingertip onto a second plate. After 48 hours at 37 °C she counts the bacterial colonies on each plate. Identify the independent variable, the dependent variable, the control, and two constants.

Solution. The independent variable is whether sanitizer was applied (yes or no). The dependent variable is the number of bacterial colonies that grow. The control is the plate pressed before sanitizer was applied. Constants include the type of agar, the incubation time (48 h), the incubation temperature (37 °C), and the same person's hand.

Check. Ask, "What did she change on purpose?" (sanitizer) and "What did she count?" (colonies). The control is the trial without the change. Each answer fits.

Biology Connection: Microbiology

The fingerprint test in Example \(\PageIndex{1}\) mirrors how microbiologists measure germs. Bacteria are far too small to count one by one, so a sample is spread on agar and each living cell that lands there grows into a visible colony. Counting colonies gives a quantitative result, reported as colony-forming units (CFU). Hospitals use the same idea to check whether surfaces are clean and whether a sterilizer is working. A good microbiology experiment always includes a control plate, because without one you cannot tell whether the sanitizer worked or the bacteria were never there.

Laws and Theories

When the same result shows up again and again, scientists summarize it. A scientific law is a short statement (often an equation) that describes what always happens. For example, the law of conservation of mass says that the total mass of the substances before a chemical reaction equals the total mass after it. A law does not explain why.

A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation of why nature behaves the way it does. The atomic theory explains the law of conservation of mass: atoms are only rearranged in a reaction, never created or destroyed, so the mass stays the same. In everyday speech "theory" means a guess, but in science a theory is the strongest kind of explanation there is, supported by many experiments. Theories can still be revised if new evidence demands it; you will see this happen to models of the atom in Chapters 2 and 3.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Law, theory, or hypothesis?

Problem. Classify each statement. (a) Gases are made of tiny particles in constant random motion, and their collisions with the container walls cause pressure. (b) At constant temperature, the pressure of a gas doubles when its volume is cut in half. (c) Adding salt to the irrigation water will lower the yield of this tomato field.

Solution. (a) is a theory: it explains why gases behave as they do (this is the kinetic molecular theory, Chapter 10). (b) is a law: it describes what always happens, and it can be written as an equation (Boyle's law). (c) is a hypothesis: a testable prediction about one situation that has not yet been tested.

Check. "Why" with a model of particles points to a theory; a repeatable relationship points to a law; a single untested prediction points to a hypothesis.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The nursing process (assess, diagnose, plan, implement, evaluate) follows the same logic as the scientific method. Assessment gathers qualitative data ("patient reports sharp pain, rates it 7 out of 10"; "skin warm and flushed") and quantitative data (heart rate 112 beats per minute, temperature 38.9 °C). The nurse then forms a working explanation, carries out an intervention ordered by the provider, and evaluates by measuring again: did the temperature come down? If not, the plan is revised, just as a scientist revises a hypothesis. Clear, quantitative charting (a number and a unit) is what lets the next shift compare results.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling a number without a unit quantitative and complete. "The mass was 12" is not a usable measurement. Write "12.4 g."
  • Mixing up the independent and dependent variables. You choose the independent variable; you measure the dependent variable.
  • Thinking a theory becomes a law once it is proven. Laws and theories do different jobs (what versus why). A theory never "graduates" into a law.
  • Writing a hypothesis that cannot be tested. If no experiment could prove it wrong, it is not a scientific hypothesis.

Summary

  • Qualitative observations use words; quantitative observations are measurements with a number and a unit, and they are only as reliable as the measurement (Sections 1.2 to 1.4).
  • The scientific method cycles through observing, asking a question, forming a falsifiable hypothesis, experimenting, and analyzing.
  • A fair experiment changes one independent variable, measures a dependent variable, keeps everything else constant, and includes a control.
  • A law describes what always happens; a theory explains why, and is supported by many experiments.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Label each observation qualitative or quantitative: (a) the urine sample is dark amber; (b) the patient weighs 72.5 kg; (c) the reaction mixture fizzed; (d) the oxygen flow is 2 L/min.

Hint

Is there a number with a unit (quantitative), or only a description in words (qualitative)?

Answer

(a) qualitative (a color, no number); (b) quantitative (number and unit); (c) qualitative; (d) quantitative.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A farmer tests whether a new drip-irrigation schedule increases almond yield. One block of trees keeps the old schedule; a matching block gets the new one. At harvest, she weighs the almonds from each block. Name the independent variable, dependent variable, and control.

Hint

The independent variable is the one thing she changes on purpose; the dependent variable is what she measures. The control is left as it was.

Answer

Independent variable: the irrigation schedule (the one thing she changed). Dependent variable: the mass of almonds harvested (what she measured). Control: the block kept on the old schedule.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which statement is a scientific law, and which is a theory? (a) Matter is made of atoms that are rearranged, not created or destroyed, in chemical reactions. (b) In a chemical reaction, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

Hint

A law states what always happens; a theory explains why, using a model. Which statement uses atoms to explain?

Answer

(a) is a theory: it explains why, using a model of atoms. (b) is a law: it states what always happens (the law of conservation of mass).
