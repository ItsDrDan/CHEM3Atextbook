Learning Objectives

  • Pull the Givens, the Desired, and the conversion factors out of a word problem, and ignore distractors.
  • Spot the giveaway phrases ("per," "in each," "in every," "is/has/makes," a slash) that signal a conversion factor.
  • Start the setup with the quantity that stands alone, not with a conversion factor.
  • Label units with the substance (mg caffeine, kg body weight) so only matching units cancel.
  • Solve multi-step dosage problems, splitting a problem with two stand-alone quantities into two setups.

By now you can convert milliliters to liters and pounds to kilograms. Real problems, on exams, in lab, and in clinical work, rarely arrive that neatly. They arrive as a paragraph, with the numbers you need mixed in with numbers and names you do not. A word problem can feel like a big puzzle, but that is all it is: the pieces fit together only one way, because the units have to cancel only one way. This section gives you a routine for finding the pieces, and then applies it to the dosage calculations that many of you will do for the rest of your careers.

A Routine for Word Problems

  1. Read slowly. Every word could hold a clue. Read the problem more than once, and say it quietly to yourself if that helps.
  2. Write the Givens. Every number with its unit and what it describes (405 mi, 72 mi/h).
  3. Write the Desired. The unit, and the substance, the answer must have. It is not always in the last sentence.
  4. Hunt for conversion factors. Some are in the problem (look for the giveaway phrases below); some are on your yellow sheet (prefixes, US–metric factors); some you already know (60 min = 1 h).
  5. Start with the term that stands alone, then let the units guide you: add factors so each unwanted unit is on the opposite side and cancels.
  6. Calculate, round, and check. Multiply the tops, divide by the bottoms, round once to the significant figures of the measured givens, and ask whether the size makes sense.

Giveaway phrases

Any sentence that links two quantities is probably a conversion factor. These phrases give them away; each one can be written either way up.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Phrases that signal a conversion factor
PhraseExampleConversion factor
___ in each ___4 quarters in each dollar4 quarters / 1 dollar
___ per ___24 hours per day24 h / 1 day
___ in every ___500 sheets in every ream of paper500 sheets / 1 ream
___ is / has / makes ___a tablet has 325 mg of drug325 mg drug / 1 tablet
a slash, "/"a speed of 65 mi/h65 mi / 1 h
percent, "%"39.3% sodium by mass39.3 g Na / 100 g NaCl (Section 1.4.5)

A slash is a fraction bar, so "65 mi/h" is already written as a conversion factor. Recipes are full of these ratios, and so are medication labels.

Start with the term that stands alone

Most word problems give you one quantity that is not a ratio: a distance, a mass, the size of a bottle. Start your setup with that term. A conversion factor can be flipped to fit wherever you need it, but the stand-alone term cannot: it has to sit on top, at the start. If you start with a conversion factor instead, you can still reach an answer, but it is much easier to set up a factor upside down.

There is one exception. If a problem gives you only conversion factors (for example, "convert 65 mi/h to m/s"), start with the given rate itself (65 mi/h), since it is the quantity you are converting, and then cancel miles and hours one factor at a time.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Find what matters, ignore what does not

Problem. How many minutes will it take to drive from Fresno to Sacramento, a distance of 171 mi, at an average speed of 65 mi/h?

Given: 171 mi (stands alone); 65 mi/h (a "/" means a conversion factor). Desired: ??? min. The city names are scene-setting, not data. Standardized tests, including the entrance exams for many nursing programs, often include details like these to distract you.

Solution. Start with 171 mi. Put miles on the bottom of the speed factor so they cancel, then use 60 min = 1 h:

\( 171\ \text{mi} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{65\ \text{mi}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{min}}{1\ \text{h}} = 157.8\ \text{min} \to 1.6 \times 10^{2}\ \text{min} \)

The speed (65 mi/h) has 2 significant figures; 60 min = 1 h is exact. The answer is 1.6 × 102 min. (Writing 160 min also shows 2 significant figures under this book's rule.)

Check. 171 mi at about 65 mi/h is a little under 3 hours, and 2.6 h × 60 ≈ 160 min.

Label Every Unit with Its Substance

Many problems contain two different things measured in the same unit: milligrams of caffeine and milligrams of coffee, kilograms of body weight and milligrams of drug. Write the substance after every unit (mg caffeine, oz tea, kg body). Then a unit cancels only when both the unit and the substance match, and you will never cancel milligrams of one thing against milligrams of another. Units do not have to be next to each other to cancel; one on top in the first factor can cancel one on the bottom four factors later.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Caffeine in cups of tea

Problem. Brewed black tea contains about 5.50 mg of caffeine per fluid ounce, and a cup holds 8.00 fl oz. How many cups of tea contain a total of 0.400 g of caffeine? (Context only: 400 mg a day is a commonly cited upper amount for healthy adults; this is a math example, not health advice.)

Given: 0.400 g caffeine (stands alone); 5.50 mg caffeine / 1 fl oz tea; 8.00 fl oz tea / 1 cup. Desired: ??? cups.

Solution. The given is in grams of caffeine, but the tea factor is in milligrams of caffeine, so go from g to mg first:

\( 0.400\ \text{g caffeine} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{fl oz tea}}{5.50\ \text{mg caffeine}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{cup}}{8.00\ \text{fl oz tea}} = 9.09\ \text{cups} \)

All the measured values have 3 significant figures: 9.09 cups.

Check. One cup holds 8 × 5.5 = 44 mg of caffeine, and 400 ÷ 44 is about 9. Notice that "mg caffeine" canceled "mg caffeine," and "fl oz tea" canceled "fl oz tea."

Professor Tip

For a long setup on the TI-36X Pro, multiply everything on the top first and press enter. Then divide by the first number on the bottom and press enter, then the next, one at a time. Typing the whole chain at once makes it easy to drop a number or divide by only part of the bottom. Do one step at a time and the calculator follows you.

Dosage Calculations

Medication orders and labels are conversion factors: an order of "4.0 mg per kg" is 4.0 mg drug / 1 kg body, and a label of "125 mg per 5.00 mL" is 125 mg drug / 5.00 mL. The routine does not change. The examples below are math practice only. (Context only; in practice, always follow the order and facility policy.)

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): From an order in grams to a volume

Problem. An order calls for 0.200 g of a drug. The oral liquid on hand contains 125 mg per 5.00 mL. What volume holds the ordered amount?

Given: 0.200 g drug (stands alone); 125 mg drug / 5.00 mL. Desired: ??? mL.

Solution. The order is in grams and the label is in milligrams, so the bridge is 1 g = 1000 mg:

\( 0.200\ \text{g drug} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{5.00\ \text{mL}}{125\ \text{mg drug}} = 8.00\ \text{mL} \)

Check. 0.200 g is 200 mg, which is 1.6 times the 125 mg in 5 mL, and 1.6 × 5 = 8 mL. Three significant figures: 8.00 mL.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Tablets from a bulk supply

Problem. A pharmaceutical plant has 2.50 kg of acetaminophen. How many 325 mg tablets can it make?

Given: 2.50 kg acetaminophen (stands alone); 325 mg / 1 tablet ("each tablet has"). Desired: ??? tablets.

Solution. Go through the base unit, grams, to get from kilograms to milligrams:

\( 2.50\ \text{kg} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{tablet}}{325\ \text{mg}} = 7692\ \text{tablets} \to 7.69 \times 10^{3}\ \text{tablets} \)

Check. 2.50 kg is 2,500,000 mg, and each tablet uses about a third of a gram, so a few thousand tablets is right. Three significant figures: 7.69 × 103 tablets.

Two stand-alone terms, two setups

Some problems give you two quantities that stand alone, such as a patient's weight and the size of a bottle. Do not try to force them into one line. Solve the problem in two parts: use the first stand-alone term to build a new conversion factor, then use that factor with the second.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): How long will the bottle last?

Problem. A drug is ordered at 4.0 mg per kg of body weight, three times a day. The patient weighs 154 lb, and the bottle holds 1.50 × 104 mg of the drug. How many days will the bottle last? (1 kg = 2.2046 lb)

Given: 154 lb body (stands alone); 1.50 × 104 mg drug (also stands alone); 4.0 mg drug per kg body per dose; 3 doses / 1 day (a count, exact). Desired: ??? days.

Solution. Part 1: start with the body weight and find the milligrams of drug per day.

\( 154\ \text{lb body} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg body}}{2.2046\ \text{lb body}} \times \dfrac{4.0\ \text{mg drug}}{1\ \text{kg body} \cdot 1\ \text{dose}} \times \dfrac{3\ \text{doses}}{1\ \text{day}} = 838.2\ \text{mg drug/day} \)

The order is per kilogram per dose, so both kg body and doses cancel. Keep 838.2; do not round yet.

Part 2: start with the bottle, and use the new factor with mg on the bottom.

\( 1.50 \times 10^{4}\ \text{mg drug} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{day}}{838.2\ \text{mg drug}} = 17.9\ \text{days} \to 18\ \text{days} \)

The order (4.0 mg/kg) has 2 significant figures, so the answer has 2: 18 days.

Check. 154 lb is about 70 kg; 70 × 4 × 3 = 840 mg a day; 15,000 ÷ 840 is a little under 18. The kilograms of body canceled each other, and the milligrams of drug canceled each other; the two masses were never mixed.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many nursing programs give a dosage-calculation exam and require a perfect or near-perfect score before students may give medications in clinical rotations, because a misplaced decimal point can mean a tenfold overdose or underdose. The skills on this page (labeling every unit, canceling only matching units, starting with the stand-alone quantity, and estimating to check) are the same ones those exams test. Pharmacists, respiratory therapists, and veterinary technicians use them daily too. (Context only; always follow the order and facility policy.)

Everyday Ratios

The same routine works for anything given "per" something. A recipe, a pitcher of lemonade, or a baker's pace is a conversion factor in disguise.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Sugar in a glass

Problem. A 1.50 L pitcher of sweet tea contains 96.0 g of sugar. How much sugar is in a 355 mL glass of it?

Given: 355 mL tea (stands alone); 96.0 g sugar / 1.50 L tea. Desired: ??? g sugar.

Solution.

\( 355\ \text{mL tea} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \text{mL}} \times \dfrac{96.0\ \text{g sugar}}{1.50\ \text{L tea}} = 22.7\ \text{g sugar} \)

Check. The glass is about a quarter of the pitcher (0.355 ÷ 1.50 ≈ 0.24), and a quarter of 96 g is about 23 g.

Common Mistakes

  • Starting with a conversion factor when a stand-alone term is available. Start with the term that stands alone.
  • Using every number in the problem. City names, temperatures, and background facts are often distractors; use only numbers that fit your map from Given to Desired.
  • Canceling the wrong milligrams. Label units with the substance (mg drug, kg body) and cancel only exact matches.
  • Forgetting the per-day or per-dose multiplier in a dosing problem.
  • Rounding between the two parts of a two-setup problem. Carry the unrounded value forward.

Summary

  • Read slowly; write the Givens (with substances) and the Desired; then hunt for conversion factors.
  • "Per," "in each," "in every," "is/has/makes," a slash, and a percent all signal conversion factors.
  • Start with the term that stands alone and let the units guide you. With two stand-alone terms, solve in two parts.
  • Dosage problems use the same routine: orders and labels are conversion factors.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A baker in Visalia makes 2 dozen cookies every 18 min. How many minutes does she need to make 150. cookies? (1 dozen = 12, exact)

Answer

\( 150.\ \text{cookies} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{dozen}}{12\ \text{cookies}} \times \dfrac{18\ \text{min}}{2\ \text{dozen}} = 112.5\ \text{min} \) → 1.1 × 102 min (18 min has 2 significant figures; the city name is a distractor).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

(Math practice only.) An order calls for 0.150 g of a drug. The liquid on hand contains 250. mg per 5.00 mL. What volume holds the ordered amount?

Answer

\( 0.150\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{5.00\ \text{mL}}{250.\ \text{mg}} = 3.00\ \text{mL} \)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A problem says, "A 12.0 oz can of soda contains 34.0 mg of caffeine." Write the conversion factor both ways up, and say which one you would use to find the caffeine in 3 cans (36.0 oz).

Answer

34.0 mg caffeine / 12.0 oz soda, or 12.0 oz soda / 34.0 mg caffeine. To go from ounces to milligrams, ounces must be on the bottom: 36.0 oz × (34.0 mg / 12.0 oz) = 102 mg caffeine.
