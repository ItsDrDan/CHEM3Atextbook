Learning Objectives

  • Explain why every measurement needs both a number and a unit.
  • Pick out the number and the unit in a measured quantity, including combined units such as mg/kg.
  • Describe what Sections 1.2.1 and 1.2.2 cover and why they matter in health care.

"I saw a whale as big as my house!" How big is that? If you live in a mansion, it is a blue whale. If you live in a studio apartment, it might be a dolphin. The sentence describes something real, but nobody else can use it, because "as big as my house" is not a standard. Science and health care fix this problem the same way: every measurement is a number together with a unit. The number says how many; the unit says how many of what.

That is also why this course sometimes feels like an applied math class. Chemistry is about quantity: how much drug is in a tablet, how many cells are in a drop of blood, how much a patient weighs. Before we can do any chemistry with those quantities, we have to be able to write them so that anyone, anywhere, reads them the same way.

A Number Without a Unit Is Not a Measurement

Suppose a chart says a child's weight is "18." Is that 18 kilograms or 18 pounds? A weight-based medication calculated for 18 kg would be a little more than twice the amount calculated for 18 lb, because 1 kg is about 2.2 lb. The number alone cannot tell you which one is right. Some units are combinations of two units, such as mg/kg ("milligrams of drug per kilogram of body weight") or beats/min. A combined unit is still one unit, and it is still required.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Number and unit

Problem. Identify the number and the unit in each quantity: (a) a heart rate of 72 beats/min; (b) 0.5 mg; (c) two tablets; (d) a dose of 15 mg/kg.

Solution. (a) Number 72, unit beats per minute (a combined unit). (b) Number 0.5, unit milligrams. (c) Number 2, unit tablets (a counted unit). (d) Number 15, unit milligrams per kilogram (a combined unit).

Check. Each quantity answers two questions: "how many?" (the number) and "of what?" (the unit). If either is missing, the quantity is incomplete.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Vital signs, lab values, and medication orders are all measurements, and every one of them carries a unit: blood pressure in mmHg (millimeters of mercury), glucose in mg/dL (milligrams per deciliter), weight in kg. Safety organizations such as the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) recommend recording patient weights in kilograms only, because a weight written in pounds but read as kilograms (or the reverse) changes every weight-based dose by a factor of about 2.2. In health care, "what unit is this?" is a patient-safety question, not a formality. (Context only; always follow the order and facility policy.)

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A note in a patient's chart reads "Temp 37.2, weight 64." What is missing, and why does it matter?

Hint

Every measurement needs a number and a unit. What units could each number be, and could a reader guess wrong?

Answer

Both units are missing. 37.2 is a normal body temperature in degrees Celsius but would be impossible in degrees Fahrenheit, and 64 could be kilograms or pounds, a difference of about 2.2 times. Without units the reader has to guess, and a guess about weight can change a weight-based dose.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Identify the number and the unit: (a) 250 mL; (b) 4.1 mmol/L of potassium; (c) one dozen syringes.

Hint

A measurement has a number and a unit. Combined units (like "per liter") and counted units (like dozen) count as units too.

Answer

(a) 250, milliliters. (b) 4.1, millimoles per liter (a combined unit). (c) 1, dozen syringes (a counted unit; one dozen is exactly 12).
