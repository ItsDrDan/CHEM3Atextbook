These practice problems cover everything in Chapter 1: the scientific method, classifying matter (including particle diagrams and the diatomic seven), states of matter, electric charge, physical and chemical properties and changes, and density. They are grouped by section, and the last group mixes ideas from several sections the way an exam does. Try each problem on paper before you open the answer. Every answer shows the reasoning, and for multiple-choice problems it also explains why each wrong choice is wrong, so read the answer even when you got it right.

1.1 The Scientific Method

Problem 1.1

Which of these is a quantitative observation?

A. The fluid draining from the wound is cloudy.
B. The patient's respiratory rate is 18 breaths per minute.
C. The tablet tastes bitter.
D. The gas in the flask is colorless.

Answer

B. A quantitative observation is a measurement: a number with a unit. "18 breaths per minute" has both.

  • A is qualitative: "cloudy" describes appearance with a word, not a number.
  • C is qualitative: taste is described with a word.
  • D is qualitative: "colorless" is a description, not a measurement.

Problem 1.2

A dental hygienist and a respiratory therapist share notes on the same patient. Label each note qualitative or quantitative.

(a) Gums bleed when probed.
(b) Probing depth at the lower left first molar is 5 mm.
(c) Patient reports that her mouth often feels dry (xerostomia, the medical term for dry mouth).
(d) SpO2 (the oxygen saturation read by a pulse oximeter clipped to the finger) is 96%.
(e) A wheezing sound is heard when the patient breathes out.

Answer

(a) Qualitative: it describes what happened in words. (b) Quantitative: a number (5) with a unit (mm). (c) Qualitative: a description reported in words. (d) Quantitative: a measured number, reported as a percentage. (e) Qualitative: a sound described in words, with no number. Notice that both kinds of observation are useful; the qualitative ones often tell the clinician what to measure next.

Problem 1.3

Which statement is a testable (falsifiable) hypothesis?

A. Tule fog is the most beautiful weather in California.
B. Sugar dissolves because it wants to be in water.
C. Raising the water temperature from 20 °C to 40 °C makes sugar dissolve faster.
D. Chemistry is more important than biology.

Answer

C. You can test it: time how long the same amount of sugar takes to dissolve at each temperature, keeping everything else the same. If the 40 °C trial is not faster, the hypothesis is shown wrong, which is what makes it falsifiable.

  • A is an opinion; no measurement could prove it wrong.
  • B gives sugar a "want," which no experiment can measure.
  • D is a value judgment, not a prediction about what will happen in an experiment.

Problem 1.4

A dairy herd manager in Tulare County writes down what she did over one summer, but the steps are out of order. Put them in the order of the scientific method, and name the control.

(P) She predicts that cows in a barn with fans and water misters will give more milk on hot days than cows in a barn without them.
(Q) For one month she records the milk from a barn with fans and misters and from a matching barn without them.
(R) She notices that her herd gives less milk during weeks when afternoon temperatures pass 38 °C.
(S) The cows in the cooled barn gave more milk, so she concludes the data support her prediction and plans to repeat the test next summer.
(T) She asks, "Is the heat causing the drop in milk?"

Answer

Order: R, T, P, Q, S. R is the observation (milk drops in hot weeks). T is the question. P is the hypothesis, a testable prediction. Q is the experiment. S is the analysis and conclusion, with a plan to repeat the work, which is how results become trusted.

The control is the barn without fans and misters: it shows what milk output would be without the change, so there is something to compare against.

Problem 1.5

A microbiology student tests whether ultraviolet (UV) light kills bacteria. She spreads the same amount of a harmless lab strain of bacteria on four identical agar plates (dishes of nutrient gel). She places three plates under a UV lamp for 10, 20, and 30 seconds, and leaves the fourth plate unexposed. All four plates then sit for 24 hours at 37 °C, and she counts the colonies on each. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable, the control, and two constants.

Answer

Independent variable: the UV exposure time (0, 10, 20, or 30 s), because that is what she changes on purpose. Dependent variable: the number of colonies that grow, because that is what she measures. Control: the unexposed plate, which shows how many colonies grow with no UV. Constants (any two): the amount and strain of bacteria, the type of agar, the incubation time (24 h), the incubation temperature (37 °C), and the distance from the lamp.

Problem 1.6

Nursing students test the hypothesis "Washing hands longer with soap leaves fewer bacteria." After each handwashing time, a student presses a fingertip onto a fresh agar plate, and the plates are incubated the same way. The table below shows the colony counts.

(a) Which trial is the control? (b) Name the independent and dependent variables. (c) Do the data support the hypothesis? (d) Describe the pattern between 20 s and 40 s. (e) Why should the students repeat the whole experiment before drawing a firm conclusion?

Answer

(a) Trial 1, with no washing: it is the trial without the change, so it is the baseline for comparison.

(b) Independent variable: handwashing time (chosen by the students). Dependent variable: the number of colonies (measured).

Problem 1.7

Classify each statement as a scientific law, a scientific theory, or a hypothesis.

(a) Matter is made of particles that are always moving, and the faster they move, the hotter the sample.
(b) Every sample of pure water, from any source, contains hydrogen and oxygen in the same proportion by mass.
(c) If I put a heat lamp in this chicken coop, the hens will lay more eggs this month.
(d) The total mass of a sealed bottle containing water and an antacid tablet is the same before and after the tablet fizzes.

Answer

(a) Theory. It explains why samples are hot or cold, using a model of moving particles.

(b) Law. It describes what always happens, based on many measurements, without explaining why.

(c) Hypothesis. It is a testable prediction about one situation that has not been tested yet.

(d) Law. It is an example of the law of conservation of mass: in a closed container, mass before a change equals mass after. (The bottle must be sealed; if the gas escaped, the measured mass would drop.)

Problem 1.8

Which statement about scientific theories is correct?

A. A theory becomes a law once it has been proven.
B. A theory is a well-tested explanation that can still be revised if new evidence requires it.
C. A theory is the scientific word for a guess.
D. Laws explain why things happen; theories only describe what happens.

Answer

B. A theory explains why nature behaves as it does and is supported by many experiments, but it stays open to revision.

  • A is wrong: theories and laws do different jobs (why versus what). A theory never "graduates" into a law.
  • C is wrong: that is the everyday meaning of "theory." In science, a theory is the strongest kind of explanation there is.
  • D has the two backwards: laws describe what happens; theories explain why.

1.2 Classifying Matter

Problem 1.9

Which of these is a compound?

A. O3 (ozone)
B. N2
C. NH3 (ammonia)
D. air

Answer

C. NH3 contains two different elements, nitrogen and hydrogen, bonded in a fixed 1:3 ratio.

  • A is an element: every atom in O3 is oxygen. A subscript does not make a compound.
  • B is an element (one of the diatomic seven): only one kind of atom.
  • D is a homogeneous mixture of several gases (mostly N2 and O2), not one substance.

Problem 1.10

Classify each as an element, a compound, a homogeneous mixture, or a heterogeneous mixture.

(a) helium in a party balloon, He
(b) baking soda, NaHCO3
(c) D5W, an intravenous (IV) fluid made of 5% dextrose (a sugar) dissolved in water
(d) a shovelful of soil from a Fresno County orchard
(e) liquid bromine, Br2
(f) pure distilled water, H2O

Answer

(a) Element: one kind of atom. (b) Compound: four different elements (Na, H, C, O) bonded in a fixed ratio. (c) Homogeneous mixture (a solution): the sugar is spread evenly through the water, so it looks the same everywhere, and the amount of sugar could be different (other IV fluids use 10% dextrose, for example). (d) Heterogeneous mixture: you can see bits of sand, clay, roots, and pebbles. (e) Element: Br2 is one of the diatomic seven; one kind of atom. (f) Compound: two elements bonded in a fixed 2:1 ratio. Distilled water has nothing else dissolved in it, so it is a pure substance.

Problem 1.11

From this list, pick the elements that exist as diatomic molecules and write the formula of each: hydrogen, helium, nitrogen, sulfur, chlorine, neon, iodine, sodium. Then say whether Cl2 is an element or a compound.

Answer

Diatomic: hydrogen (H2), nitrogen (N2), chlorine (Cl2), and iodine (I2). The full set of seven is H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2; helium, sulfur, neon, and sodium are not on that list.

Cl2 is an element. Each molecule has two atoms, but both are chlorine; a compound needs two or more different elements.

Problem 1.12

The table below describes six particle diagrams in words. In each box, atoms listed as "bonded" are joined into one particle. Classify each box as a pure element, a pure compound, a mixture of elements, a mixture of compounds, or a mixture of an element and a compound.

Answer

First ask "is every particle the same?" Then ask whether each kind of particle has one kind of atom (element) or more (compound).

  • A: mixture of elements. Two kinds of particles; each is a single atom of one element.
  • B: pure compound. Every particle is identical (CO2), and each contains two different elements bonded together.
  • C: pure element. Every particle is Cl2, with only one kind of atom.
  • D: mixture of compounds. Two kinds of particles, and each contains two different elements.
  • E: mixture of an element and a compound. N2 is an element; NH3 is a compound.
  • F: pure element. Argon exists as single atoms; every particle is the same.

Problem 1.13

(a) Box G contains 4 O2 molecules and 2 O3 molecules. A student says, "Every atom is oxygen, so this is a pure element." Is the student right? Classify the box.
(b) Box H contains 6 identical particles, each one N atom bonded to three H atoms. Classify it.
(c) One N2 molecule is added to box H. Classify the box now.

Answer

(a) The student is only partly right. Both O2 and O3 are forms of the element oxygen, but they are two different substances (different particles with different properties). A pure substance needs every particle to be the same, so box G is a mixture of two elements.

(b) Every particle is the same NH3 molecule, made of two different elements: pure compound.

(c) Now there are two kinds of particles, NH3 (a compound) and N2 (an element), not bonded to each other: mixture of an element and a compound.

Problem 1.14

Which of these is a molecule but not a compound?

A. H2O
B. NaCl
C. Br2
D. CH4 (methane)

Answer

C. Br2 is two atoms bonded together (a molecule), but both atoms are bromine, so it is an element, not a compound.

  • A is both a molecule and a compound (H and O bonded).
  • B is a compound, but it is made of Na+ and Cl− ions in formula units, not molecules. It is the reverse case: a compound but not a molecule.
  • D is both a molecule and a compound (C and H bonded).

Problem 1.15

Box 1 holds 4 H2 molecules and 2 O2 molecules. Box 2 holds 4 H2O molecules. Both boxes contain exactly 8 H atoms and 4 O atoms. Compare the two boxes: classify each, and give two differences in how they behave.

Answer

Box 1 is a mixture of two elements. The H atoms are bonded only to H, and the O atoms only to O; the two kinds of molecules are just mixed. Box 2 is a pure compound. Each H2O particle has H and O bonded together in a fixed 2:1 ratio.

Differences (any two):

  • Composition: you could add more H2 or more O2 to box 1 and it would still be a hydrogen–oxygen mixture; water is always 2 H to 1 O.
  • Separation: the gases in box 1 could be separated by physical means; getting hydrogen and oxygen out of water takes a chemical change.
  • Properties: a hydrogen–oxygen mixture can explode when sparked; water does not burn at all. A compound's properties differ from those of its elements.

Counting atoms is not enough to classify matter; you must look at how the atoms are bonded into particles.

Problem 1.16

Choose a physical method to separate each mixture, and name the property it uses.

(a) silt and sand from irrigation canal water before it enters a drip system
(b) drinkable water from salty groundwater in the western San Joaquin Valley
(c) cream from whole milk at a Valley dairy plant (the cream is less dense than the rest of the milk)
(d) solid salt from a brine (salt water) solution, when the water does not need to be kept

Answer

(a) Filtration (particle size: the solid is trapped while water passes through). Letting the silt settle and decanting also works (density).

(b) Distillation (boiling point): the water boils off, and the vapor is condensed and collected, leaving the dissolved salts behind. Plain evaporation would leave the salt but lose the water you want.

(c) Centrifugation (density): spinning sends the denser skim milk outward and lets the less dense cream separate. Dairy cream separators are centrifuges.

(d) Evaporation (boiling point): the water leaves and the dissolved salt stays behind.

All four are physical methods, which work because the parts of a mixture are not bonded to each other.

Problem 1.17

A nursing student looks at a bag of normal saline, sees "0.9% sodium chloride (NaCl)" on the label, and says, "Saline has a formula, so it is a compound." Explain the error and classify normal saline correctly.

Answer

NaCl is the formula of only one part of the bag's contents. Normal saline is sodium chloride dissolved in water, so it contains two substances that are physically combined, not bonded into one substance. Its composition can vary: hospitals also stock 0.45% ("half-normal") and 3% saline. A compound always has a fixed composition. Saline looks the same throughout, so it is a homogeneous mixture (a solution). Sodium chloride by itself, the white solid, is the compound.

Problem 1.18

Some dental offices offer nitrous oxide–oxygen sedation, a mild sedation in which the patient breathes a mixture of nitrous oxide gas (N2O) and oxygen gas (O2) through a small nose mask. The dentist or a trained hygienist sets the percentage of each gas on the machine. Classify (a) N2O, (b) O2, and (c) the gas the patient breathes. (d) What fact in the description shows that (c) is a mixture?

Answer

(a) Compound: two different elements, N and O, bonded in a fixed ratio. (b) Element: one kind of atom (diatomic oxygen). (c) Homogeneous mixture: the two gases are spread evenly through each other, so every breath has the same makeup. (d) The clinician can change the percentage of each gas. Only a mixture can have its composition adjusted; a compound always has the same ratio of elements.

1.3 States of Matter

Problem 1.19

A sample keeps its own shape no matter what container it is placed in. Which description fits its particles?

A. Far apart and moving fast in all directions
B. Touching, but sliding past one another
C. Closely packed and vibrating in place
D. Closely packed and not moving at all

Answer

C. A definite shape means a solid, and solid particles are packed closely and vibrate in fixed positions.

  • A describes a gas, which fills its container and has no definite shape.
  • B describes a liquid, which flows and takes the shape of its container.
  • D is wrong because particles in a solid still vibrate; they just do not move out of place.

Problem 1.20

Three samples are each moved from a 100 mL flask into a 250 mL flask. The table below records what happened. Identify the state of each sample, and say which one could be squeezed into a much smaller volume.

Observations when samples are moved to a larger flask
SampleVolume in the 100 mL flaskVolume in the 250 mL flaskShape in the 250 mL flask

Y100 mL (fills the flask)250 mL (fills the flask)Fills the whole flask

Answer

X is a liquid: its volume stayed the same (definite volume), but its shape changed to fit the flask (indefinite shape). Y is a gas: its volume changed to fill whatever container it was in. Z is a solid: both its shape and volume stayed the same.

Y could be compressed, because gas particles are far apart and most of a gas's volume is empty space. In X and Z the particles already touch, so there is almost no space to remove.

Problem 1.21

In a skills lab, a nursing student caps the tip of a syringe filled with 10 mL of air and pushes the plunger. Then she does the same with a capped syringe filled with 10 mL of water. Predict what happens in each case and explain with the particle view.

Answer

Air: the plunger moves in a good distance, so the air is squeezed into a smaller volume. Gas particles are far apart, and pushing removes some of the empty space between them. When she lets go, the plunger springs back as the moving particles push outward.

Water: the plunger barely moves. Liquid particles are already touching, so there is almost no empty space to remove; liquids are nearly incompressible.

Problem 1.22

Four particle diagrams are described below. Identify the state shown in each box. For the two solids, say which is crystalline and which is amorphous.

Box P: particles touching one another in neat, repeating rows; each particle jiggles in place.
Box Q: particles touching one another, arranged randomly, filling only the bottom part of the box; particles slide past one another.
Box R: a few particles spread far apart through the whole box, each moving in a straight line until it hits another particle or a wall.
Box S: particles touching one another in a jumbled, non-repeating arrangement; each particle jiggles in place but none moves out of position.

Answer

P: solid, crystalline (packed, vibrating in place, in a regular repeating pattern). Q: liquid (touching but moving past one another, with a definite volume that sits at the bottom of the container). R: gas (far apart, fast, filling the container). S: solid, amorphous (fixed positions, like a solid, but no repeating pattern, like glass).

Problem 1.23

Some patients who need oxygen at home use a liquid oxygen system: a reservoir holds very cold liquid O2, which warms and turns back into a gas before the patient breathes it. A respiratory therapist explains that a liquid oxygen reservoir holds far more oxygen than a gas cylinder of the same size. (a) Explain why, using the particle view. (b) Is the change from liquid oxygen to oxygen gas a physical or a chemical change? Name it.

Answer

(a) In liquid oxygen the O2 molecules are touching. In oxygen gas they are far apart with mostly empty space between them. The same number of molecules therefore takes up far less space as a liquid (when liquid oxygen turns to gas at room temperature, it takes up more than 800 times as much volume), so a small reservoir can store a lot of oxygen.

(b) Physical change: vaporization (liquid to gas). The molecules are still O2; only their spacing and motion change.

Problem 1.24

When you breathe in, your diaphragm (the dome-shaped muscle under your lungs) contracts and moves down, making the chest cavity larger. The air already in the lungs spreads out to fill the larger space. Which statement explains this?

A. Gases have a definite volume, so the air keeps the same volume.
B. Gases have no definite volume; their particles are far apart and move freely, so they spread to fill any space available.
C. Gas particles are touching, so they push each other into the new space.
D. The gas particles themselves get bigger when the lungs expand.

Answer

B. A gas has no definite volume. Its fast-moving particles spread out to fill whatever space they have.

  • A is wrong: a definite volume is a property of solids and liquids, not gases.
  • C is wrong: gas particles are far apart, not touching.
  • D is wrong: the particles do not change size; the space between them changes.

1.4 Electric Charge

Problem 1.25

Which pair of particles repels?

A. Mg2+ and Cl−
B. a proton and an electron
C. F− and O2−
D. Li+ and Br−

Answer

C. Both particles are negative, and like charges repel. (The sizes of the charges differ, but only the signs decide attract or repel.)

  • A: positive and negative attract.
  • B: a proton is positive and an electron is negative, so they attract.
  • D: positive and negative attract.

Problem 1.26

The table below describes three pairs of oppositely charged ions. Rank the pairs from strongest attraction to weakest, and say which pair would take the most energy to pull apart.

Three ion pairs: charges and distances

Answer

Change one thing at a time. Pairs 1 and 2 are at the same distance, and pair 2 has larger charges, so pair 2 is stronger than pair 1. Pairs 1 and 3 have the same charges, and pair 1 is closer, so pair 1 is stronger than pair 3.

Ranking: 2 > 1 > 3. Pair 2, with the strongest attraction, takes the most energy to pull apart.

Problem 1.27

For each event, say whether energy must be put in or energy is released.

(a) A K+ ion is pulled away from a Cl− ion.
(b) An electron moves closer to a proton.
(c) Two Na+ ions are pushed closer together.
(d) Two electrons that were held close together are allowed to fly apart.

Answer

(a) Energy put in: separating opposite charges works against their attraction. (b) Energy released: opposite charges coming together is "downhill." (c) Energy put in: forcing like charges together works against their repulsion. (d) Energy released: like charges moving apart is the reverse of (c), so it releases energy.

Problem 1.28

Each proton carries one unit of positive charge and each electron carries one unit of negative charge. Find the net charge of each particle and say whether it is neutral or an ion.

(a) 9 protons and 9 electrons
(b) 12 protons and 10 electrons
(c) 17 protons and 18 electrons

Answer

Net charge = (number of protons) − (number of electrons).

(a) 9 − 9 = 0: neutral. (b) 12 − 10 = +2: an ion with a 2+ charge (more positive than negative). (c) 17 − 18 = −1: an ion with a 1− charge (one extra electron).

Problem 1.29

A Na+ ion and a Cl− ion are a certain distance apart. Which single change makes the attraction between the two particles stronger?

A. Move the two ions farther apart.
B. Replace Na+ with Mg2+, keeping the same distance.
C. Replace Cl− with a second Na+.
D. Replace both ions with neutral atoms.

Answer

B. At the same distance, a larger charge gives a stronger attraction, and 2+ is larger than 1+.

  • A weakens the attraction, because the force gets weaker as charges move apart.
  • C gives two positive ions, which repel instead of attract.
  • D removes the net charges, so there is no attraction between ions at all.

Problem 1.30

The inside of a resting nerve cell is slightly negative compared with the fluid outside it. Considering only the electric charges: (a) Is a Na+ ion outside the cell pulled toward the inside or pushed away? (b) What about a chloride ion, Cl−, outside the cell? (c) The cell uses pumps to move Na+ from inside to outside. Does moving a positive ion away from the negative interior take energy or release it?

Answer

(a) Pulled in. Na+ is positive and the interior is negative; opposite charges attract. This is the pull that makes Na+ rush in when a nerve fires.

(b) Pushed away. Cl− and the negative interior have like charges, so the electric force alone repels it.

(c) Takes energy. Moving a positive charge away from a negative region is separating opposite charges, which requires an energy input. That is one reason nerve cells spend so much of their energy (as ATP) running these pumps.

Problem 1.31

In an X-ray tube, a heated filament (the cathode) releases electrons, and a large voltage makes the target (the anode) strongly positive. The radiographer sets this voltage as the kVp (peak kilovoltage) on the control panel. (a) Why do the electrons travel from the cathode to the anode? (b) If the anode were made negative instead, what would happen to the electrons? (c) Explain why raising the kVp gives the electrons more energy when they hit the anode.

Answer

(a) Electrons are negative and the anode is positive; opposite charges attract, so the electrons are pulled toward the anode.

(b) Electrons and a negative anode have like charges, so they would repel. The electrons would be pushed away from the target instead of slamming into it, and no X-rays would be produced at the target.

(c) A higher kVp makes the anode more strongly positive compared with the cathode, so the pull on each electron is stronger. The electrons speed up more on the way across and arrive with more energy, which produces more penetrating X-rays.

Problem 1.32

Some Central Valley vineyards and orchards use electrostatic sprayers, which give each tiny spray droplet a small negative charge as it leaves the nozzle. (a) Why do the charged droplets spread out into an even cloud instead of clumping together? (b) The surface of the plant, which is connected to the ground, takes on a slight positive charge on the side facing the cloud of droplets. Why does this help the droplets coat the leaves, even on their undersides?

Answer

(a) Every droplet carries the same (negative) charge, and like charges repel, so the droplets push away from each other and spread out evenly.

(b) The negative droplets and the slightly positive plant surface have opposite charges, which attract. The droplets are pulled toward the leaves and can curve around to stick to hidden surfaces, so less spray drifts away or lands on the ground. The attraction is strongest when the droplets get close to the leaves.

1.5 Physical and Chemical Properties and Changes

Problem 1.33

Which of these is a chemical property?

A. Isopropyl alcohol evaporates quickly at room temperature.
B. Iron tends to rust in moist air.
C. Copper conducts electricity.
D. Glucose dissolves in water.

Answer

B. Rusting turns iron into a new substance (iron oxide), so the tendency to rust describes how iron changes into something else: a chemical property.

  • A is a physical property: evaporating alcohol is still alcohol, now as a gas.
  • C is a physical property: electricity passes through copper, and it is still copper afterward.
  • D is a physical property: dissolving is a physical change, and evaporating the water gives the glucose back.

Problem 1.34

Classify each change as physical or chemical, and give your reason.

(a) Chewing breaks a cracker into small pieces.
(b) Amylase, an enzyme in saliva (a protein that speeds up a reaction), breaks the starch in the cracker into smaller sugar molecules.
(c) A pharmacy dissolves dextrose in sterile water to make an IV fluid.
(d) A wooden match burns.
(e) An autoclave (a pressurized steam sterilizer used in clinics and labs) turns water into steam.
(f) Chlorine bleach removes the color from a coffee stain.

Answer

(a) Physical: smaller pieces of the same substances. (b) Chemical: starch becomes new substances (sugars). (c) Physical: dissolving; evaporating the water would give the dextrose back. (d) Chemical: wood burns into new substances (carbon dioxide, water vapor, ash), with heat and light given off. (e) Physical: vaporization; the steam is still H2O. (f) Chemical: the bleach reacts with the colored substances in the stain, turning them into new, colorless substances.

Problem 1.35

A lab notebook has five notes about magnesium. Label each as a physical property, a chemical property, a physical change, or a chemical change.

(a) Magnesium is a shiny, silvery-gray metal.
(b) A strip of magnesium burned with a blinding white light and left a white powder.
(c) Magnesium melts at 650 °C.
(d) Magnesium reacts with acids to produce hydrogen gas.
(e) A magnesium ribbon was bent into a coil.

Answer

First decide "property" (a description of what the substance is or can do) or "change" (an event that happened). Then decide physical or chemical by whether a new substance forms.

(a) Physical property: appearance, observed without changing the metal. (b) Chemical change: an event in which a new substance (the white powder) formed, with light given off. (c) Physical property: a measured description; melting does not change what the substance is. (d) Chemical property: describes a reaction magnesium can undergo, forming new substances. (e) Physical change: an event, but the coil is still magnesium.

Problem 1.36

The table below lists six events. Name the change of state in each, and say whether any of them is a chemical change.

Six everyday changes of state
EventDescription
(a)Mothballs slowly shrink in a closed closet without ever becoming wet.
(b)Droplets form on a bathroom mirror during a hot shower.
(c)Liquid nitrogen poured into a cup in a dermatology clinic boils away.
(d)On a freezing February night in a Valley orchard, frost forms directly on almond blossoms from water vapor in the air.

Answer

(a) Sublimation (solid → gas; no liquid ever appears). (b) Condensation (water vapor → liquid). (c) Vaporization, here boiling (liquid → gas). (d) Deposition (gas → solid, skipping the liquid). (e) Freezing (liquid → solid). (f) Melting (solid → liquid).

None is a chemical change. In every change of state, the particles stay the same; only their spacing and motion change.

Problem 1.37

The large bubbles that rise in a pot of water at a full, rolling boil are mostly:

A. hydrogen gas and oxygen gas
B. air
C. water vapor
D. heat

Answer

C. Boiling is vaporization: liquid water turns into gaseous water (H2O) inside the liquid, and those pockets of vapor rise as bubbles. It is a physical change.

  • A would require breaking water molecules apart, a chemical change that boiling does not cause.
  • B is a common mix-up: the tiny bubbles that appear early, as the water first warms, are dissolved air coming out, but the big bubbles of a rolling boil are water vapor.
  • D is wrong because heat is energy, not matter; it cannot fill a bubble.

Problem 1.38

Which pair contains two intensive properties?

A. mass and density
B. boiling point and color
C. volume and melting point
D. mass and volume

Answer

B. Boiling point and color are the same for a drop or a drum of a substance, so neither depends on the amount.

  • A: density is intensive, but mass is extensive (more sample, more mass).
  • C: melting point is intensive, but volume is extensive.
  • D: both are extensive.

Problem 1.39

A lab technician has two unlabeled bottles, each holding 50.0 mL of a clear, colorless liquid. She can measure any of these: volume, mass, boiling point, density, color. Which measurements could help identify the liquids? Explain.

Answer

Boiling point and density. Both are intensive properties that are characteristic of a substance, so they can be compared with values in a reference table.

  • Volume and mass are extensive: they depend on how much liquid is in the bottle, not on what it is. (Mass is useful as one step, because mass divided by volume gives density.)
  • Color is intensive, but both liquids are colorless, so it cannot tell them apart.

Problem 1.40

A dental hygienist places a sealant (a thin protective coating) on the chewing surface of a molar. The sealant goes on as a thick liquid resin, and a curing light (a bright blue light) makes it harden in seconds as its small molecules link together into a hard solid. The hardened sealant does not turn back into a liquid when warmed. (a) Is curing a physical or a chemical change? (b) Compare it with candle wax hardening as it cools.

Answer

(a) Chemical change. The resin's molecules bond together into a new substance with different properties (hard, not runny), and warming does not bring back the original liquid.

(b) Wax hardening is a physical change (freezing). The wax molecules are the same before and after, and warming the wax melts it back into a liquid. Both changes turn a liquid into a solid, so "it became solid" is not enough to decide; the question is whether new substances formed.

Problem 1.41

Classify each observation as a sign of a physical or a chemical change, and explain.

(a) An effervescent (fizzing) tablet dropped into water releases a stream of gas bubbles, though the water is not being heated.
(b) Two clear IV medications run through the same line form a cloudy white solid.
(c) The sugar coating of a tablet dissolves in the mouth.

Answer

(a) Chemical. A gas forming without boiling is a sign of a chemical change. Ingredients in the tablet react with each other once wet and produce a new substance, carbon dioxide gas.

(b) Chemical (a sign of one). A solid forming when two solutions are mixed is a precipitate, one of the clues of a chemical change. Medications that react this way are called incompatible, which is one reason nurses check IV compatibility and follow pharmacy guidance and facility policy before giving drugs through the same line.

(c) Physical. Dissolving spreads the sugar among water molecules; no new substance forms.

Problem 1.42

A Central Valley dairy runs these steps. Classify each as a physical or chemical change.

(a) Fresh milk is chilled from body temperature to about 4 °C.
(b) A centrifuge separates cream from the milk.
(c) Bacteria added to milk turn lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid, thickening it into yogurt.
(d) In a covered manure lagoon (a digester), microbes that live without oxygen break down manure and give off methane gas.
(e) The methane is burned in an engine to generate electricity.

Answer

(a) Physical: only the temperature changes; it is still milk. (b) Physical: separating a mixture by density does not change any substance. (c) Chemical: lactose becomes a new substance, lactic acid. (d) Chemical: manure is broken down into new substances, including methane. (e) Chemical: burning turns methane and oxygen into carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy.

Steps (c) and (d) are both done by living microbes; much of the chemistry of life, from yogurt to digestion, is chemical change.

1.6 Density

Problem 1.43

A clinical lab measures 10.0 mL of a urine sample and finds that it has a mass of 10.2 g. (a) Calculate its density. (b) Why is the sample denser than pure water (1.00 g/mL)?

Answer

(a) \( d = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{10.2\ \text{g}}{10.0\ \text{mL}} = 1.02\ \text{g/mL} \)

(b) Urine is mostly water, but it also carries dissolved substances such as salts and urea. Those dissolved particles add mass without adding much volume, so a milliliter of urine has a little more mass than a milliliter of pure water. (Clinicians often report this as specific gravity, the density of the sample compared with that of water.)

Check: the mass in grams is slightly more than the volume in milliliters, so the density should be slightly more than 1 g/mL, and it is.

Problem 1.44

A radiologic technology student tests a small, flat sample cut from an old X-ray shielding sheet. The sample has a volume of 3.00 cm3 and a mass of 33.9 g. Calculate its density and use the density table in Section 1.6 to identify the metal.

Answer

\( d = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{33.9\ \text{g}}{3.00\ \text{cm}^3} = 11.3\ \text{g/cm}^3 \)

That matches lead (11.3 g/cm3). Lead's high density is one reason it has long been used to block X-rays in shielding and protective aprons. Because 1 cm3 = 1 mL, this is the same as 11.3 g/mL.

Problem 1.45

A student finds the volume of an irregular metal bolt by water displacement: she reads the water level in a graduated cylinder, slides the bolt in, and reads the level again. The rise in the water level equals the bolt's volume. Her data are in the table below.

Water displacement data for a metal bolt
MeasurementValue
Mass of bolt33.8 g
Water level before adding the bolt25.0 mL
Water level after adding the bolt37.5 mL

(a) Find the volume of the bolt. (b) Calculate its density. (c) Use the Section 1.6 table to suggest what metal it is.

Answer

(a) Volume = 37.5 mL − 25.0 mL = 12.5 mL.

(b) \( d = \dfrac{33.8\ \text{g}}{12.5\ \text{mL}} = 2.70\ \text{g/mL} \) (the calculator shows 2.704; keep three digits).

(c) Aluminum (2.70 g/mL). Check: the bolt is denser than water, which fits with it sinking to the bottom of the cylinder.

Problem 1.46

A teaching lab pours four liquids that do not mix into one tall cylinder, then drops in four small objects. The densities are in the table below.

Densities of the liquids and objects in a density column
MaterialTypeDensity (g/mL)
Honeyliquid1.42
Vegetable oilliquid0.92
Dish soapliquid1.06
Waterliquid1.00
Bead Xsolid0.95
Bead Ysolid1.20
Bead Zsolid1.50
Corksolid0.24

(a) List the liquid layers from top to bottom. (b) Where does each object come to rest?

Answer

(a) Least dense on top: vegetable oil (0.92), water (1.00), dish soap (1.06), honey (1.42) on the bottom.

(b) Each object sinks through every liquid less dense than itself and floats on the first liquid denser than itself.

  • Bead X (0.95): denser than oil, less dense than water, so it rests between the oil and water layers.
  • Bead Y (1.20): denser than oil, water, and soap, less dense than honey, so it rests between the soap and honey layers.
  • Bead Z (1.50): denser than every liquid, so it sinks to the bottom.
  • Cork (0.24): less dense than every liquid, so it floats on top of the oil.

Problem 1.47

In winter, many Valley almond and stone-fruit growers spray dormant trees with a horticultural oil mixed into water. A pest-control adviser measures 20.0 mL of the oil and finds a mass of 17.0 g. (a) Calculate the oil's density. (b) If the spray tank sits without stirring, where will the oil end up? (c) Is the oil-and-water spray a homogeneous or heterogeneous mixture, and why must the tank be kept stirred?

Answer

(a) \( d = \dfrac{17.0\ \text{g}}{20.0\ \text{mL}} = 0.850\ \text{g/mL} \)

(b) 0.850 g/mL is less than water's 1.00 g/mL, so the oil rises and floats on top of the water.

(c) It is a heterogeneous mixture: oil and water do not dissolve in each other, so they form separate regions. Stirring keeps the oil spread through the water so each part of the orchard gets the same mix; without it, the first trees sprayed would get mostly water and the last ones mostly oil.

Problem 1.48

A copper block is cut exactly in half. Which statement about one of the halves is correct?

A. Its density is half the density of the original block.
B. Its mass and volume are each half of the original, and its density is unchanged.
C. Its density is twice the density of the original block.
D. Its mass is half of the original, but its volume is unchanged.

Answer

B. Mass and volume are extensive, so both are cut in half. Density is mass ÷ volume, and halving both leaves the ratio the same: density is intensive.

  • A and C confuse density with an extensive property; the substance is still copper, so its density stays the same.
  • D is wrong: a half block takes up half the space, so its volume also halves.

Problem 1.49

Two people have their body density measured by underwater weighing. Person A has a whole-body density of 1.04 g/mL and person B has a whole-body density of 1.07 g/mL. Fat tissue has a density of about 0.90 g/mL and lean tissue about 1.10 g/mL. (a) Which person has the higher percentage of body fat? (b) Which person floats more easily in a pool?

Answer

(a) Person A. Whole-body density falls between the densities of fat and lean tissue, and more fat pulls it closer to 0.90 g/mL. Person A's lower density (1.04 versus 1.07 g/mL) means a larger share of fat.

(b) Person A. A lower density is closer to (and, with a breath of air in the lungs, can drop below) water's 1.00 g/mL, and an object less dense than water floats.

Problem 1.50

Which object will float in water (1.00 g/mL)?

A. mass 12.0 g, volume 10.0 mL
B. mass 9.50 g, volume 10.0 mL
C. mass 25.5 g, volume 25.0 mL
D. mass 5.00 g, volume 4.00 mL

Answer

B. Calculate each density and compare with 1.00 g/mL:

  • A: 12.0 g ÷ 10.0 mL = 1.20 g/mL, denser than water, so it sinks.
  • B: 9.50 g ÷ 10.0 mL = 0.950 g/mL, less dense than water, so it floats.
  • C: 25.5 g ÷ 25.0 mL = 1.02 g/mL, slightly denser than water, so it sinks (slowly).
  • D: 5.00 g ÷ 4.00 mL = 1.25 g/mL, denser than water, so it sinks.

Notice that C has the largest mass and B is not the lightest object; floating depends on density, not on mass alone.

Mixed and Integrative Practice

Problem 1.51

A science-night demonstrator drops a chunk of dry ice (solid CO2, density about 1.5 g/cm3) into a bowl of warm water. It bubbles, and a thick white fog rolls over the rim of the bowl.

First, the white fog is mostly:

A. carbon dioxide gas
B. tiny droplets of liquid water condensed from water vapor in the air that the very cold CO2 gas chilled
C. smoke from the dry ice burning
D. hydrogen and oxygen gas from water breaking apart

Then answer: (e) Does the dry ice sink or float? (f) Name the change of state the dry ice undergoes. (g) Classify CO2 as an element, compound, or mixture. (h) Are the bubbles evidence of a chemical change?

Answer

B. Carbon dioxide gas is colorless and invisible. The very cold CO2 gas chills the moist air above the bowl, and the water vapor condenses into tiny droplets that you see as fog (the same physical change that makes tule fog).

  • A is wrong: CO2 gas is invisible, so it cannot be the white fog.
  • C is wrong: nothing is burning; there is no flame and no new substance.
  • D is wrong: breaking water into hydrogen and oxygen is a chemical change that warm water and dry ice do not cause.

(e) Sinks: about 1.5 g/cm3 is greater than water's 1.00 g/mL (1 cm3 = 1 mL).

(f) Sublimation (solid → gas), a physical change.

(g) Compound: carbon and oxygen bonded in a fixed 1:2 ratio.

(h) No. The bubbles are CO2 gas from the dry ice subliming, and the CO2 was already there as a solid. A gas forming is only a clue to a chemical change; here no new substance forms.

Problem 1.52

Heliox is a breathing gas made of helium and oxygen, commonly 80% helium and 20% oxygen, though 70:30 blends are also used. Respiratory therapists sometimes deliver it, on a provider's order, to patients with a narrowed airway, because a less dense gas flows through a narrow passage more easily. Near room temperature, 80:20 heliox has a density of about 0.40 g/L, and air about 1.2 g/L.

(a) Classify helium, oxygen, and heliox. (b) What fact in the description shows heliox is a mixture? (c) Which is less dense, heliox or air? (d) Why are the densities of gases given in g/L instead of g/mL? (e) Is density an intensive or extensive property, and would 1 L and 10 L of the same heliox blend have the same density?

Answer

(a) Helium: element (exists as single He atoms). Oxygen: element (diatomic O2). Heliox: homogeneous mixture of two elements (the gases are spread evenly through each other).

(b) It comes in different proportions (80:20 or 70:30). A mixture's composition can vary; a compound's cannot.

(c) Heliox: 0.40 g/L is about one-third of air's 1.2 g/L.

(d) Gas particles are far apart, so a milliliter of gas has a tiny mass. Using liters gives numbers that are easier to read (1.2 g/L instead of 0.0012 g/mL).

(e) Density is intensive, so 1 L and 10 L of the same blend at the same temperature and pressure have the same density; the 10 L sample has ten times the mass and ten times the volume.

Problem 1.53

A piece of pure copper is drawn into a thin wire. When the wire is held in a flame, its surface turns black as copper combines with oxygen from the air. A different piece of the same copper has a mass of 44.8 g and a volume of 5.00 mL.

(a) Classify copper as an element, compound, or mixture. (b) Is drawing copper into wire a physical or a chemical change? (c) Is the black coating that forms in the flame a sign of a physical or a chemical change? (d) Calculate the density of the copper. (e) What would the density of a piece twice as large be?

Answer

(a) Element: pure copper contains only Cu atoms.

(b) Physical change: the shape changes, but it is still copper.

(c) Chemical change: copper and oxygen combine into a new black substance, a compound of copper and oxygen. The new color is a clue, and a new substance really did form.

(d) \( d = \dfrac{44.8\ \text{g}}{5.00\ \text{mL}} = 8.96\ \text{g/mL} \)

(e) Still 8.96 g/mL. Doubling the piece doubles both mass and volume; density is intensive.

Problem 1.54

A student finds an unlabeled bottle of clear liquid in the stockroom and forms the hypothesis "This liquid is ethanol (density 0.789 g/mL)." She measures three different volumes of the liquid. Her data are in the table below.

Mass and volume of three samples of an unknown liquid
TrialVolume (mL)Mass (g)
120.015.8
215.011.8
325.019.7

(a) Calculate the density for each trial. (b) Do the data support her hypothesis? (c) The volumes were different, so why are the densities nearly the same? (d) Do the data prove the liquid is ethanol? Suggest one more physical property she could measure.

Answer

(a) Trial 1: 15.8 g ÷ 20.0 mL = 0.790 g/mL. Trial 2: 11.8 g ÷ 15.0 mL = 0.787 g/mL (calculator: 0.78667). Trial 3: 19.7 g ÷ 25.0 mL = 0.788 g/mL.

(b) Yes. All three values are within 0.002 g/mL of ethanol's 0.789 g/mL.

(c) Density is intensive. A larger sample has proportionally more mass and more volume, so the ratio stays the same. The small differences come from normal measurement limits.

(d) No. The data support the hypothesis but do not prove it; another liquid could have a similar density. Measuring a second intensive property, such as the boiling point (ethanol boils at 78 °C), would be a stronger test. If both match, she can be much more confident. This is the scientific method at work: test a hypothesis more than one way.
