Learning Objectives

  • Predict whether two charged particles attract or repel.
  • Describe how the size of the charges and the distance between them change the strength of the attraction or repulsion.
  • Explain why pulling opposite charges apart takes energy and letting them come together releases energy.
  • Recognize that an object with equal positive and negative charge is electrically neutral.

Rub a balloon on your hair and it clings to the wall; pull a sweater off on a dry Valley winter day and you may hear it crackle. Both are signs of electric charge, a property of matter that causes pushes and pulls between particles. Charge is the reason atoms hold together, the reason table salt is a hard crystal, and the reason water dissolves so many things. You need just a few rules about charge to understand most of this course.

Two Kinds of Charge

There are two kinds of electric charge, called positive (+) and negative (−). The rule for how they interact is short (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)):

  • Like charges repel: + pushes away +, and − pushes away −.
  • Opposite charges attract: + and − pull toward each other.

An object with equal amounts of positive and negative charge has a net charge of zero; it is electrically neutral. Most everyday matter is neutral. Inside every atom (Chapter 2) are positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons; a neutral atom has equal numbers of each. An atom that gains or loses electrons becomes a charged particle called an ion. A static shock happens when electrons rub off one object onto another, leaving one slightly negative and the other slightly positive.

How Strong Is the Force?

Two things control how strongly two charged particles attract or repel:

  1. The size of the charges. Bigger charges give a stronger force. A 2+ particle and a 2− particle attract more strongly than a 1+ and a 1− at the same distance.
  2. The distance between them. The force weakens quickly as the particles move apart and strengthens as they come closer.

You will use these two ideas again and again: to explain why the electrons closest to a nucleus are held most tightly (Chapter 3), why some ionic compounds melt at much higher temperatures than others (Chapter 6), and why water dissolves salts (Chapter 11).

Charge and energy

Because opposite charges attract, it takes energy to pull them apart, the way it takes effort to pull two magnets apart. When opposite charges are allowed to come together, energy is released. The reverse is true for like charges: forcing two + charges together takes energy. This idea, that attraction is "downhill" in energy, explains why atoms form bonds and why forming bonds releases energy (Chapters 6 and 8).

Professor Tip

When you compare forces, change one thing at a time. Same distance? The pair with the larger charges wins. Same charges? The pair that is closer wins. If both change, you will be told which matters more, or the question is about direction (attract or repel) only.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Attract or repel?

Problem. Predict whether each pair attracts or repels: (a) a sodium ion, Na+, and a chloride ion, Cl−; (b) two electrons; (c) a calcium ion, Ca2+, and a sodium ion, Na+; (d) a proton and an electron.

Solution. (a) + and −: attract. (b) − and −: repel. (c) + and + (the sizes differ, but both are positive): repel. (d) + and −: attract.

Check. Only the signs decide the direction. Each pair with matching signs repels; each pair with opposite signs attracts.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Which attraction is stronger?

Problem. Two pairs of ions are held at the same distance apart. Pair A is Na+ and Cl−. Pair B is Mg2+ and O2−. Which pair attracts more strongly?

Solution. The distance is the same, so compare the sizes of the charges. Pair B has charges of 2+ and 2−, larger than the 1+ and 1− of pair A, so pair B attracts more strongly.

Check. This matches what is observed: magnesium oxide melts at about 2800 °C, while sodium chloride melts at about 800 °C. Stronger attraction between ions means more energy is needed to pull them apart.

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

Every nerve impulse depends on separated charges. A resting nerve cell keeps more sodium ions (Na+) outside its membrane and more potassium ions (K+) inside, and the inside of the cell ends up slightly negative compared with the outside. This charge difference across the membrane is the resting membrane potential, about −70 millivolts in a typical neuron. When the nerve fires, channels open and Na+ rushes in, drawn by the negative interior: opposite charges attract. Keeping the charges separated costs energy; the cell spends a large share of its ATP (its energy currency) running pumps that move Na+ out and K+ back in.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

An X-ray tube is a machine built on "opposite charges attract." Inside a sealed glass or metal tube, a heated wire filament (the cathode) releases electrons. A very large voltage, often tens of thousands of volts and commonly 60 to 120 kilovolts (kVp) for diagnostic images, makes the target at the other end (the anode) strongly positive compared with the cathode. The negatively charged electrons are pulled toward the positive anode, speeding up as they go, and when they slam into the anode, part of their energy comes out as X-rays. Raising the kVp increases the attraction, so the electrons arrive with more energy and produce more penetrating X-rays. Radiographers set kVp for each exam because of this relationship.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking two positive particles attract if one is "more positive." Any two positive charges repel.
  • Forgetting distance. The same two charges attract much more strongly when they are close together.
  • Thinking it takes energy to bring opposite charges together. It is the other way around: separating them takes energy; bringing them together releases it.

Summary

  • There are two kinds of charge. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.
  • Larger charges and shorter distances give stronger forces.
  • Separating opposite charges takes energy; letting them come together releases energy.
  • Equal positive and negative charge makes an object neutral. Atoms contain positive protons and negative electrons; ions are atoms with a net charge.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Will a fluoride ion, F−, attract or repel (a) a potassium ion, K+; (b) a chloride ion, Cl−?

Hint

Opposite signs attract; like signs repel. Compare the sign of F− with each ion.

Answer

(a) attract (opposite signs); (b) repel (both negative).

K+ has the opposite sign to F−, so they attract; Cl− has the same negative sign as F−, so they repel.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A + charge and a − charge are moved from 2 nm apart to 1 nm apart. Does the attraction get stronger or weaker? Was energy released or absorbed as they moved closer?

Hint

The closer opposite charges are, the stronger the attraction. Is moving opposite charges together "downhill" or "uphill" in energy?

Answer

Stronger, because they are closer. Energy was released, because opposite charges came together.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

At the same distance, which pair attracts more strongly: K+ with Cl−, or Ca2+ with O2−?

Hint

At the same distance, larger charges give a stronger attraction. Compare the sizes of the charges in each pair.

Answer

Ca2+ with O2−: at the same distance, larger charges give a stronger attraction.
