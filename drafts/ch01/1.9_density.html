Learning Objectives

  • Define density as mass per unit volume and explain it with the particle view.
  • Calculate density from a measured mass and volume (d = m/V).
  • Predict whether an object floats or sinks, and the order of liquid layers, from densities.
  • Explain why density is an intensive physical property that can help identify a substance.

Oil floats on vinegar in salad dressing, a steel nut drops straight to the bottom of a glass of water, and a person with more body fat floats more easily in a pool. All three come down to one physical property: density, how much mass is packed into a given volume.

What Density Means

Density is the mass of a substance divided by its volume:

\( d = \dfrac{m}{V} \qquad \text{density} = \dfrac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} \)

For liquids and solids, density is usually given in grams per milliliter (g/mL) or grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm3); these are the same, because 1 mL = 1 cm3. Gases are so spread out that their densities are usually given in grams per liter (g/L).

In the particle view, density depends on how heavy the particles are and how tightly they are packed. Gas particles are far apart, so gases have very low densities. For most substances the solid is a bit denser than the liquid. Water is a famous exception: ice (about 0.92 g/mL) is less dense than liquid water (1.00 g/mL), which is why ice floats and lakes freeze from the top down.

Density is an intensive property (Section 1.8): a gram of aluminum and a kilogram of aluminum have the same density, 2.70 g/cm3. That makes density useful for identifying a substance. Density also changes a little with temperature, because most substances expand when warmed, so tables list the temperature at which each density was measured.

Floating, Sinking, and Layers

An object floats in a liquid if it is less dense than the liquid and sinks if it is more dense. Liquids that do not mix stack in layers, with the densest on the bottom (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Calculate a density

Problem. A student measures 10.0 mL of an unknown clear liquid in a graduated cylinder. The liquid has a mass of 7.89 g. What is its density? Using the table above, what might the liquid be?

Solution.

\( d = \dfrac{m}{V} = \dfrac{7.89\ \text{g}}{10.0\ \text{mL}} = 0.789\ \text{g/mL} \)

The density matches ethanol (0.789 g/mL).

Check. The mass in grams is a little less than the volume in milliliters, so the density should be a little less than 1 g/mL, and it is. The units are g/mL, as a density should be. (Both measurements have three significant figures, so the answer keeps three; see Section 1.3.)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Float or sink?

Problem. A small plastic bead has a mass of 2.10 g and a volume of 2.00 mL. Will it float or sink in (a) water, (b) corn syrup?

Solution. First find the bead's density:

\( d = \dfrac{2.10\ \text{g}}{2.00\ \text{mL}} = 1.05\ \text{g/mL} \)

(a) 1.05 g/mL is greater than water's 1.00 g/mL, so the bead sinks in water. (b) 1.05 g/mL is less than corn syrup's 1.37 g/mL, so it floats on corn syrup. In Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\), that is why the bead (P) rests between the water and syrup layers.

Check. Compare densities directly: denser than the liquid means sink; less dense means float.

Professor Tip

Write the formula with units every time: d = m/V, so g ÷ mL gives g/mL. If you ever divide volume by mass you will get mL/g, a sure sign the fraction is upside down. In Section 1.9.1 you will also use density "backwards," as a conversion factor between mass and volume; the units will tell you which way to flip it.

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

Body tissues have different densities. Fat tissue is less dense than water (about 0.90 g/mL), while lean tissue (muscle, bone, organs taken together) is denser (about 1.10 g/mL). That difference is the idea behind hydrostatic (underwater) weighing, a classic way to estimate body composition: a person is weighed on land and again fully under water, and their whole-body density is calculated from the two masses. A lower body density means a higher percentage of body fat. It is also why people with more body fat float more easily. Whole blood (about 1.06 g/mL) is slightly denser than water because it carries cells and dissolved proteins, and red blood cells are denser still, which is why they settle to the bottom of a centrifuged blood tube (Section 1.5).

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

On an X-ray image, bone looks white and the air-filled lungs look dark. Part of the reason is density. X-rays pass easily through low-density matter such as air (0.0012 g/mL), so more of them reach the detector and the area looks dark (radiolucent). Bone is far denser and also contains calcium, a heavier element than the carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen of soft tissue, so it absorbs more X-rays and looks bright (radiopaque). Soft tissues such as muscle and fat, with densities close to water's, show up in shades of gray in between. Radiographers adjust exposure settings for thicker or denser body parts for the same reason.

In the Lab

In the Making Measurements experiment you find the density of a metal bar two ways. First you measure its length, width, and height with a ruler and calculate its volume (V = l × w × h); then you find its volume again by water displacement: the rise in the water level in a graduated cylinder when the bar slides in. Record every digit the analytical balance shows, and read the ruler and the cylinder one digit past the smallest marking (Section 1.3). Comparing the two densities tells you how precise your measurements were, and comparing them with the known value tells you how accurate they were.

Common Mistakes

  • Dividing volume by mass. Density is mass ÷ volume; check that your answer is in g/mL.
  • Thinking heavier objects always sink. A large log is heavy but floats, because its density is less than water's. Compare densities, not masses.
  • Thinking a bigger piece of a substance is denser. Density is intensive; cutting a sample in half halves both mass and volume and leaves the density unchanged.

Summary

  • Density is mass per unit volume: d = m/V, usually in g/mL or g/cm3 for liquids and solids and g/L for gases.
  • Objects less dense than a liquid float in it; denser objects sink. Liquids that do not mix layer with the densest at the bottom.
  • Density is an intensive physical property that helps identify substances. Gases have far lower densities than liquids and solids.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A metal cube has a mass of 54.0 g and a volume of 20.0 cm3. Calculate its density and use the table to identify the metal.

Answer

d = 54.0 g ÷ 20.0 cm3 = 2.70 g/cm3, which matches aluminum.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Vegetable oil (about 0.92 g/mL) and corn syrup (about 1.37 g/mL) are poured into a cylinder of water, and none of the three liquids mix. What is the order of layers from top to bottom? Where would a piece of cork (about 0.24 g/mL) end up?

Answer

Least dense on top: vegetable oil, then water, then corn syrup on the bottom. The cork is less dense than all three, so it floats on top of the oil.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A 500. g block and a 5.00 g chip are cut from the same piece of oak. Which has the greater density?

Answer

Neither: both are oak, and density is intensive, so it does not depend on the size of the sample.
