Learning Objectives

  • Explain, with a line, a square, and a cube, why 1 m2 = 104 cm2 and 1 m3 = 106 cm3.
  • Build a squared or cubed conversion factor by raising both the number and the unit to the power.
  • Use 1 mL = 1 cm3 = 1 cc and 1 L = 1000 cm3 to move between volume units.
  • Convert areas and volumes, including body surface area, wound area, and container volumes.

Squared and cubed units are everywhere once you look for them. The floor of a hospital room is measured in square feet, a wound in square centimeters, and a patient's body surface area in square meters. Every syringe is marked in milliliters, which are really cubic centimeters. Converting these units is the hardest kind of conversion in this chapter, because the factors you need are not printed on your reference sheet. You have to build them. The good news: if you can do this kind of conversion, you can do any of them.

A Line, a Square, and a Cube

Here is the most common wrong idea about cubed units: "1 m = 100 cm, so 1 m3 must be 100 cm3." Build the shapes and see why it fails.

  • A line. Draw a line 1 m long. Measured in centimeters, it is 100 cm long. So 1 m = 100 cm.
  • A square. Make a square that is 1 m on each side, like a floor tile. Its area is length × width. In meters, 1 m × 1 m = 1 m2. In centimeters, 100 cm × 100 cm = 10,000 cm2. So 1 m2 = 104 cm2, not 100 cm2.
  • A cube. Give the square a height of 1 m. Its volume is length × width × height: 1 m × 1 m × 1 m = 1 m3, or 100 cm × 100 cm × 100 cm = 1,000,000 cm3. So 1 m3 = 106 cm3.

A cubic meter is a box big enough to hold a large washing machine; a cubic centimeter is about the size of a sugar cube. It takes a million sugar cubes, not a hundred, to fill the box.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): How one length equality grows when the unit is squared or cubed
ShapeUnitEqualityConversion factor
Line (length)m, cm1 m = 100 cm\( \dfrac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}} \)
Square (area)m2, cm21 m2 = 104 cm2\( \left(\dfrac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}}\right)^{2} = \dfrac{10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{2}}{1\ \text{m}^{2}} \)
Cube (volume)m3, cm31 m3 = 106 cm3\( \left(\dfrac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}}\right)^{3} = \dfrac{10^{6}\ \text{cm}^{3}}{1\ \text{m}^{3}} \)

The rule hidden in the table: raise the whole conversion factor to the power, the number and the unit. Squaring only the unit (writing 100 cm2/1 m2) is the classic mistake. The same rule works in the other direction: since 1 cm = 10−2 m, 1 cm3 = (10−2)3 m3 = 10−6 m3.

Volume: Liters, Milliliters, and Cubic Centimeters

The liter is defined from a cube: 1 L is the volume of a cube 10 cm on each side, so 1 L = 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm = 1000 cm3. Divide both sides by 1000 and you get the equality that every health-care student should know by heart:

\( 1\ \text{mL} = 1\ \text{cm}^{3} = 1\ \text{cc} \)

A "cc" is just an abbreviation for cubic centimeter. A 3 mL syringe and a 3 cc syringe hold exactly the same volume. Because a liter is 10 cm on a side, a liter is also a cubic decimeter: 1 L = 1 dm3.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Cubic meters to milliliters

Problem. Convert 0.0820 m3 to milliliters. Which answer is right: (A) 8.20 × 10−4 mL, (B) 82 mL, (C) 8.2 mL, (D) 8.20 × 104 mL?

Given: 0.0820 m3. Desired: ??? mL. Strategy: m3 → cm3 (build the cubed factor) → mL (1 cm3 = 1 mL).

Solution.

\( 0.0820\ \text{m}^{3} \times \left(\dfrac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}}\right)^{3} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{cm}^{3}} = 0.0820 \times 10^{6}\ \text{mL} = 8.20 \times 10^{4}\ \text{mL} \)

The answer is (D), 8.20 × 104 mL, with the 3 significant figures of 0.0820 (the factors are exact). (A) divides by 100 instead of multiplying by 106: the factor was flipped and not cubed. (B) treats cubic meters as if they were liters. (C) multiplies by 100, forgetting to cube the factor.

Check. m3 cancels m3 only because the whole factor was cubed. A cubic meter is huge compared with a milliliter, so the number must get much bigger. Also, 0.0820 m3 is 82.0 L (1 m3 = 1000 L), about the volume of a large trash can, and 82.0 L is 82,000 mL.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): A wound measured in square millimeters

Problem. A nurse measures a skin wound as 3.2 cm long and 1.5 cm wide and charts its area as length × width. A wound-care study reports areas in square millimeters. What is the area in mm2?

Given: 3.2 cm and 1.5 cm. Desired: ??? mm2. Strategy: area in cm2, then square the factor 10 mm = 1 cm.

Solution. First the area: 3.2 cm × 1.5 cm = 4.8 cm2 (multiplying the units gives cm2; 2 significant figures). Then:

\( 4.8\ \text{cm}^{2} \times \left(\dfrac{10\ \text{mm}}{1\ \text{cm}}\right)^{2} = 4.8\ \text{cm}^{2} \times \dfrac{100\ \text{mm}^{2}}{1\ \text{cm}^{2}} = 480\ \text{mm}^{2} = 4.8 \times 10^{2}\ \text{mm}^{2} \)

Check. A 1 cm × 1 cm square is 10 mm × 10 mm = 100 mm2, so every cm2 is 100 mm2. The answer, 4.8 × 102 mm2, has 2 significant figures, like the measurements. (Measuring length × width overestimates the area of an oval wound; it is a quick bedside estimate.)

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Cubic inches to liters

Problem. A rectangular plastic tank for soaking lab glassware measures 12.0 in × 8.00 in × 6.50 in on the inside. What volume does it hold, in liters?

Given: three lengths in inches. Desired: ??? L. Strategy: V = l × w × h in in3; then in3 → cm3 (cube 2.54 cm = 1 in) → L (1 L = 1000 cm3).

Solution. V = 12.0 in × 8.00 in × 6.50 in = 624 in3. Then:

\( 624\ \text{in}^{3} \times \left(\dfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}}\right)^{3} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \text{cm}^{3}} = 624 \times 16.387 \div 1000\ \text{L} = 10.23\ \text{L} \to 10.2\ \text{L} \)

Each length has 3 significant figures, and 2.54 is exact, so the answer is 10.2 L.

Check. On the calculator, type 2.54, then the x3 (or ^3) key, to get 16.387 cm3 per in3. Cubing the 2.54 by hand as 2.54 × 3 = 7.62 is a common slip and would give 4.75 L. A box about a foot long holding about 10 L (roughly 2.7 gallons) is reasonable.

Professor Tip

Cubed factors are not on your yellow sheet, so derive them every time: start from the length equality you know (1 m = 100 cm, 1 in = 2.54 cm), put it in parentheses, and put the power outside the parentheses. Write the power on the unit you are canceling too, so you can see m3 cancel m3. If the units do not cancel completely, you forgot to raise something to the power.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Squared and cubed units show up throughout patient care:

  • Syringes and IV fluids. Volumes are marked in mL, and 1 mL = 1 cm3 = 1 cc. You will still hear "cc" spoken, but write mL: the Institute for Safe Medication Practices lists the handwritten "cc" as error-prone, because it can be misread as "u" (units).
  • Body surface area (BSA). Some medications, including many cancer chemotherapy drugs, are dosed per square meter of body surface area (mg/m2) rather than per kilogram. An average adult's BSA is roughly 1.7 to 2.0 m2, calculated from height and weight by a formula.
  • Wounds and burns. Wound size is tracked in cm2 so healing can be measured over time.

In each case, a unit squared or cubed by mistake, or not at all, changes the number by a factor of 100 or 1000 or more. (Context only; this book gives no dosing guidance.)

Common Mistakes

  • Writing 1 m3 = 100 cm3. Cube the whole factor: 1 m3 = (100)3 cm3 = 106 cm3.
  • Raising the unit but not the number (or the number but not the unit). Both go inside the parentheses, and the power goes outside.
  • Multiplying by the power instead of raising to it: 2.543 = 16.387, not 2.54 × 3 = 7.62.
  • Forgetting that mL and cm3 are the same. Once you reach cm3, you are already at mL.

Summary

  • When a unit is squared or cubed, raise the entire conversion factor, number and unit, to the same power.
  • 1 m2 = 104 cm2; 1 m3 = 106 cm3; 1 in3 = 16.387 cm3.
  • 1 mL = 1 cm3 = 1 cc, and 1 L = 1000 cm3 = 1 dm3. In charting, write mL, not cc.
  • Squared and cubed factors are not on the reference sheet; build them from a length equality.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A patient's body surface area is 1.85 m2. Express it in cm2.

Hint

The unit is squared, so square the factor (100 cm/1 m) too.

Answer

\( 1.85\ \text{m}^{2} \times \left(\dfrac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}}\right)^{2} = 1.85 \times 10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{2} \) (18,500 cm2; 3 significant figures).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Convert 2.5 × 105 cm3 to m3.

Hint

The unit is cubed, so cube the factor (1 m/100 cm). How many cm3 are in one m3?

Answer

\( 2.5 \times 10^{5}\ \text{cm}^{3} \times \left(\dfrac{1\ \text{m}}{100\ \text{cm}}\right)^{3} = 2.5 \times 10^{5} \div 10^{6} = \) 0.25 m3. Dividing by 100 instead (2.5 × 103 m3) means the factor was not cubed.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A medicine cup holds 5.0 mL. Express this volume in (a) cc, (b) cm3, (c) L.

Hint

Use 1 mL = 1 cm3 = 1 cc. For liters, use 1000 mL = 1 L with mL on the bottom.

Answer

(a) 5.0 cc and (b) 5.0 cm3, since 1 mL = 1 cm3 = 1 cc. (c) \( 5.0\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \text{mL}} = \) 5.0 × 10−3 L.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Explain in one or two sentences why 1 cm2 = 100 mm2, using a drawing in words.

Hint

Picture a square 1 cm on each side. How many millimeters long is each side, and what is length × width?

Answer

A square 1 cm on each side is also 10 mm on each side, and its area is 10 mm × 10 mm = 100 mm2. A grid of 10 rows of 10 tiny 1 mm squares fills it.
