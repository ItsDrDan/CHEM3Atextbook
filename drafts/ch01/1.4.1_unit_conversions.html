Learning Objectives

  • Write any equality between two units as a conversion factor, either way up.
  • Set up a one-step conversion in a T-table so the unwanted unit cancels.
  • Organize a problem by writing the Given, the Desired, and a strategy before doing any math.
  • Tell exact conversion factors (metric prefixes, counted numbers, 1 in = 2.54 cm) from measured ones, and use the right number of significant figures in the answer.
  • Use the US–metric equalities on the CHEM 3A yellow sheet.

You already convert units all the time without calling it that. You know that 4 quarters make a dollar, that 12 inches make a foot, and that a 2-hour lab is 120 minutes. Chemistry asks you to do the same thing with less familiar units, and to do it in a way that is written down, checkable, and hard to get wrong. That way is dimensional analysis: a method for converting a measured quantity from one unit to another by multiplying it by conversion factors.

Conversion Factors: Fractions Equal to 1

A conversion factor is an equality between two units written as a fraction. Take 100 cm = 1 m. Because the top and bottom describe the same length, the fraction equals 1, and it can be written either way up:

\( 100\ \text{cm} = 1\ \text{m} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}} \quad \text{or} \quad \dfrac{1\ \text{m}}{100\ \text{cm}} \)

Multiplying a quantity by 1 does not change the quantity; it only changes the unit it is written in. That is why conversions are allowed. The only decision you ever make is which way up to write the factor, and the units make that decision for you.

Units cancel just like numbers do in fractions. In \( \tfrac{2}{7} \times \tfrac{3}{2} \), the 2 on top and the 2 on the bottom cancel. In the same way, "cm" on top and "cm" on the bottom cancel. A unit cancels only when the same unit appears once on top and once on the bottom.

The translator analogy

Picture a rabbit who speaks only Spanish and a bird who speaks only French. They cannot talk to each other until a second rabbit who speaks both languages steps in as a translator. A conversion factor is that translator: it "speaks" both the unit you have and the unit you want. Written as fractions, Spanish on top of the first term cancels Spanish on the bottom of the translator, and only French is left:

\( \text{Spanish} \times \dfrac{\text{French}}{\text{Spanish}} = \text{French} \)

The T-table

Instead of writing a row of fractions, many chemists use a T-table: one long horizontal line, with tops above it and bottoms below it, and a vertical bar between factors. It is the same math, just easier to read and easier to check. In a T-table, a unit cancels when it appears once above the line and once below it, in any column.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Centimeters to inches

Problem. A model car is 15 cm long. How long is it in inches?

Given: 15 cm. Desired: ??? in. Strategy: the yellow sheet gives 1 in = 2.54 cm, which "speaks" both units.

Solution. Write the factor both ways: \( \tfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}} \) or \( \tfrac{1\ \text{in}}{2.54\ \text{cm}} \). The given has cm on top, so pick the version with cm on the bottom:

\(15\ \text{cm}\)\(1\ \text{in}\)
 \(2.54\ \text{cm}\)

\(=\; \dfrac{15 \times 1}{2.54}\ \text{in} = 5.906\ \text{in} \to 5.9\ \text{in} \)

Centimeters above the line cancel centimeters below it, leaving inches. The measurement 15 cm has 2 significant figures; 2.54 cm = 1 in is an exact definition. The answer is 5.9 in.

Check. An inch is longer than a centimeter, so the number of inches must be smaller than the number of centimeters: 5.9 < 15. If you had used the factor upside down, you would get 15 × 2.54 = 38 "cm2/in," a unit that makes no sense, which is the units warning you.

Professor Tip

Let the units guide you. Before you touch the calculator, look at the unit you need to get rid of and ask, "Where does this unit have to be so it cancels?" If it is on top, the next factor must have it on the bottom. Also notice that in most factors the bigger unit gets the 1 and the smaller unit gets the bigger number (1 in = 2.54 cm, 1 L = 1000 mL). That pattern helps you write factors without looking them up twice.

Organize Before You Calculate

In chemistry math, organization is the key. Every conversion in this book follows the same routine:

  1. Given. Write the number and unit you start with (and what it describes, such as "kg body").
  2. Desired. Write the unit the answer must have, with question marks for the number: ??? lb.
  3. Strategy. List the factors that connect them. Is outside information needed (the yellow sheet, a definition you know)?
  4. Set up and cancel. Start with the given, the term that stands alone, never with a conversion factor. Add factors so each unwanted unit cancels.
  5. Calculate, round, check. Multiply the tops, divide by the bottoms, round once at the end, and ask whether the size of the answer makes sense.

Starting with the term that stands alone matters because a conversion factor can be flipped to fit wherever you need it, but your given cannot. Section 1.4.4 shows how to find that term inside a word problem.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): A newborn's weight

Problem. A newborn is weighed at 3.62 kg. The parents ask for the weight in pounds. (1 kg = 2.2046 lb)

Given: 3.62 kg. Desired: ??? lb. Strategy: kg → lb using the yellow sheet's 1 kg = 2.2046 lb.

Solution.

\(3.62\ \text{kg}\)\(2.2046\ \text{lb}\)
 \(1\ \text{kg}\)

\(=\; 7.981\ \text{lb} \to 7.98\ \text{lb} \)

The weight 3.62 kg has 3 significant figures and the factor 2.2046 has 5, so the answer has 3: 7.98 lb, just under 8 lb.

Check. A kilogram is a bit more than 2 pounds, so the number of pounds should be a bit more than twice the number of kilograms: 2 × 3.62 ≈ 7.2, and 7.98 is a bit more than that.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Blood volume in milliliters

Problem. An adult has about 5.2 L of blood. Express this volume in milliliters.

Given: 5.2 L. Desired: ??? mL. Strategy: 1 L = 1000 mL (a metric prefix, exact).

Solution.

\( 5.2\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{L}} = 5200\ \text{mL} = 5.2 \times 10^{3}\ \text{mL} \)

The 1000 is exact, so the answer keeps the 2 significant figures of 5.2 L. Under this book's rule, "5200 mL" already shows 2 significant figures; scientific notation, 5.2 × 103 mL, makes it unmistakable.

Check. A milliliter is a thousandth of a liter, so there must be 1000 times as many milliliters as liters.

Exact and Measured Conversion Factors

Some conversion factors are exact; they never limit the significant figures in your answer:

  • Metric prefixes are definitions: 1 kg = 1000 g and 1 mL = 10−3 L exactly.
  • Counted numbers and definitions within one system: 12 in = 1 ft, 60 min = 1 h, 4 doses in a day.
  • 1 in = 2.54 cm, the one US–metric factor that is exact by definition.

Every other factor that bridges the US and metric systems is measured (rounded from a longer value), so its digits count. When you have a choice, pick the factor with more significant figures, so the factor does not limit your answer. Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Which factor should I use?

Problem. A patient's weight is recorded as 165 lb. Convert it to kilograms two ways, using 1 kg = 2.2046 lb and using 1 lb = 453.59 g (both from the yellow sheet). Do the two answers agree?

Given: 165 lb. Desired: ??? kg.

Solution.

\( 165\ \text{lb} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{2.2046\ \text{lb}} = 74.84\ \text{kg} \to 74.8\ \text{kg} \)

\( 165\ \text{lb} \times \dfrac{453.59\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{lb}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \text{g}} = 74.84\ \text{kg} \to 74.8\ \text{kg} \)

Both factors have more significant figures than the 3 in 165 lb, so the measurement limits the answer either way: 74.8 kg.

Check. The unrounded values (74.844 and 74.842 kg) agree to four digits because both factors are rounded versions of the same defined value. A kilogram is about 2.2 lb, so 165 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 75 kg.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Health care runs on the metric system because it is the global language of medicine: doses are written in milligrams (mg) and micrograms, volumes in milliliters (mL), and weights in kilograms (kg). A few habits protect patients from unit errors:

  • Weigh in kilograms. Most weight-based doses are written per kilogram. Recording a weight in pounds where kilograms are expected makes the dose about 2.2 times too large.
  • Write "mcg," not "µg." A handwritten µ can be misread as "m," turning micrograms into milligrams, a thousandfold error. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) lists "µg" as an error-prone abbreviation; health care writes micrograms as mcg.
  • Write "mL," not "cc." They are the same volume (Section 1.4.3), but a handwritten "cc" can be misread as "u" (units).
  • Leading zero always, trailing zero never. On medication orders, The Joint Commission's "Do Not Use" list forbids ".5 mg" (the decimal point can be missed, so 0.5 becomes 5) and "5.0 mg" (it can be read as 50). Write 0.5 mg and 5 mg. Lab reports follow significant-figure rules instead (Section 1.3), so "5.0 mL" is correct in your lab notebook; the two settings have different rules for different risks.

Common Mistakes

  • Using a factor upside down. If the unit you want to remove is on top, the factor must have it on the bottom. Strange leftover units (cm2/in) mean a factor is flipped.
  • Starting with a conversion factor instead of the given quantity. Start with the term that stands alone.
  • Counting the digits of exact factors. 1000 mL = 1 L and 2.54 cm = 1 in never limit significant figures.
  • Ignoring the digits of measured factors. The yellow sheet's 1 kg = 2.2046 lb has 5 significant figures, but 1 qt = 0.946 L has only 3, and a measured factor can limit a precise measurement.
  • Skipping the size check. Converting to a smaller unit must give a bigger number, and the reverse.

Summary

  • A conversion factor is an equality written as a fraction equal to 1; it can be written either way up.
  • Choose the version that puts the unwanted unit on the opposite side so it cancels. Let the units guide you.
  • Organize every problem: Given, Desired, strategy, then set up starting with the given.
  • Metric-prefix factors, counted numbers, and 1 in = 2.54 cm are exact. Other US–metric factors are measured; prefer the one with more digits.
  • In health care, weights are recorded in kg, micrograms are written mcg, and mL is used instead of cc.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write two conversion factors for each equality: (a) 1 L = 1000 mL; (b) 1 kg = 2.2046 lb. Which factors are exact?

Hint

Write each equality as a fraction both ways up. Metric prefixes and definitions are exact; rounded US–metric values are measured.

Answer

(a) \( \tfrac{1000\ \text{mL}}{1\ \text{L}} \) and \( \tfrac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \text{mL}} \), exact (a metric prefix). (b) \( \tfrac{2.2046\ \text{lb}}{1\ \text{kg}} \) and \( \tfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{2.2046\ \text{lb}} \), measured (5 significant figures, the yellow sheet's value).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A patient is 68.5 in tall. What is the height in centimeters?

Hint

Use 1 in = 2.54 cm with inches on the bottom so they cancel. The factor is exact, so only 68.5 in limits the significant figures.

Answer

\( 68.5\ \text{in} \times \dfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}} = 173.99\ \text{cm} \to \) 174 cm (3 significant figures from 68.5 in; 2.54 is exact).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A 2.00 L bottle of an oral rehydration drink holds how many quarts? (1 qt = 0.946 L)

Hint

Use 1 qt = 0.946 L with liters on the bottom so liters cancel.

Answer

\( 2.00\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{qt}}{0.946\ \text{L}} = 2.114\ \text{qt} \to \) 2.11 qt (3 significant figures from 2.00 L and from 0.946).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A student converts 12.5 oz to grams using the yellow sheet's 1 g = 0.03527 oz and writes 12.5 oz × (0.03527 oz / 1 g) = 0.441. What went wrong, and what is the correct answer?

Hint

Check whether ounces cancel in the student's setup. Size check: a gram is much smaller than an ounce, so the number must get bigger.

Answer

The factor is upside down: ounces are on top twice, so they cannot cancel, and the answer's unit would be oz2/g. Correct: \( 12.5\ \text{oz} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{0.03527\ \text{oz}} = 354.4\ \text{g} \to \) 354 g. Size check: a gram is much smaller than an ounce, so the number must get bigger.
