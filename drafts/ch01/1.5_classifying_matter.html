Learning Objectives

  • Define matter and tell atoms, molecules, and formula units apart.
  • Classify a sample as an element, a compound, a homogeneous mixture, or a heterogeneous mixture.
  • Read a particle diagram and decide what kind of matter it shows.
  • Name the seven elements that exist as diatomic molecules.
  • Choose a physical method (filtering, decanting, centrifuging, distilling) to separate a mixture.

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space: the air in your lungs, the saline in an IV bag, the enamel on your teeth, the soil in an orchard. Light, heat, and sound are not matter; they are forms of energy. Chemists sort matter by what it is made of, and that sorting tells you a lot about how a sample will behave and how you could separate it.

Atoms, Molecules, and Formula Units

All matter is made of tiny particles. The smallest particle of an element that still has the properties of that element is an atom. There are 118 known elements, each with its own kind of atom and its own symbol on the periodic table (H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, Na for sodium). You will look inside atoms in Chapter 2.

  • A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together as a unit. A molecule can contain one element (O2, oxygen gas) or several (H2O, water).
  • A formula unit is the smallest repeating ratio of ions in an ionic compound such as table salt, NaCl. Salt is not made of separate NaCl molecules; it is a huge, orderly stack of Na+ and Cl− ions in a 1:1 ratio. (Ions are charged particles; see Section 1.7.)

A chemical formula lists the elements in a substance with subscripts that count the atoms. In H2O the subscript 2 means two H atoms; no subscript on O means one O atom.

The diatomic seven

Seven elements never exist as lone atoms under normal conditions. Their atoms pair up into diatomic (two-atom) molecules: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, and I2. They are still elements, because each molecule contains only one kind of atom.

Professor Tip

To find the diatomic seven on the periodic table, start at element 7 (nitrogen), go across to fluorine to make a "7" shape (N, O, F), then go down the halogen column (Cl, Br, I), and do not forget hydrogen, sitting alone at the top left. If the formula has only one element symbol, it is an element, subscript or not: O2 and O3 (ozone) are both forms of the element oxygen.

Pure Substances and Mixtures

The first question to ask about any sample is: is every particle the same? (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

  • A pure substance has a fixed composition: every particle is the same. There are two kinds.
    • An element contains only one kind of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical change. Examples: helium (He), iron (Fe), oxygen gas (O2).
    • A compound contains two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. Water is always 2 H atoms to 1 O atom, wherever it comes from. A compound can be broken down into its elements only by a chemical change, and its properties are very different from those of its elements: sodium is a reactive metal and chlorine is a toxic gas, but sodium chloride is table salt.
  • A mixture contains two or more substances that are physically combined, not bonded. Its composition can vary (you can make tea weak or strong), each component keeps its own properties, and the components can be separated by physical means.
    • A homogeneous mixture (also called a solution) has the same composition throughout; you cannot see separate parts. Examples: air, normal saline (salt dissolved in water), brass, clear apple juice.
    • A heterogeneous mixture is not uniform; you can see (or a microscope can show) different regions. Examples: salad dressing, granite, wet sand, whole blood.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Classify the sample

Problem. Classify each as an element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture: (a) carbon dioxide, CO2; (b) sterile normal saline (0.9% NaCl in water); (c) a gold ring that is pure gold; (d) chocolate chip cookie dough; (e) nitrogen gas, N2.

Solution.

  1. CO2 has two different elements (C and O) bonded in a fixed 1:2 ratio: compound.
  2. Saline is salt dissolved evenly in water; it looks the same everywhere and its concentration could be different: homogeneous mixture (a solution).
  3. Pure gold contains only Au atoms: element.
  4. You can see the chips in the dough: heterogeneous mixture.
  5. N2 has only one kind of atom (it is one of the diatomic seven): element.

Check. Every formula with one element symbol (Au, N2) came out "element"; the one formula with two symbols bonded came out "compound"; the two items with no single formula came out "mixture."

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Read a particle diagram

Problem. Look at box (e) of Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). Is it a pure substance or a mixture? If a mixture, is it a mixture of elements, of compounds, or of both?

Solution. The box holds two kinds of particles, N2 molecules and O2 molecules, that are not bonded to each other, so it is a mixture. Each molecule contains only one kind of atom, so both components are elements: a mixture of elements. (This is a simple model of air.)

Check. A compound would need different atoms bonded within the same particle. No particle in box (e) has two different symbols, so there is no compound present.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

The air you breathe is a homogeneous mixture: by volume, dry air is about 78% nitrogen (N2), 21% oxygen (O2), 0.9% argon, and about 0.04% carbon dioxide, plus a variable amount of water vapor. Medical oxygen is much closer to a pure substance. The United States Pharmacopeia (USP) standard for Oxygen requires not less than 99.0% O2 by volume, so a cylinder labeled "Oxygen USP" is almost entirely one element. Respiratory therapists describe how much oxygen a patient breathes as the FiO2 (fraction of inspired oxygen): room air has an FiO2 of about 0.21, and giving supplemental oxygen raises it. Changing the proportions of a mixture is exactly what a mixture allows; you could never change the ratio of H to O in a water molecule.

Source: United States Pharmacopeia, Oxygen monograph (USP–NF).

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Look at the products on an operatory tray. A fluoride mouth rinse is a homogeneous mixture: sodium fluoride dissolved evenly in water, so every sip has the same fluoride concentration. Toothpaste is a heterogeneous mixture: solid abrasive particles (such as hydrated silica or calcium carbonate) are suspended in a gel, which is why it is opaque and gritty. Prophy paste, used for polishing, is even more coarsely heterogeneous. Knowing which is which explains the instructions on the label: a suspension can settle and may need stirring, while a true solution never separates on standing.

Separating Mixtures

Because the parts of a mixture are not bonded, you can separate them using differences in their physical properties (properties you can observe without changing what the substance is; see Section 1.8).

Common physical methods for separating mixtures
MethodWhat it usesExample
FiltrationParticle size: a solid is trapped while a liquid passes throughRemoving sand from water; a coffee filter
DecantingDensity: a solid or a denser liquid settles, and the top layer is poured offPouring water off settled mud
CentrifugationDensity: spinning forces denser parts to the bottom quicklySeparating plasma from blood cells
DistillationBoiling point: the liquid that boils first is collected as vapor and condensedPurifying water; separating alcohol from water
EvaporationBoiling point: the liquid leaves and a dissolved solid stays behindSea salt from seawater

The table above lists each method with the physical property it relies on. A compound, by contrast, cannot be separated by any of these methods; boiling water just gives water vapor, still H2O. Breaking water into hydrogen and oxygen takes a chemical change.

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

Your body is a mixture. By mass, about 96% of the human body is just four elements: oxygen (about 65%), carbon (about 18%), hydrogen (about 10%), and nitrogen (about 3%). Almost none of it is present as the free elements; the atoms are bonded into compounds such as water, proteins, fats, and DNA.

Blood is a heterogeneous mixture. It looks uniform, but under a microscope you can see cells floating in a liquid. When a blood sample is spun in a centrifuge, the denser red blood cells pack at the bottom (in healthy adults they make up roughly 36 to 50% of the volume; this percentage is the hematocrit), a thin "buffy coat" of white blood cells and platelets sits above them, and the yellowish plasma rises to the top. Plasma itself is a homogeneous mixture: water with dissolved salts, glucose, proteins, and gases.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling O2 or N2 a compound because it has a subscript. A compound needs two or more different elements. One symbol means element.
  • Calling every clear liquid a pure substance. Saline, vinegar, and tap water are clear but are homogeneous mixtures.
  • Confusing "molecule" with "compound." O2 is a molecule but not a compound; NaCl is a compound but is made of formula units, not molecules.
  • Thinking a compound keeps the properties of its elements. It does not; water does not burn like hydrogen or support burning like oxygen.

Summary

  • Matter has mass and takes up space. It is made of atoms, which may be bonded into molecules or arranged as ions in formula units.
  • Pure substances have a fixed composition: elements (one kind of atom) and compounds (two or more elements bonded in a fixed ratio).
  • Mixtures are physically combined and have variable composition: homogeneous (uniform, a solution) or heterogeneous (not uniform).
  • H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, and I2 exist as diatomic molecules but are still elements.
  • Mixtures can be separated by physical methods; compounds can be broken down only by chemical changes.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify each: (a) sucrose (table sugar), C12H22O11; (b) sugar dissolved in iced tea; (c) chlorine gas, Cl2; (d) a bowl of cereal with milk.

Answer

(a) compound (three elements bonded in a fixed ratio); (b) homogeneous mixture (dissolved evenly; strength can vary); (c) element (one kind of atom; a diatomic element); (d) heterogeneous mixture (you can see the parts).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Box (f) in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) contains H2O molecules and O2 molecules. Explain why it is a mixture even though every particle contains oxygen.

Answer

There are two different kinds of particles (H2O and O2), and they are not bonded to each other. A pure substance would need every particle to be identical.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which method would you use to separate (a) sand from water, (b) the dissolved salt from seawater, and (c) red blood cells from plasma?

Answer

(a) filtration (or decanting); (b) evaporation (or distillation, which also recovers the water); (c) centrifugation.
