Learning Objectives

  • Explain why unit conversion is a core skill in chemistry and in health care.
  • Name the five parts of this section and what each one teaches.

A child arrives at an emergency department and is weighed: 22 pounds. Someone types "22" into a chart field that expects kilograms. Every weight-based dose calculated from that chart is now more than twice what it should be, because 22 lb is only about 10 kg. Nobody made a math error. The number was right; the unit was wrong. Mix-ups like this are common enough that patient-safety groups such as the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) recommend that hospitals weigh and record patients in kilograms only.

Converting units, also called dimensional analysis, is arguably the most important math skill in this course. It appears in almost every chapter after this one: in moles (Chapter 5), stoichiometry (Chapter 8), gases (Chapter 10), and solutions (Chapter 11). It is also the exact skill tested on the dosage-calculation exams that nursing, pharmacy technician, and other allied-health programs give. A conversion error in a lab report costs points. The same error at a bedside can cost much more.

The good news is that every conversion, from centimeters to inches to a weight-based drug dose, uses one method. You write what you are given, write what you want, and multiply by fractions that are equal to 1 until the units you do not want cancel. The units do the thinking for you, which is why the most common advice in this section is: let the units guide you.

Professor Tip

Take your time with this section and practice as many problems as you can. Students who master conversions now find the rest of the course much easier.

Clinical Connection: Nursing and Pharmacy Technology

A medication order, a drug label, and a patient's weight are all quantities with units, and a dose is what you get when you connect them. Nurses check doses calculated by pharmacy, and pharmacy technicians prepare them, so both must be able to set up a conversion and also judge whether an answer makes sense. A dose that comes out as 10 or 1,000 times the usual amount almost always means a unit was not converted (mg treated as g, or lb treated as kg). (Context only; this book gives no dosing guidance.)
