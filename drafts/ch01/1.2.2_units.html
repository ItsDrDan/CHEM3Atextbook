Learning Objectives

  • Name the SI base units for length, mass, temperature, time, and amount of substance, and explain where the liter fits.
  • Give the symbol and power of ten for each metric prefix from tera- to pico-, using a memory aid.
  • Read a prefix entry on the yellow sheet and rewrite it in whole-number form (100 cm = 1 m).
  • Recognize the health-care forms of metric units (mcg, mL, kg) and why they are written that way.
  • Locate the US–metric equalities you will use for conversions, and tell which are exact.

The United States runs on miles, pounds, and gallons. Almost no one else does, and neither does science, nor medicine. A nurse in Fresno, a dental hygienist in Manila, and a pharmacist in Mexico City all read "2.5 mL" the same way. That shared language is the metric system, and its modern scientific form is the International System of Units (SI). Hospitals and clinics in the United States use metric units for doses, lab values, and patient weights so that everyone on the care team, and every device, speaks the same language. If you plan to work in health care, the metric system is the language you will work in every day.

SI Base Units

The SI is built on seven base units. Every other unit, such as a unit of volume or speed, is made from them. Five of the seven are used in chemistry, and they are listed below. (The other two, the ampere for electric current and the candela for brightness of light, belong mostly to physics.)

Notice that the base unit of mass, the kilogram, already has a prefix. That is a historical quirk; for prefixes, the gram is treated as the base unit of mass. Notice also that volume is missing. Volume is a derived unit, a length cubed. The SI unit is the cubic meter, which is far too large for a lab bench or a syringe, so chemistry and medicine use the metric liter (L), which is exactly 1000 cm3. One milliliter is exactly one cubic centimeter: 1 mL = 1 cm3. Section 1.4.3 shows why.

Metric Prefixes

A prefix is a letter in front of a unit that changes its size by a power of ten. A kilogram is a thousand grams; a milligram is a thousandth of a gram. Because every prefix is a power of ten, changing prefixes only moves the decimal point; there are no 12s, 16s, or 5,280s to remember. Prefixes keep the numbers in a comfortable range: a 500 mg tablet is easier to read and say than a 0.500 g tablet or a 500,000 mcg tablet, though all three describe the same mass.

The table lists the prefixes you will use, from largest to smallest. Your CHEM 3A yellow sheet lists each prefix with its symbol and its power of ten, the "Multiplier" column here (for example, micro- µ- 10−6). The "equality" column reads that multiplier as one prefixed unit compared with the base unit (1 µm = 10−6 m). The "whole-number form" is the same equality flipped, where needed, so that both sides are whole numbers. Hecto- and deca- are not on the yellow sheet; the sheet does list one prefix below pico-, femto- (f, 10−15), which you will rarely use.

Read the table as a ladder. Everything above the base unit is big compared with the base unit, and everything below it is tiny. From kilo- down, the most-used prefixes are 1000 times apart: kilo-, base, milli-, micro-, nano-, pico-. Capital letters matter. A capital M (mega-, a million) and a lowercase m (milli-, a thousandth) differ by a factor of a billion. The liter's symbol is a capital L so that it is not mistaken for the number 1: mL, not ml.

Professor Tip

Learn the order of the prefixes with a sentence: "The great Mighty King Henry died by drinking chocolate or chunky milk Monday night." ('or chunky' is just filler) The (tera), great (giga), Mighty (mega), King (kilo), Henry (hecto), died (deca), by (the base unit), drinking (deci), chocolate (centi), milk (milli), Monday (micro), night (nano). Pico- is one step past nano-. Hecto- and deca- are not on your yellow sheet and are rarely used in chemistry; the sheet adds femto- (10−15) after pico-, but you will rarely need it. Then make flashcards with the prefix on one side and its power of ten on the other. You will use these values in every chapter, so practice them until they are automatic.

Reading the yellow sheet

Your reference sheet lists each prefix with its symbol and its power of ten, for example "centi- c- 10−2" and "micro- µ- 10−6." Read each entry as an equality with the base unit: centi- = 10−2 means 1 cm = 10−2 m, and micro- = 10−6 means 1 µL = 10−6 L. That is correct, but it puts a small decimal into every calculation, and small decimals are where calculator mistakes happen. You can flip any small prefix into whole numbers. Because 1 cm is one hundredth of a meter, 100 cm make a meter: 100 cm = 1 m. Your calculator confirms the flip: 1 ÷ 10−2 = 100. Either form gives the same answer in a conversion; use the one you make fewer mistakes with.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Flip a yellow-sheet entry

Problem. The yellow sheet lists micro- (µ) with the power of ten 10−6, so 1 µL = 10−6 L. Rewrite this equality in whole-number form, and write both conversion factors it gives.

Solution. A microliter is one millionth of a liter, so a million microliters make a liter: 1 ÷ 10−6 = 106, and

\( 10^{6}\ \mu\text{L} = 1\ \text{L} \qquad \dfrac{10^{6}\ \mu\text{L}}{1\ \text{L}} \quad \text{or} \quad \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{10^{6}\ \mu\text{L}} \)

Check. The smaller unit (µL) gets the bigger number (106). If you wrote 1 µL = 106 L, a single microliter would be a million liters, a swimming pool in a drop.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Symbols and sizes

Problem. Give the symbol for each unit and its size in base units: (a) kiloliter; (b) microgram; (c) decimeter; (d) nanosecond.

Solution. (a) kL; 1 kL = 103 L = 1000 L. (b) µg (written mcg in health care); 1 µg = 10−6 g. (c) dm; 1 dm = 10−1 m = 0.1 m. (d) ns; 1 ns = 10−9 s.

Check. Kilo- is above the base unit (bigger, positive exponent); micro-, deci-, and nano- are below it (smaller, negative exponents).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Which is larger?

Problem. Which is the larger mass in each pair? (a) 0.125 mg or 125 mcg; (b) 2 kg or 2000 mg; (c) 50 ng or 50 pg.

Solution. (a) Milli- and micro- are 1000 times apart: 1 mg = 1000 mcg, so 0.125 mg = 125 mcg. They are equal. (b) 2000 mg is 2 g, and 2 kg is 2000 g, so 2 kg is a thousand times larger. (c) Nano- is above pico- on the ladder, so 50 ng is larger (by 1000 times).

Check. In (a), the same mass is written two ways: a small number of a bigger unit, or a bigger number of a smaller unit.

Metric Units in Health Care

Health care uses the same metric units as chemistry, with a few safety habits added, because a misread unit can mean a dose a thousand times too large or too small:

  • mcg, not µg. A handwritten µ can look like an m, which would turn micrograms into milligrams, a thousandfold error. Medication orders and labels write micrograms as mcg.
  • mL, not cc. A cubic centimeter is exactly a milliliter, but a handwritten "cc" can be misread as "u" (units), so orders use mL.
  • kg for body weight. Weight-based doses are written per kilogram (for example, mg/kg). A weight recorded in pounds and read as kilograms makes the dose about 2.2 times too large.

Clinical Connection: Pharmacy Technology

Some medications come in microgram strengths and others in milligram strengths, sometimes for the same patient. Levothyroxine, a thyroid hormone, is labeled in micrograms (for example, 50 mcg), while many other tablets are labeled in milligrams. Because 50 mcg is 0.05 mg, confusing the two units would multiply or divide the amount by 1000. Pharmacy technicians check the unit on the label, the order, and the stock bottle every time. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) lists "µg" and "cc" among its error-prone abbreviations and recommends "mcg" and "mL" instead. (Context only; not dosing guidance.)

US–Metric Equalities

Sometimes you will need to cross between the US system and the metric system: a patient reports a weight in pounds, or a recipe uses ounces. Your yellow sheet lists the US–metric equalities you will use (such as 1 in = 2.54 cm, 1 kg = 2.2046 lb, and 1 gal = 3.785 L); Section 1.4.1, Table 1, lists them all and marks which are exact. Only 1 in = 2.54 cm is exact among the US–metric bridges (the sheet prints it "exactly"); the others are rounded values, so they can limit the significant figures of an answer (Section 1.3.2). Section 1.4 shows how to use them as conversion factors.

Two quick benchmarks help you check answers: a kilogram is a little more than 2 lb, and a liter is a little more than a quart.

Common Mistakes

  • Flipping a prefix the wrong way. 1 cm = 10−2 m becomes 100 cm = 1 m, not 100 m = 1 cm. The smaller unit always gets the bigger number.
  • Mixing up m, M, and µ. Milli- (m) is 10−3, mega- (M) is 106, micro- (µ, mcg) is 10−6. Case and symbol matter.
  • Thinking the kilogram is not a base unit. It is the SI base unit of mass, even though prefixes are attached to the gram.
  • Treating every US–metric factor as exact. Only 1 in = 2.54 cm (and 1 ft = 30.48 cm, built from it) and 1 L = 1000 cm3 are exact; the yellow sheet's 1 kg = 2.2046 lb has 5 significant figures.
  • Writing "ml," "Kg," or "cc." Write mL, kg, and (in health care) mL instead of cc.

Summary

  • Science and health care use the metric system, a shared language. The SI base units used in chemistry are the meter, kilogram, kelvin, second, and mole; the liter (1 L = 1000 cm3) is the everyday unit of volume, and 1 mL = 1 cm3.
  • Prefixes multiply a unit by a power of ten, from tera- (1012) to pico- (10−12). Above the base unit is big; below it is tiny.
  • The yellow sheet lists each prefix with its power of ten (centi- = 10−2, so 1 cm = 10−2 m). That equality can be flipped into whole numbers: 100 cm = 1 m.
  • In health care, write mcg (not µg) and mL (not cc), and record body weight in kg.
  • Of the US–metric equalities, only 1 in = 2.54 cm (with 1 ft = 30.48 cm) and 1 L = 1000 cm3 are exact; the rest are rounded.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the symbol and the power of ten for each prefix: (a) mega-; (b) centi-; (c) micro-; (d) pico-.

Hint

Use the yellow sheet's prefix table. Mega- is larger than the base unit; centi-, micro- and pico- are smaller.

Answer

(a) M, 106. (b) c, 10−2. (c) µ (mcg for micrograms in health care), 10−6. (d) p, 10−12.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

The yellow sheet lists milli- as 10−3 and nano- as 10−9, so 1 mm = 10−3 m and 1 ng = 10−9 g. Rewrite each equality in whole-number form.

Hint

Divide both sides of each equality by the power of ten so the prefixed unit has a whole-number count.

Answer

1000 mm = 1 m (because 1 ÷ 10−3 = 1000), and 109 ng = 1 g (because 1 ÷ 10−9 = 109).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which is larger: (a) 0.50 mg or 450 mcg; (b) 1.2 dL or 120 mL?

Hint

Convert each pair to the same unit before comparing. (a) 1 mg = 1000 mcg. (b) Deci- means one-tenth, so how many mL are in 1 dL?

Answer

(a) 0.50 mg = 500 mcg, so 0.50 mg is larger. (b) 1 dL = 0.1 L = 100 mL, so 1.2 dL = 120 mL: they are equal.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

(Context only.) A child's weight is entered as "20" in a chart field labeled kg, but the child was weighed on a scale reading pounds. Is a weight-based dose calculated from this entry too large or too small, and by about how much?

Hint

Use 1 kg = 2.2046 lb. Is 20 lb more or less than 20 kg? Then compare how the dose would change.

Answer

Too large. 20 lb is only about 9 kg (20 ÷ 2.2046 = 9.07), so treating it as 20 kg makes the calculated dose about 2.2 times too large. This is why many facilities record weights in kilograms only.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Which of these US–metric equalities are exact: 1 in = 2.54 cm; 1 kg = 2.2046 lb; 1 L = 1000 cm3; 1 gal = 3.785 L?

Hint

Exact equalities are definitions (like metric prefixes and the inch). Rounded values show a limited number of significant figures.

Answer

1 in = 2.54 cm and 1 L = 1000 cm3 are exact (definitions). 1 kg = 2.2046 lb (5 significant figures) and 1 gal = 3.785 L (4 significant figures) are rounded values.
