Chemistry in the Community: A Fresno Morning

It is 7:15 on a January morning in Fresno. Highway 99 is wrapped in tule fog, so thick that the overpass ahead fades to gray. In a kitchen off Shields Avenue, a nursing student pours coffee, stirs in sugar until it disappears, and tops a bowl of oatmeal with raisins grown a few miles away. Her younger brother scrapes frost off the windshield. Down the street, a bus pulls away, its engine burning diesel fuel and leaving a faint smell behind.

Every part of that morning is chemistry. The fog is water changing from gas to liquid. The sweet coffee is a mixture whose parts you cannot see, while the oatmeal is a mixture whose parts you can. The frost formed straight from water vapor, and the bus engine turned fuel and oxygen into entirely new substances. This chapter gives you the vocabulary to describe all of it: what matter is, how to sort it, how it changes, and how scientists ask and answer questions about it.

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Describe the steps of the scientific method and tell qualitative from quantitative observations (Section 1.1).
  • Classify matter as an element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture, including from particle diagrams (Section 1.2).
  • Describe solids, liquids, and gases at the large scale and the particle scale (Section 1.3).
  • Predict how charged particles interact (Section 1.4).
  • Distinguish physical from chemical properties and changes (Section 1.5).
  • Calculate density and use it to predict floating, sinking, and layering (Section 1.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Whether you are headed for nursing, dental hygiene, radiologic technology, respiratory therapy, biology, or engineering, you will spend your career observing matter and the changes it goes through: a wound that foams, a gas that fills a tank, a blood sample that separates in a centrifuge, an X-ray that passes through soft tissue but not bone. This chapter builds the language for describing those observations. The ideas here (pure substance versus mixture, physical versus chemical change, attraction between opposite charges) come back in every chapter that follows.
