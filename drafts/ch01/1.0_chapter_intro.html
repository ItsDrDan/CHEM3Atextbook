Chemistry in the Community: A Fresno Morning

It is 7:15 on a January morning in Fresno. Highway 99 is wrapped in tule fog, so thick that the overpass ahead fades to gray. In a kitchen off Shields Avenue, a nursing student pours coffee, stirs in sugar until it disappears, and tops a bowl of oatmeal with raisins grown a few miles away. Her younger brother scrapes frost off the windshield. Down the street, a bus pulls away, its engine burning diesel fuel and leaving a faint smell behind.

Every part of that morning is chemistry. The fog is water changing from gas to liquid. The sweet coffee is a mixture whose parts you cannot see, while the oatmeal is a mixture whose parts you can. The frost formed straight from water vapor, and the bus engine turned fuel and oxygen into entirely new substances. By 9:00 she is at her clinical site, where every number she charts (a weight in kilograms, a dose in milligrams, a temperature in degrees Celsius) has to be measured carefully, written with the right unit, and converted correctly. This chapter gives you the tools for all of it: how to measure, how to sort matter, how matter changes, and how scientists ask and answer questions.

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Describe the steps of the scientific method and tell qualitative from quantitative observations (Section 1.1).
  • Write measurements with a number and a unit, use scientific notation, and use SI units and metric prefixes (Section 1.2).
  • Count significant figures, round correctly, and report calculated answers with the right precision; tell accuracy from precision (Section 1.3).
  • Solve unit-conversion problems with dimensional analysis, including multi-step conversions and squared or cubed units (Section 1.4).
  • Classify matter as an element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture, including from particle diagrams (Section 1.5).
  • Describe solids, liquids, and gases at the large scale and the particle scale (Section 1.6).
  • Predict how charged particles interact (Section 1.7).
  • Distinguish physical from chemical properties and changes (Section 1.8).
  • Calculate density, use it to predict floating and sinking, and use it as a conversion factor between mass and volume (Section 1.9).

Why Measurement Comes First

Almost everything you will do in a health career, a science lab, or an engineering job comes down to a number with a unit. A nurse calculates a weight-based dose in milligrams per kilogram. A respiratory therapist sets oxygen flow in liters per minute. A radiologic technologist reads radiation dose in milligray. A dental hygienist checks fluoride concentration in parts per million. A biologist dilutes a culture by a factor of a thousand, and an engineer specifies a part to a tenth of a millimeter. In every one of these jobs, a wrong unit, a misplaced decimal point, or a number reported with false precision can cause real harm.

That is why this book starts with measurement. The skills in Sections 1.2 to 1.4 (writing numbers in scientific notation, using metric prefixes, knowing how many digits a measurement can honestly claim, and converting units step by step) are the same skills you will use on your first day of clinicals, in your first research lab, or on your first job site. They also run through every chapter that follows: you cannot do a stoichiometry problem, a gas law, or a dilution without them. Learn them well here, and the rest of the course gets much easier.

Professor Tip

Treat units as part of the number, never as decoration. If you carry the units through every step of a calculation, they will tell you when you have set up a problem wrong, long before you get a wrong answer. Professionals check units first; so should you.
