Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish physical properties from chemical properties.
  • Classify a change as physical or chemical, using the particle view and the signs of a chemical change.
  • Name the phase changes (melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, sublimation, deposition) and explain why they are physical changes.
  • Tell intensive properties from extensive properties.

A bottle of hydrogen peroxide sits in a first-aid kit. It is a clear, colorless liquid, and pure hydrogen peroxide boils at 150 °C: those are its physical properties. Pour some on a cut and it foams as it breaks down into water and oxygen gas: that is a chemical property in action. Learning to tell physical from chemical is one of the most useful skills in the course, because it tells you whether the particles themselves have changed.

"The only constant in life is change," a saying traced back to the Greek philosopher Heraclitus, is a good summary of chemistry: chemistry is the study of how and why matter changes. Picture a shiny red sports car left for decades near the coast, where the air carries salt. It is still the same car, but its red paint has faded and its steel body has turned to flaky orange-brown rust. Both changes are chemical: sunlight breaks down the pigment molecules in the paint, and the steel turns into rust, a new substance. Sorting out which changes make new substances and which do not is the job of this section.

Physical and Chemical Properties

A property is a characteristic that describes a substance.

  • A physical property can be observed or measured without changing the identity of the substance. Examples: color, odor, state, mass, volume, melting point, boiling point, density (Section 1.9), hardness, and whether it dissolves in water.
  • A chemical property describes how a substance changes into a different substance. You can only observe it by trying the change. Examples: flammability (it burns), reactivity with acid, the tendency of iron to rust, and the tendency of hydrogen peroxide to decompose.

Intensive and extensive properties

Some physical properties depend on how much of the sample you have; these are extensive properties, such as mass and volume. Others are the same for a drop or a drum of the substance; these are intensive properties, such as color, boiling point, and density. Intensive properties are the ones useful for identifying a substance: 5 mL of water and 5 L of water both boil at 100 °C at sea level.

Physical and Chemical Changes

A physical change alters the form or appearance of a substance, but the particles stay the same. Cutting, crushing, bending, dissolving, and all changes of state are physical changes. When ice melts, the H2O molecules simply move farther apart and more freely; they are still H2O.

A chemical change (a chemical reaction) turns one or more substances into new substances with different compositions and properties. Atoms are rearranged into new particles. Burning, rusting, cooking, digesting food, and the foaming of peroxide on a wound are chemical changes.

Signs that a chemical change may have happened

  • A gas forms (bubbles) when no boiling is going on.
  • A new color appears.
  • A solid (a precipitate) forms when two solutions are mixed.
  • Heat or light is given off or absorbed without being supplied from outside.
  • A new odor appears.

A classic classroom demonstration shows several clues at once. Concentrated sulfuric acid is poured onto white table sugar (sucrose). Within a minute the sugar turns yellow, then brown, then black; it bubbles and steams; the beaker gets very hot; and a black column of mostly carbon rises out of the beaker like a "carbon snake," many times the volume of the sugar. A color change, a gas, heat, and a solid that was not there before: the acid has pulled water out of the sugar and left a new substance behind. Compare that with smashing a drinking glass into a thousand pieces or tearing a napkin into shreds. The pieces look very different from the starting object, but every piece is still glass, or still napkin: those are physical changes. (The sugar demonstration uses a corrosive acid and is done only by an instructor, behind a shield, in a fume hood.)

These are clues, not proof. Boiling water makes bubbles, but it is still water. The real test is the particle view: did new substances form? You will study chemical reactions in detail in Chapter 7.

Professor Tip

Ask one question: "Is it still the same stuff?" If you could, in principle, get the original substance back just by cooling, warming, drying, or picking pieces apart, the change was physical. Dissolving sugar in tea is physical, because evaporating the water gives back sugar. Toasting the sugar into caramel is chemical, because no amount of cooling turns caramel back into white sugar.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Physical or chemical change?

Problem. Classify each change: (a) a tablet is crushed before it is mixed with applesauce; (b) a silver necklace darkens (tarnishes) in air; (c) an alcohol prep pad dries on the skin; (d) yeast in bread dough turns sugar into carbon dioxide and ethanol; (e) salt dissolves in water.

Solution.

  1. Physical: the tablet is in smaller pieces but is the same substance.
  2. Chemical: silver reacts with sulfur compounds in air to form a new, dark substance (silver sulfide).
  3. Physical: the alcohol evaporates (liquid to gas); its molecules are unchanged.
  4. Chemical: sugar is turned into new substances, carbon dioxide and ethanol.
  5. Physical: the salt is spread among the water molecules; evaporating the water gives the salt back.

Check. In every chemical change above, you can name a new substance that formed (silver sulfide; carbon dioxide and ethanol). In every physical change, the substance's name stayed the same.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Property or change? Physical or chemical?

Problem. A technician writes four notes about ethanol. Label each as a physical property, a chemical property, a physical change, or a chemical change. (a) Ethanol boils at 78 °C. (b) Ethanol is flammable. (c) A spill of ethanol evaporated. (d) Ethanol burned in a lab burner, forming carbon dioxide and water.

Solution. (a) physical property (a description, measured without changing the ethanol); (b) chemical property (describes a change it can undergo); (c) physical change (something happened, but it is still ethanol, now as a gas); (d) chemical change (something happened, and new substances formed).

Check. "Properties" describe what a substance is or can do; "changes" are events that happened. Then decide physical or chemical by whether new substances form.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Two common first-aid liquids show the difference between physical and chemical change. When isopropyl alcohol (rubbing alcohol) is wiped on skin before an injection, it evaporates quickly, a physical change that also cools the skin. When 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) touches a wound, it foams. The bubbles are oxygen gas from a chemical change: an enzyme in blood and tissue, catalase, breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. Current wound-care guidance generally discourages routine use of hydrogen peroxide on wounds, because it can also damage healthy tissue that is trying to heal; follow the provider's order and facility policy. The chemistry lesson is the foam itself: a gas forming with no heating is a sign of a chemical change.

Biology Connection: Microbiology

Microbiologists use the same peroxide reaction to identify bacteria. In the catalase test, a little of a bacterial colony is mixed with a drop of hydrogen peroxide. Bacteria that make the enzyme catalase, such as Staphylococcus, break the peroxide down and the drop bubbles vigorously with oxygen. Bacteria that lack it, such as Streptococcus, produce no bubbles. One quick observation of a chemical change helps narrow down which organism is causing an infection.

Valley Connection: Tule Fog

On many winter mornings, Highway 99 disappears into tule fog, the thick ground fog named after the tule reeds of the Valley's old wetlands. Tule fog is a physical change you can watch. After winter rain, the soil is damp and the air near the ground holds a lot of water vapor. On a long, clear, calm night, the ground loses heat to the sky and cools the air just above it. Cold air cannot hold as much water vapor, so the vapor condenses into tiny liquid droplets, which hang in the air as fog. The National Weather Service calls this radiation fog and names tule fog as a special kind that forms each winter in California's Central Valley. When the sun warms the air later in the day, the droplets evaporate and the fog lifts. The water molecules never changed; only their state did.

Fog matters to more than drivers. Researchers at UC Berkeley used 32 winters of satellite records and found that the number of winter fog events in the Central Valley fell by about 46% on average, with large swings from year to year. They proposed that less fog may be contributing to the loss of winter "chill," the cold hours that fruit and nut trees such as almonds, pistachios, and cherries need to bloom and set fruit well, because fog shades the buds and keeps them cool.

Sources: National Weather Service, "Radiation Fog" (weather.gov/safety/fog-radiation); Baldocchi, D., and Waller, E. (2014), "Winter fog is decreasing in the fruit growing region of the Central Valley of California," Geophysical Research Letters 41, doi:10.1002/2014GL060018.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling boiling a chemical change because of the bubbles. The bubbles in boiling water are water vapor. No new substance forms.
  • Calling dissolving a chemical change. Dissolving salt or sugar is physical; the solid comes back when the water evaporates.
  • Mixing up a chemical property with a chemical change. "Gasoline is flammable" is a property; "the gasoline burned" is a change.
  • Calling density or boiling point extensive. They do not depend on the amount, so they are intensive.

Summary

  • Physical properties are observed without changing a substance's identity; chemical properties describe how it changes into new substances.
  • Physical changes (cutting, dissolving, changes of state) keep the same particles; chemical changes form new substances.
  • Gas formation, color change, a precipitate, heat or light, and new odors are signs of a chemical change; the test is whether new substances formed.
  • Extensive properties depend on the amount of sample; intensive properties do not and can identify a substance.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify each as a physical or chemical change: (a) a frozen IV bag thaws; (b) milk sours; (c) a nail rusts; (d) dry ice shrinks as it sits on a lab bench.

Hint

Ask whether a new substance forms. Changes of state are physical; souring and rusting make new substances.

Answer

(a) physical (melting); (b) chemical (bacteria turn sugars into lactic acid, a new substance); (c) chemical (iron forms iron oxide); (d) physical (sublimation of CO2).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which of these properties of copper are intensive: mass, color, melting point, volume, density?

Hint

An intensive property is the same no matter how much of the sample you have. Which properties double when the sample doubles?

Answer

Color, melting point, and density are intensive. Mass and volume are extensive.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Frost forms on a windshield overnight with no liquid water ever appearing. Name this change of state and say whether it is physical or chemical.

Hint

The water goes straight from gas to solid. What is that change of state called, and does it make a new substance?

Answer

Deposition (gas to solid). It is a physical change: the water molecules are unchanged.
