Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the number of digits in a measurement carries information about how carefully it was made.
  • Describe what Sections 1.3.1, 1.3.2, and 1.3.3 cover.

Two lab reports describe the same patient. One says hemoglobin "about 14." The other says 13.5 g/dL. Both may be true, but only the second tells you how well the value is known: to the nearest tenth of a gram per deciliter. The digits in a measurement are a promise. Every digit you write says "I measured this," and the last one says "I estimated this, carefully." Write too few, and you throw away information someone paid for. Write too many, and you claim a precision no instrument gave you.

That is the whole idea behind significant figures: the digits in a measurement that carry real meaning, all the certain digits plus one estimated digit. Science depends on honesty. When everyone reports exactly as many digits as they measured, a nurse, a lab scientist, and a pharmacist can trust each other's numbers, and you can trust your own.

Clinical Connection: Clinical Laboratory Science

A chemistry analyzer might compute a serum potassium of 4.1372 mmol/L, but the laboratory reports 4.1 mmol/L. The method is reliable to about a tenth of a millimole per liter, so the extra digits would suggest a precision the test does not have. Each lab value is reported with the number of digits its method supports: glucose often in whole mg/dL (for example, 92 mg/dL), hemoglobin to the tenth (13.5 g/dL). When you read a lab report, the digits tell you how far to trust the number, and when you record a measurement, your digits make the same promise. (Context only; reporting practices vary by laboratory.)

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

A bathroom scale shows 68 kg; a clinic scale shows 68.4 kg for the same person a minute later. Which reading tells you more, and why?

Hint

Which reading has more significant figures, and to what place is each known?

Answer

The clinic reading, 68.4 kg. It has three significant figures and is known to the nearest tenth of a kilogram; 68 kg is known only to the nearest kilogram. (Whether either scale is accurate is a separate question; see Section 1.3.1.)
