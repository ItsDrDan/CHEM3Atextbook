Learning Objectives

  • Explain what "percent" means and convert between decimals and percents.
  • Identify the part and the total in a mass percent.
  • Write a percent as a conversion factor, either way up, and use it in dimensional analysis.
  • Calculate a mass percent from the masses of the parts.

Percents are everywhere in chemistry and in health care: a 0.9% saline IV bag, a fertilizer labeled 34-0-0, a lab report with a 92% yield, the fact that the human body is roughly 60% water. Each of those percents hides a conversion factor. Once you can see it, a percent problem becomes one more dimensional-analysis problem (Section 1.4.4).

What "Percent" Means

Read the word in two pieces: per cent. "Per" is the same giveaway word you look for in any conversion factor, and "cent" means one hundred, as in the 100 cents in a dollar or the 100 years in a century. So a percent is always a number out of 100:

\( 12\% = \dfrac{12}{100} = 0.12 \)

  • Percent to decimal: divide by 100 (94.2% = 0.942).
  • Decimal to percent: multiply by 100 (0.373 = 37.3%).

Any percent compares a part with the total, like one slice compared with a whole pizza:

\( \text{percent} = \dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{total}} \times 100\% \)

In chemistry the most common kind is mass percent (also called percent by mass), where the part and the total are both masses.

A Percent Is a Conversion Factor in Disguise

Because a percent is "out of 100," you can write any mass percent as a conversion factor between the part and the total. Sterling silver is 92.5% silver by mass. That means

\( \dfrac{92.5\;\text{g silver}}{100\;\text{g sterling silver}} \qquad \text{or, flipped,} \qquad \dfrac{100\;\text{g sterling silver}}{92.5\;\text{g silver}} \)

The 100 is exact (it is part of the definition of percent), so it never limits significant figures. Any mass unit works, as long as the part and the total use the same one: 92.5 kg silver per 100 kg sterling silver is the same ratio. The table below gives a few more to practice reading.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Find the part from the total

Problem. Sodium chloride is 39.3% sodium by mass. How many grams of sodium are in 750. mg of sodium chloride?

Given: 750. mg NaCl (stands alone; the decimal point makes 3 significant figures); 39.3 g Na / 100 g NaCl. Desired: ??? g Na.

Solution. Convert to grams, then use the percent with NaCl on the bottom so it cancels:

\( {750.\;\text{mg NaCl}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{g}}{1000\;\text{mg}} \times \dfrac{39.3\;\text{g Na}}{100\;\text{g NaCl}} = {0.295\;\text{g Na}} \)

Check. A bit less than 40% of 0.750 g is a bit less than 0.30 g. Three significant figures: 0.295 g Na. Sodium is the part, so it must be less than the total.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Flip the factor to find the total

Problem. A Central Valley almond grower needs to apply 15.0 kg of nitrogen to a block of trees. Her fertilizer is 34.0% nitrogen by mass. What mass of fertilizer does she need?

Given: 15.0 kg N (stands alone); 34.0 kg N / 100 kg fertilizer. Desired: ??? kg fertilizer.

Solution. This time the part is given and the total is wanted, so flip the percent to put nitrogen on the bottom:

\( {15.0\;\text{kg N}} \times \dfrac{100\;\text{kg fertilizer}}{34.0\;\text{kg N}} = {44.1\;\text{kg fertilizer}} \)

Check. Only about a third of the fertilizer is nitrogen, so she needs about three times as much fertilizer as nitrogen: 3 × 15 = 45 kg. The total must be larger than the part. Three significant figures: 44.1 kg.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Calculate a mass percent from the parts

Problem. A sample of a compound contains 9.00 g of carbon and 2.25 g of hydrogen and nothing else. What is the mass percent of carbon?

Solution. The total is the sum of the parts: 9.00 g + 2.25 g = 11.25 g.

\( \%\;\text{C} = \dfrac{9.00\;\text{g C}}{11.25\;\text{g sample}} \times 100\% = 80.0\%\;\text{C} \)

Check. The hydrogen is 2.25 ÷ 11.25 × 100% = 20.0%, and 80.0% + 20.0% = 100.0%, as the parts of any whole must. A common slip is dividing by the mass of the other part (9.00 ÷ 2.25 gives 400%), which is impossible for a part of a whole.

Professor Tip

The moment you see a percent in a problem, write it as a fraction over 100 with both substances labeled: "34.0% N" becomes 34.0 g N / 100 g fertilizer. That one step is the hardest part of every percent problem; after it, the problem is ordinary dimensional analysis. Then ask the size check: a part is always smaller than its total.

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

Water makes up roughly 50–60% of an adult's body mass, a bit more in lean adults and much more in infants (about 75%). For an adult who is 60.% water (two significant figures) and weighs 70.0 kg, that is 70.0 kg × (60. kg water / 100 kg body) = 42 kg of water. Clinicians use percents like this, along with laboratory values reported as percents (such as hematocrit, the percent of blood volume made up of red blood cells), every day. You will meet concentrations written as percents again in Section 11.3, and the percent composition of compounds in Section 6.6.

Common Mistakes

  • Using the percent as a plain number (multiplying by 39.3 instead of 39.3/100).
  • Leaving the substances off the percent factor, then canceling grams of the part against grams of the total.
  • Not flipping the factor when the part is given and the total is wanted. Check: the total must come out larger than the part.
  • Dividing by the other part instead of by the total when calculating a mass percent.

Summary

  • Percent means "out of 100": divide by 100 for a decimal, multiply a decimal by 100 for a percent.
  • Mass percent = (mass of part ÷ mass of total) × 100%.
  • A percent is a conversion factor: X% A in B = X g A / 100 g B, usable either way up. The 100 is exact.
  • A part is always smaller than the total; the parts of a whole add to 100%.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write 0.0825 as a percent, and 4.6% as a decimal.

Hint

To make a percent, multiply the decimal by 100; to make a decimal, divide the percent by 100.

Answer

0.0825 × 100 = 8.25%; 4.6 ÷ 100 = 0.046.

Percent means "per hundred," so a decimal becomes a percent by multiplying by 100 and a percent becomes a decimal by dividing by 100.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A copper ore is 0.25% copper by mass. What mass of ore, in kilograms, contains 1.00 kg of copper?

Hint

Write the percent as a factor with both substances labeled: 0.25 kg Cu / 100 kg ore. The part is given and the total is wanted, so flip it so Cu cancels.

Answer

\( {1.00\;\text{kg Cu}} \times \dfrac{100\;\text{kg ore}}{0.25\;\text{kg Cu}} = {400\;\text{kg ore}} \) → 4.0 × 102 kg ore (0.25% has 2 significant figures).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A 12.0 g piece of sterling silver (92.5% silver by mass) is in a ring. What mass of silver does it contain, and what mass is other metals?

Hint

Write 92.5% as 92.5 g Ag / 100 g sterling. The rest of the mass is everything that is not silver.

Answer

12.0 g × (92.5 g Ag / 100 g sterling) = 11.1 g silver; the other metals are 12.0 g − 11.1 g = 0.9 g (one decimal place in the subtraction).
