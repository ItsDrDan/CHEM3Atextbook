Learning Objectives

  • Read a solubility curve and use it to classify a solution and predict crystallization.
  • Explain how a supersaturated solution is made by cooling a saturated one.
  • Describe how temperature affects the solubility of solids and of gases.
  • Use Henry's law to relate the solubility of a gas to its partial pressure.

Hot tea dissolves more sugar than iced tea, but a warm soda goes flat faster than a cold one. Temperature affects solids and gases in opposite ways, and for gases, pressure matters too.

Temperature and the Solubility of Solids

For most solid solutes, solubility increases as temperature rises: the faster-moving solvent molecules break up the solid more easily. A solubility curve plots solubility (grams per 100 g of water) against temperature. Some solids, such as KNO3, become far more soluble when hot; others, such as NaCl, change very little. You have seen this in the kitchen: sugar dissolves quickly in hot tea but sits at the bottom of iced tea or iced coffee, which is why coffee shops sweeten cold drinks with liquid syrup instead.

A point on a curve is a saturated solution; a point below the curve is unsaturated; a point above it is supersaturated (Section 11.1). If a saturated solution is cooled, the excess solute usually crystallizes; chemists use this to purify solids.

Labeling a Solution from the Curve

Every point on the graph is a temperature (x axis) and a number of grams dissolved in 100 g of water (y axis). To label a solution:

  1. Find the temperature on the x axis and trace straight up to the curve for your substance.
  2. Trace across to the y axis and read the solubility: the most that can dissolve at that temperature.
  3. Compare the grams actually dissolved: on the line is saturated, below it is unsaturated, and above it is supersaturated.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Reading a solubility curve

Problem. The solubility of KNO3 is 110 g per 100 g of water at 60 °C and 31.6 g per 100 g at 20 °C. A student dissolves 80.0 g of KNO3 in 100. g of water at 60 °C. (a) Is the solution saturated? (b) If it is cooled to 20 °C, about how much KNO3 crystallizes?

Solution. (a) Unsaturated: 80.0 g is less than the 110 g that can dissolve at 60 °C. (b) At 20 °C only 31.6 g stays dissolved, so 80.0 − 31.6 = 48.4 g crystallizes.

Check. Cooling moves the point from below the curve to above it, so solid must come out until the solution is saturated again.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): On, below, or above the line

Problem. Each solute below is fully dissolved in 100. g of water. Label each solution unsaturated, saturated, or supersaturated. (a) 40 g of NaCl at 100 °C (use Figure 1). (b) 20.0 g of KNO3 at 60 °C (the solubility is 110 g, from Example 1). (c) 100. g of NaNO3 at 10 °C; a full solubility chart, not shown here, reads about 80 g for NaNO3 at 10 °C.

Solution. (a) At 100 °C the NaCl curve reads about 40 g, so this point sits on the line: saturated. (b) 20.0 g is far below 110 g: unsaturated; about 90 g more could still dissolve. (c) 100. g is above the 80 g limit: supersaturated.

Check. Each comparison uses the same 100 g of water and the same temperature as the curve reading. A supersaturated point like (c) can only exist if the solution was made hot and cooled without being disturbed.

Making a Supersaturated Solution by Cooling

The curve also shows how a supersaturated solution (Section 11.1) is made. Dissolve 110 g of KNO3 in 100. g of water at 60 °C: that point is on the curve, a saturated solution. Now let it cool slowly to 20 °C without stirring or bumping it. The curve says only 31.6 g can stay dissolved at 20 °C, yet if no crystal starts to form, all 110 g stays in solution. The point is now 78.4 g above the curve: the solution is supersaturated. One seed crystal, a scratch on the glass, or a bump, and about 78 g of KNO3 crystallizes at once, leaving a saturated solution behind. Example 1 is the same cooling, but there the excess came out as the solution cooled.

Temperature and the Solubility of Gases

Gases behave the opposite way: their solubility in water decreases as temperature rises. Faster-moving gas molecules escape from the liquid more easily (Chapter 10). Warm soda loses its fizz faster, and warm lakes and rivers hold less dissolved oxygen, which can stress or kill fish when water warms in summer or near warm discharges.

Why the opposite trend? Use kinetic molecular theory (Section 10.1). Dissolved gas molecules are not held in a crystal; they behave like billiard balls bouncing around inside the liquid. A higher temperature means more kinetic energy, so they bounce harder and more of them break away from the liquid's surface into the air. Picture a stadium crowd after a game-winning goal: the more energy in the room, the harder it is to keep everyone in their seats. In the solution, nothing holds the gas molecules back except weak attractions to water, so heating sends them out.

Pressure and Gas Solubility: Henry's Law

Pressure has almost no effect on solids and liquids, but it matters a great deal for gases. Henry's law says that, at constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid:

\( \dfrac{S_1}{P_1} = \dfrac{S_2}{P_2} \qquad (\text{same gas, same temperature}) \)

Double the pressure of a gas above a liquid and twice as much of it dissolves. A sealed soda is bottled under high CO2 pressure; when you open it, the CO2 pressure above the liquid drops, the gas becomes less soluble, and bubbles form. Every time you open a 2-liter bottle, the CO2 in the space above the soda escapes; after you recap it, more CO2 leaves the liquid to refill that space. So the soda goes a little flatter with each opening, and opening it fewer times keeps it fizzy longer.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Carbonating a drink

Problem. At 25 °C, 1.45 g of CO2 dissolves in 1.00 L of water when the CO2 pressure is 1.00 atm. How much dissolves in 1.00 L at a CO2 pressure of 3.60 atm and the same temperature?

Solution.

\( S_2 = S_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = 1.45\;\text{g/L} \times \dfrac{3.60\;\text{atm}}{1.00\;\text{atm}} = 5.22\;\text{g/L} \)

Check. More pressure, more dissolved gas: a direct relationship, like Charles's law in Section 10.3.

Professor Tip

Remember the opposites: solids usually dissolve better when hot; gases always dissolve worse when hot. Pressure matters only for gases, and more pressure means more dissolved gas. Picture a can of soda: cold and sealed keeps the fizz in; warm and open lets it out.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

Henry's law is why breathing oxygen helps. The amount of oxygen dissolved in plasma is proportional to the partial pressure of O2 in the alveoli (Section 10.6), so raising the inspired oxygen raises the dissolved oxygen, which then loads onto hemoglobin. In a hyperbaric chamber, a patient breathes 100% oxygen at two to three times atmospheric pressure, so far more oxygen dissolves in the plasma than is possible at 1 atm. Hyperbaric oxygen is used for carbon monoxide poisoning, decompression sickness, and some hard-to-heal wounds. The reverse also matters: a diver who rises too fast lowers the pressure on nitrogen dissolved in the blood, and the nitrogen can come out of solution as bubbles, causing decompression sickness ("the bends"). (Context only; follow the orders and protocols.)

Biology Connection: Oxygen in Warm Water

Fish and other aquatic animals breathe oxygen dissolved in water, and there is far less of it than in air: about 9 mg per liter at 20 °C, compared with about 280 mg of O2 in a liter of air. Because warm water holds even less (Figure 1), a hot spell can drop dissolved oxygen below what fish need, especially when algae blooms die and decomposing bacteria use up the remaining oxygen. Salmon and trout need cold, well-oxygenated water, which is one reason cold-water releases from reservoirs are managed in the rivers below dams. The same chemistry is why a warming climate is especially hard on aquatic life: what do fish need to live? Oxygen dissolved in the water, and warmer oceans and rivers hold less of it, which can mean fewer fish and smaller ones.

Common Mistakes

  • Applying the solid rule to gases: gases are less soluble at higher temperature.
  • Thinking pressure affects the solubility of solids: it matters only for gases.
  • Reading a solubility curve for the wrong amount of water: values are per 100 g of water.
  • Tracing the wrong curve: on a chart with several salts, follow the line for your substance all the way up from the temperature.
  • Calling a point above the curve impossible: it is a supersaturated solution, made by cooling carefully, and it is unstable.

Summary

  • Most solids: solubility rises with temperature. Gases: solubility falls with temperature, because hotter gas molecules have more kinetic energy and escape.
  • On a solubility curve: on the line = saturated, below = unsaturated, above = supersaturated. Cooling a saturated solution without disturbing it makes it supersaturated.
  • Henry's law: gas solubility is proportional to the gas's partial pressure, S1/P1 = S2/P2.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Using Figure 1, about how much NaCl dissolves in 100. g of water at 80 °C? If 50.0 g of NaCl is stirred into 100. g of water at 80 °C, what happens?

Hint

Find 80 °C on the x axis of Figure 1, trace up to the NaCl curve and across to read grams per 100 g of water. Compare 50.0 g with that limit.

Answer

About 38 g dissolves. The extra 12 g or so stays undissolved on the bottom, and the solution above it is saturated.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Why does a cold soda stay fizzy longer after opening than a warm one?

Hint

How does temperature affect the solubility of a gas, the opposite of most solids? Think about how fast the CO2 molecules move.

Answer

CO2, like all gases, is more soluble in cold water, so less of it leaves solution as bubbles.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

The solubility of N2 in blood at a partial pressure of 0.78 atm is S. What is its solubility when a diver is at a depth where the N2 partial pressure is 2.34 atm?

Hint

Henry's law: S1/P1 = S2/P2 at constant temperature. By what factor did the N2 partial pressure increase?

Answer

S2 = S × 2.34/0.78 = 3.0 S: three times as much N2 dissolves.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

The solubility of KNO3 is 31.6 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C. A clear solution at 20 °C holds 50.0 g of KNO3 in 100. g of water. (a) Label it. (b) How was it most likely made? (c) What happens if a crystal of KNO3 is dropped in?

Hint

(a) Compare 50.0 g with the 20 °C limit. (b) How can extra solute stay dissolved? Think about cooling a hot solution. (c) Only the amount above the limit comes out.

Answer

(a) Supersaturated: 50.0 g is above the 31.6 g limit. (b) By dissolving the KNO3 in hot water and cooling it carefully without disturbing it. (c) The extra 18.4 g crystallizes, leaving a saturated solution.
