Learning Objectives

  • Read a solubility curve and use it to classify a solution and predict crystallization.
  • Describe how temperature affects the solubility of solids and of gases.
  • Use Henry's law to relate the solubility of a gas to its partial pressure.

Hot tea dissolves more sugar than iced tea, but a warm soda goes flat faster than a cold one. Temperature affects solids and gases in opposite ways, and for gases, pressure matters too.

Temperature and the Solubility of Solids

For most solid solutes, solubility increases as temperature rises: the faster-moving solvent molecules break up the solid more easily. A solubility curve plots solubility (grams per 100 g of water) against temperature. Some solids, such as KNO3, become far more soluble when hot; others, such as NaCl, change very little.

A point on a curve is a saturated solution; a point below the curve is unsaturated; a point above it is supersaturated (Section 11.1). If a saturated solution is cooled, the excess solute usually crystallizes; chemists use this to purify solids.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Reading a solubility curve

Problem. The solubility of KNO3 is 110 g per 100 g of water at 60 °C and 31.6 g per 100 g at 20 °C. A student dissolves 80.0 g of KNO3 in 100. g of water at 60 °C. (a) Is the solution saturated? (b) If it is cooled to 20 °C, about how much KNO3 crystallizes?

Solution. (a) Unsaturated: 80.0 g is less than the 110 g that can dissolve at 60 °C. (b) At 20 °C only 31.6 g stays dissolved, so 80.0 − 31.6 = 48.4 g crystallizes.

Check. Cooling moves the point from below the curve to above it, so solid must come out until the solution is saturated again.

Temperature and the Solubility of Gases

Gases behave the opposite way: their solubility in water decreases as temperature rises. Faster-moving gas molecules escape from the liquid more easily (Chapter 10). Warm soda loses its fizz faster, and warm lakes and rivers hold less dissolved oxygen, which can stress or kill fish when water warms in summer or near warm discharges.

Pressure and Gas Solubility: Henry's Law

Pressure has almost no effect on solids and liquids, but it matters a great deal for gases. Henry's law says that, at constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid:

\( \dfrac{S_1}{P_1} = \dfrac{S_2}{P_2} \qquad (\text{same gas, same temperature}) \)

Double the pressure of a gas above a liquid and twice as much of it dissolves. A sealed soda is bottled under high CO2 pressure; when you open it, the CO2 pressure above the liquid drops, the gas becomes less soluble, and bubbles form.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Carbonating a drink

Problem. At 25 °C, 1.45 g of CO2 dissolves in 1.00 L of water when the CO2 pressure is 1.00 atm. How much dissolves in 1.00 L at a CO2 pressure of 3.60 atm and the same temperature?

Solution.

\( S_2 = S_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = 1.45\ \text{g/L} \times \dfrac{3.60\ \text{atm}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 5.22\ \text{g/L} \)

Check. More pressure, more dissolved gas: a direct relationship, like Charles's law in Section 10.3.

Professor Tip

Remember the opposites: solids usually dissolve better when hot; gases always dissolve worse when hot. Pressure matters only for gases, and more pressure means more dissolved gas. Picture a can of soda: cold and sealed keeps the fizz in; warm and open lets it out.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

Henry's law is why breathing oxygen helps. The amount of oxygen dissolved in plasma is proportional to the partial pressure of O2 in the alveoli (Section 10.6), so raising the inspired oxygen raises the dissolved oxygen, which then loads onto hemoglobin. In a hyperbaric chamber, a patient breathes 100% oxygen at two to three times atmospheric pressure, so far more oxygen dissolves in the plasma than is possible at 1 atm. Hyperbaric oxygen is used for carbon monoxide poisoning, decompression sickness, and some hard-to-heal wounds. The reverse also matters: a diver who rises too fast lowers the pressure on nitrogen dissolved in the blood, and the nitrogen can come out of solution as bubbles, causing decompression sickness ("the bends"). (Context only; follow the orders and protocols.)

Biology Connection: Oxygen in Warm Water

Fish and other aquatic animals breathe oxygen dissolved in water, and there is far less of it than in air: about 9 mg per liter at 20 °C, compared with about 280 mg of O2 in a liter of air. Because warm water holds even less (Figure 1), a hot spell can drop dissolved oxygen below what fish need, especially when algae blooms die and decomposing bacteria use up the remaining oxygen. Salmon and trout need cold, well-oxygenated water, which is one reason cold-water releases from reservoirs are managed in the rivers below dams.

Common Mistakes

  • Applying the solid rule to gases: gases are less soluble at higher temperature.
  • Thinking pressure affects the solubility of solids: it matters only for gases.
  • Reading a solubility curve for the wrong amount of water: values are per 100 g of water.

Summary

  • Most solids: solubility rises with temperature. Gases: solubility falls with temperature.
  • Henry's law: gas solubility is proportional to the gas's partial pressure, S1/P1 = S2/P2.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Using Figure 1, about how much NaCl dissolves in 100. g of water at 80 °C? If 50.0 g of NaCl is stirred into 100. g of water at 80 °C, what happens?

Answer

About 38 g dissolves. The extra 12 g or so stays undissolved on the bottom, and the solution above it is saturated.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Why does a cold soda stay fizzy longer after opening than a warm one?

Answer

CO2, like all gases, is more soluble in cold water, so less of it leaves solution as bubbles.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

The solubility of N2 in blood at a partial pressure of 0.78 atm is S. What is its solubility when a diver is at a depth where the N2 partial pressure is 2.34 atm?

Answer

S2 = S × 2.34/0.78 = 3.0 S: three times as much N2 dissolves.
