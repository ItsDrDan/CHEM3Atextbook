Learning Objectives

  • Predict solubility from intermolecular forces, temperature, and pressure.
  • Calculate and convert concentrations (percent, ppm, molarity, molality).
  • Prepare and dilute solutions, find the concentration of each ion, and do solution stoichiometry (including limiting reactant) and titration calculations.
  • Use osmolality and colligative properties to explain freezing, boiling, and osmosis.

How to Use This Guide

Read the Key Ideas after the sections, use the video moments for anything still unclear, work the examples with the answers covered, and then go to the Practice Problems. Most of this chapter is one idea used many ways: a concentration is a conversion factor between an amount of solute and an amount of solution. Write the units in full and let them cancel. Each Key Idea ends with the section where it is taught.

Key Ideas

1. Like dissolves like

Polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents such as water; nonpolar in nonpolar. Ionic solutes dissociate, and the subscript becomes a coefficient (Na3PO4 → 3 Na+ + PO43−); molecular solutes such as glucose stay whole. Unsaturated (not enough) < saturated (just right) < supersaturated (too much, still dissolved, unstable). (Section 11.1)

2. Temperature and pressure

Most solids dissolve better when hot; gases dissolve worse when hot, because hotter gas molecules have more kinetic energy and escape. On a solubility curve: on the line = saturated, below = unsaturated, above = supersaturated. Henry's law: gas solubility is proportional to its partial pressure. (Section 11.2)

3. Concentration units

%(m/m) and %(m/v) use the whole solution; a percent is a conversion factor both ways (start with the term by itself). 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L in water; molarity M = mol/L of solution, and 1 M = 1000 mM. (Section 11.3)

4. Molarity and dilution

mol = M × L. [ion] = (ions per formula unit) × [compound]. Dilution keeps moles constant: M1V1 = M2V2 with matching units and V2 the total volume. The stock is the most concentrated solution: you can only dilute down, and you fill to the line. (Section 11.4)

5. Solution stoichiometry and titration

V × M → mol → mole ratio → mol → V, M, or g. Grams, gas volumes, and solution volumes are three on-ramps to the same mole road map. Balance, write the givens under the equation, and start from the side with both V and M, with the term by itself. V and M for both reactants means a limiting-reactant problem. In a titration, H+ + OH− → H2O; the endpoint (color change) comes just after the equivalence point (moles match). (Section 11.5)

6. Colligative properties

Molality m = mol/kg solvent; osmolality = i × m. ΔTf = iKfm; ΔTb = iKbm. The main skill is finding ΔT (how much the freezing point drops or the boiling point rises); CHEM 3A problems usually stop there, and the new freezing or boiling point, if asked, is one last subtraction or addition. Solutions freeze lower and boil higher; dissolved ions also hold water with ion–dipole attractions, which is why a solution needs a higher temperature to boil. Any dissolved particle has an effect; more particles, bigger effect. Compare solutions by counting particles first (i × m). Water flows toward higher solute concentration; isotonic fluids such as 0.9% NaCl match blood. (Section 11.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Make a stock, then dilute it

Problem. (a) What mass of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) makes 100.0 mL of 0.500 M stock? (b) What is the molarity after 5.00 mL of the stock is diluted to 50.00 mL?

Solution.

\( \text{(a)}\;{0.1000\;\text{L}} \times 0.500\;\tfrac{\text{mol}}{\text{L}} \times 58.44\;\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{mol}} = {2.92\;\text{g}} \qquad \text{(b)}\;M_2 = \dfrac{(0.500\;M)({5.00\;\text{mL}})}{50.00\;\text{mL}} = 0.0500\;M \)

Check. A tenfold dilution lowers the molarity tenfold.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Titrating a diprotic acid

Problem. A 10.00 mL sample of H2SO4 requires 24.60 mL of 0.1200 M NaOH to reach the phenolphthalein endpoint. What is the molarity of the H2SO4?

H2SO4(aq) + 2 NaOH(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2 H2O(l)

Solution.

\( {0.02460\;\text{L}} \times 0.1200\;\tfrac{\text{mol NaOH}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol H}_2\text{SO}_4}{2\;\text{mol NaOH}} = {1.476 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{mol}} \qquad M = \dfrac{1.476 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{mol}}{0.01000\;\text{L}} = 0.1476\;M \)

Check. Forgetting the 1 : 2 ratio would double the answer.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Comparing freezing points

Problem. Rank 0.20 m KBr, 0.15 m CaCl2, and 0.30 m glucose from lowest to highest freezing point, and find the freezing point depression of the CaCl2 solution.

Solution. Particle molality (i × m): KBr 2 × 0.20 = 0.40; CaCl2 3 × 0.15 = 0.45; glucose 1 × 0.30 = 0.30. More particles, lower freezing point:

CaCl2 (lowest) < KBr < glucose (highest)

\( \Delta T_f = (1.86\;{}^\circ\text{C}/m)(0.45\;m) = 0.84\;{}^\circ\text{C} \)

If asked for the new freezing point: \( T_f = 0.00 - 0.84 = -0.84\;{}^\circ\text{C} \).

Check. The glucose solution has the highest molality but the fewest particles. Two significant figures come from 0.15 m.

Common Mistakes

  • Dividing by the mass of solvent instead of solution in a percent; using liters of solution for molality.
  • Forgetting to convert mL to L, or mM to M, before using molarity.
  • Using the water added, not the total volume, as V2 in a dilution.
  • Assuming every titration is 1 : 1; using a buret reading instead of the volume used.
  • Starting a setup with the molarity instead of the term by itself; missing a limiting-reactant problem when both solutions have a V and an M.
  • Forgetting the coefficient when finding an ion's concentration; ranking colligative effects by i alone when the molalities differ.
  • Forgetting i for ionic solutes; reversing the direction of osmosis.
