Learning Objectives

  • Use molarity as a conversion factor between volume of solution and moles of solute.
  • Calculate the mass of solute needed to prepare a solution of a given molarity.
  • Use a dissolving equation to find the moles and the concentration of each ion in a solution.
  • Use M1V1 = M2V2 to plan and calculate dilutions, including millimolar units, and choose which stock solution can be diluted.

Hospital pharmacies, clinical labs, and research labs rarely weigh out a solid every time they need a solution. They prepare a concentrated stock solution once and dilute small portions of it as needed. Both steps, making the stock and diluting it, are molarity calculations.

Molarity as a Conversion Factor

Because molarity is moles per liter, it converts between the volume of a solution and the moles of solute in it:

\( \text{mol} = M \times V\,(\text{L}) \qquad V\,(\text{L}) = \dfrac{\text{mol}}{M} \)

This is the "solution on-ramp" to the mole road map you used in Section 8.3.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Moles in a volume

Problem. How many moles of KCl are in 25.0 mL of 0.150 M KCl?

Solution.

\( 0.0250\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{0.150\ \text{mol KCl}}{1\ \text{L}} = 3.75 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol KCl} \)

Check. A fortieth of a liter of a 0.15 M solution holds a small fraction of a mole.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Preparing a solution

Problem. What mass of glucose (180.156 g/mol) is needed to prepare 500.0 mL of 0.250 M glucose? Describe how to make it.

Solution.

\( 0.5000\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{0.250\ \text{mol}}{1\ \text{L}} \times \dfrac{180.156\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 22.5\ \text{g glucose} \)

Weigh 22.5 g of glucose, transfer it to a 500-mL volumetric flask, dissolve it in about half a flask of water, then add water up to the line and mix.

Check. Adding the solute to 500.0 mL of water would give a little more than 500.0 mL of solution; filling to the line gives exactly 500.0 mL of solution.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Volume for a given amount

Problem. What volume of 0.500 M NaOH contains 0.0350 mol of NaOH?

Solution.

\( 0.0350\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{0.500\ \text{mol}} = 0.0700\ \text{L} = 70.0\ \text{mL} \)

Check. Dividing by molarity turns moles into liters.

Ions in Solution: Concentration of Each Ion

A bottle labeled "0.100 M Ba(OH)2" does not actually contain Ba(OH)2 units floating around. It contains barium ions and hydroxide ions. To work with the ions, write the dissolving equation (Section 11.1). The subscript becomes a coefficient, and that coefficient is a mole ratio:

Ba(OH)2(s) → Ba2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq)

One mole of Ba(OH)2 releases one mole of Ba2+ and two moles of OH−, so in any Ba(OH)2 solution [Ba2+] = [Ba(OH)2] and [OH−] = 2 × [Ba(OH)2]. In general:

\( [\text{ion}] = (\text{number of that ion per formula unit}) \times [\text{compound}] \)

This is the same particle counting that gives the van 't Hoff factor i in Section 11.6, and it is the first step of every pH problem with a strong base or strong acid in Chapter 12, where the whole question is how much H3O+ or OH− is in the solution.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Hydroxide ion from grams of a base

Problem. 4.28 g of barium hydroxide, Ba(OH)2, is dissolved in water to make 250.0 mL of solution. Find [Ba(OH)2], [Ba2+], and [OH−].

Given and Desire. Given 4.28 g Ba(OH)2 and 250.0 mL of solution; desire mol/L of each ion. Molar mass: 137.33 + 2(15.999 + 1.008) = 171.34 g/mol.

Map. g Ba(OH)2 → mol Ba(OH)2 → mol OH− (dissolving equation) → divide by liters of solution.

Solution.

\( 4.28\ \text{g Ba(OH)}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Ba(OH)}_2}{171.34\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol OH}^-}{1\ \text{mol Ba(OH)}_2} = 0.04996\ \text{mol OH}^- \qquad [\text{OH}^-] = \dfrac{0.04996\ \text{mol}}{0.2500\ \text{L}} = 0.200\ M \)

[Ba(OH)2] = 0.02498 mol ÷ 0.2500 L = 0.0999 M, so [Ba2+] = 0.0999 M and [OH−] = 0.200 M.

Check. [OH−] is twice [Ba(OH)2], as the coefficient 2 says. Three significant figures, from 4.28 g.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Volume that holds a given amount of one ion

Problem. What volume of 0.150 M Na3PO4 contains 0.0270 mol of Na+?

Solution. Start with the term by itself, 0.0270 mol Na+, not the molarity (a fraction that could be flipped either way). Na3PO4(s) → 3 Na+(aq) + PO43−(aq), so 3 mol Na+ = 1 mol Na3PO4.

\( 0.0270\ \text{mol Na}^+ \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Na}_3\text{PO}_4}{3\ \text{mol Na}^+} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{0.150\ \text{mol Na}_3\text{PO}_4} = 0.0600\ \text{L} = 60.0\ \text{mL} \)

Check. [Na+] is 3 × 0.150 = 0.450 M, and 0.0270 mol ÷ 0.450 mol/L = 0.0600 L, the same answer by a second route.

Dilution

Mix up a pitcher of Kool-Aid or lemonade, taste it, and it is far too sweet. What do you do? Add water. As you pour, the color fades and the taste weakens, but the amount of powder in the pitcher has not changed; the same powder is spread through more volume. That is a dilution: when the volume goes up, the concentration goes down.

To dilute a solution, add more solvent. The moles of solute do not change; they are just spread through a larger volume, so the concentration goes down. Since moles = M × V before and after,

\( M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2 \)

Subscript 1 is the concentrated (stock) solution and the volume of it you use; subscript 2 is the diluted solution and its final total volume. Like Boyle's law (Section 10.3), this is an inverse relationship: a larger final volume means a lower concentration. The same form works with any concentration unit (M, mM, %) and any volume unit, as long as each matches on both sides. (You may also see it written C1V1 = C2V2, with C for any concentration.)

A stock solution is the most concentrated solution you have on hand, the bottle on the stockroom shelf. Dilution only goes one way: adding water can take a solution down in concentration, never up. So the stock is always side 1, and any solution you make from it must be weaker than it.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Diluting a saline stock

Problem. A 10.00 mL portion of 1.80 M NaCl is diluted to a total volume of 25.00 mL. What is the new molarity?

Solution.

\( M_2 = \dfrac{M_1 V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{(1.80\ M)(10.00\ \text{mL})}{25.00\ \text{mL}} = 0.720\ M \)

Check. The volume grew 2.5 times, so the concentration dropped by a factor of 2.5.

Example \(\PageIndex{7}\): Mateo's nebulizer

Problem. A respiratory therapist, following an order, draws up 0.500 mL of a 0.500% (m/v) medication solution and adds sterile saline to a total volume of 3.00 mL. What is the concentration in the nebulizer cup?

Solution. The units match on both sides, so use percent directly:

\( C_2 = \dfrac{C_1 V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{(0.500\%)(0.500\ \text{mL})}{3.00\ \text{mL}} = 0.0833\% \)

Check. The volume increased 6 times, so the concentration is one-sixth as large. (Context only; medications are prepared exactly as ordered and checked against the label.)

Example \(\PageIndex{8}\): Planning a dilution in millimolar

Problem. How many milliliters of 0.600 M stock are needed to make 250.0 mL of 15.0 mM solution?

Solution. Make the units match: 15.0 mM = 0.0150 M (1 M = 1000 mM).

\( V_1 = \dfrac{M_2 V_2}{M_1} = \dfrac{(0.0150\ M)(250.0\ \text{mL})}{0.600\ M} = 6.25\ \text{mL} \)

Pipet 6.25 mL of the stock into a 250-mL volumetric flask and fill to the line with deionized water.

Check. The stock is 40 times more concentrated, so you need one-fortieth of the final volume.

You could calculate that 250.0 − 6.25 = 243.75 mL of water is needed, but do not measure that out. The volumetric flask is calibrated to hold exactly 250.0 mL at its line, so filling to the line is both easier and more accurate than measuring the water separately.

Example \(\PageIndex{9}\): Which stock can you use?

Problem. You need 2.00 L of 0.900% (m/v) NaCl. The stockroom has two bottles: 0.450% NaCl and 23.4% NaCl. Which one can you dilute, and what volume of it do you need?

Solution. Dilution only lowers a concentration. 0.450% is already weaker than 0.900%, so no amount of water can turn it into 0.900%. Use the 23.4% stock. The percents match on both sides, so use them directly:

\( V_1 = \dfrac{C_2 V_2}{C_1} = \dfrac{(0.900\%)(2.00\ \text{L})}{23.4\%} = 0.0769\ \text{L} = 76.9\ \text{mL} \)

Measure 76.9 mL of the 23.4% stock and add water to a total volume of 2.00 L.

Check. The stock is 26 times as concentrated as the target (23.4 ÷ 0.900 = 26.0), so you need one twenty-sixth of the final volume: 2000 mL ÷ 26.0 = 76.9 mL. (Context only; concentrated NaCl is a high-alert product, and IV solutions are prepared by pharmacies under protocol.)

Professor Tip

Before you plug into M1V1 = M2V2, label each number: which concentration is the strong one (1) and which is the weak one (2)? Then check that the units match on both sides (convert mM to M or M to mM). Finally, check the direction: the diluted concentration must be smaller, and the final volume larger. Remember that V2 is the total final volume, not the volume of water added.

In the Lab: Experiment 9a, Solutions and Dilutions

This experiment is Examples 5 and 6 of Section 11.3 and Example 6 here, with your own numbers. You weigh roughly 5 g of NaCl into a 50-mL volumetric flask and fill it to the line to make stock "Solution A," then pipet exactly 10.00 mL of it into a 25-mL volumetric flask and fill to the line to make "Solution B." By weighing the flasks before and after, you find each solution's mass percent, mass/volume percent, molarity, and density, and you can check that M1V1 = M2V2 predicts Solution B's molarity. Volumetric glassware is so precise that its volumes count as 50.00, 25.00, and 10.00 mL.

Biology Connection: Serial Dilutions in Microbiology

How many bacteria are in a milliliter of milk or a urine sample? Far too many to count directly. Microbiologists make a serial dilution: 1.0 mL of sample is added to 9.0 mL of sterile saline (a 1-to-10 dilution), then 1.0 mL of that is diluted again by 10, and so on. After six steps the sample is diluted 106 times. Spreading 0.1 mL of a dilute tube on an agar plate gives a countable number of colonies, and multiplying back by the dilution factor gives the original count. Each step is M1V1 = M2V2 with "cells per mL" as the concentration.

Common Mistakes

  • Mixing M and mM (or L and mL) in M1V1 = M2V2.
  • Using the volume of water added as V2: V2 is the total final volume.
  • Getting a diluted concentration larger than the stock: the numbers are swapped.
  • Forgetting to convert mL to L when using molarity to find moles.
  • Forgetting the coefficient for an ion: in Ba(OH)2, [OH−] is twice the molarity of the compound.
  • Choosing a weaker stock: dilution only goes down, so the stock must be more concentrated than the solution you want.

Summary

  • mol = M × L; L = mol ÷ M; grams from moles with molar mass.
  • [ion] = (ions per formula unit) × [compound], from the dissolving equation.
  • Dilution: moles unchanged; M1V1 = M2V2 with matching units; V2 is the total volume. The stock is the most concentrated solution; dilute down only, and fill to the line.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many grams of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) are in 250.0 mL of 0.154 M NaCl, the molarity of normal saline?

Hint

Liters times molarity gives moles (change mL to L first); then multiply by the molar mass to get grams.

Answer

0.2500 L × 0.154 mol/L = 0.03850 mol; × 58.44 g/mol = 2.25 g.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

What is the molarity after 15.0 mL of 6.00 M HCl is diluted to 250.0 mL?

Hint

Dilution: M1V1 = M2V2. The stock is side 1, and V2 is the total final volume. The answer must be weaker than the stock.

Answer

(6.00)(15.0) ÷ 250.0 = 0.360 M.

The moles of HCl do not change on dilution, so M2 = M1V1/V2; the mL units cancel because both volumes are in mL.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What volume of 2.00 M KCl stock is needed to prepare 100.0 mL of 0.0500 M KCl?

Hint

Label the strong solution 1 and the weak one 2 in M1V1 = M2V2, then solve for V1.

Answer

V1 = (0.0500)(100.0) ÷ 2.00 = 2.50 mL.

All the moles in the final solution come from the stock, so V1 = M2V2/M1; the stock is 40 times as concentrated, so you need one-fortieth of 100.0 mL.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

What is the chloride ion concentration in 0.250 M AlCl3?

Hint

Write the dissolving equation. The subscript on Cl becomes a coefficient, and that coefficient multiplies the compound's molarity.

Answer

AlCl3(s) → Al3+(aq) + 3 Cl−(aq), so [Cl−] = 3 × 0.250 M = 0.750 M.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

5.00 mL of 1.20 M KCl is diluted to a concentration of 30.0 mM. What is the final volume?

Hint

Make the units match first: change mM to M (or M to mM). Then solve M1V1 = M2V2 for V2.

Answer

Match units: 30.0 mM = 0.0300 M. V2 = (1.20 M)(5.00 mL) ÷ 0.0300 M = 200. mL. (Or convert the stock: 1.20 M = 1200 mM, and (1200 mM)(5.00 mL) ÷ 30.0 mM = 200. mL.)
