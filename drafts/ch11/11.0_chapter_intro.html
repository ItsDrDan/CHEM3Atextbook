Patient Case: Mateo's IV Bag and Nebulizer

During his overnight admission (Chapter 10), Mateo Herrera has an IV line in his arm. Hanging above it is a 1-liter bag labeled 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection, USP, the fluid most people call normal saline. Every 100 mL of it contains 0.9 g of NaCl. Why that exact number? Because at that concentration the fluid has about the same total concentration of dissolved particles as his blood, so his red blood cells neither swell nor shrink as it flows in. A bag of pure water would make them swell and burst.

A few hours later, a respiratory therapist prepares a nebulizer treatment as ordered: a small volume of a concentrated medication solution is diluted with sterile saline to the volume the nebulizer needs, and the mist carries the medication into his airways. Both jobs, choosing the IV fluid and preparing the dilution, depend on concentration: how much solute is dissolved in how much solution.

In this chapter you will learn why some substances dissolve and others do not, how temperature and pressure change solubility, how to express and calculate concentrations, how to dilute solutions and use them in reactions and titrations, and how dissolved particles change the properties of water, including the osmosis that makes 0.9% saline the right choice. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Explain how solutions form and predict solubility with "like dissolves like" (Section 11.1).
  • Describe how temperature and pressure affect the solubility of solids and gases, including Henry's law (Section 11.2).
  • Calculate concentrations as mass percent, parts per million, and molarity (Section 11.3).
  • Use molarity as a conversion factor, prepare solutions, and do dilution calculations (Section 11.4).
  • Do solution stoichiometry and titration calculations, with liquid and solid knowns (Section 11.5).
  • Calculate molality and osmolality and explain freezing point depression, boiling point elevation, and osmosis (Section 11.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Nearly every fluid in health care is a solution: IV fluids, eye drops, mouth rinses, contrast agents, blood plasma, and urine. Nurses check concentrations on every medication label, laboratory technologists dilute samples and titrate, dental hygienists use fluoride and chlorhexidine solutions, and dietitians and physicians read serum osmolality on lab reports. This chapter builds on intermolecular forces (Chapter 9), stoichiometry (Chapter 8), and gas pressure (Chapter 10), and it leads straight into acids and bases (Chapter 12).
