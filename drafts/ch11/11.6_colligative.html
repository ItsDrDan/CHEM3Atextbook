Learning Objectives

  • Calculate molality and explain why it is used for colligative properties.
  • Count dissolved particles (i) and calculate osmolality.
  • Calculate freezing point depression and boiling point elevation, and explain osmosis and tonicity.
  • Explain at the particle level why dissolved solutes raise the boiling point, and rank solutions by their particle concentration (i × m).

Salt trucks spread NaCl on icy mountain roads, antifreeze keeps a car's radiator from freezing or boiling over, and a hospital chooses 0.9% saline rather than pure water for an IV. All three depend on a single fact: dissolved particles change the properties of water, and what matters is how many particles there are, not what they are.

Molality

Molality (m, "molal") is moles of solute per kilogram of solvent:

\( m = \dfrac{\text{mol solute}}{\text{kg solvent}} \)

Unlike molarity, molality uses the mass of the solvent, not the volume of the solution. Masses do not change with temperature, while volumes expand and contract, so molality is the unit used for properties that are measured as the temperature changes, such as freezing and boiling points. For dilute water solutions, molality and molarity are nearly equal, because 1 L of dilute solution contains about 1 kg of water.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Molality of a glucose solution

Problem. What is the molality of a solution made by dissolving 12.0 g of glucose (180.156 g/mol) in 250.0 g of water?

Solution.

\( m = \dfrac{12.0\ \text{g} \div 180.156\ \text{g/mol}}{0.2500\ \text{kg}} = \dfrac{0.06661\ \text{mol}}{0.2500\ \text{kg}} = 0.266\ m \)

Check. The denominator is kilograms of water (the solvent), converted from grams.

Counting Particles: i and Osmolality

Colligative properties depend on the number of dissolved particles. A molecular solute such as glucose stays as one particle; an ionic compound separates into its ions (Section 7.4). The number of particles per formula unit is the van 't Hoff factor, i:

  • glucose, urea, ethylene glycol (nonelectrolytes): i = 1
  • NaCl → Na+ + Cl−: i = 2
  • CaCl2 → Ca2+ + 2 Cl−: i = 3
  • Ca(C2H3O2)2 → Ca2+ + 2 C2H3O2−: i = 3 (the acetate ion stays together)
  • FeCl3 → Fe3+ + 3 Cl−: i = 4

Finding i is just counting: write the dissolving equation (Section 11.1) and add up the coefficients on the right.

The concentration of particles is the osmolality, in osmoles per kilogram of water (Osm/kg), or more often milliosmoles (mOsm/kg) in medicine:

\( \text{osmolality} = i \times m \)

Normal serum osmolality, the total particle concentration of blood plasma, is about 275 to 295 mOsm/kg, mostly from Na+, Cl−, HCO3−, glucose, and urea.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Why normal saline is "normal"

Problem. Normal saline contains about 9.00 g of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) per kilogram of water. Estimate its osmolality and compare it with serum.

Solution. m = 9.00 ÷ 58.44 = 0.154 mol/kg; i = 2.

\( \text{osmolality} = 2 \times 0.154 = 0.308\ \text{Osm/kg} = 308\ \text{mOsm/kg} \)

Check. That is close to serum's 275–295 mOsm/kg. (The measured value, about 286 mOsm/kg, is a little lower than this ideal estimate because the ions in real solutions do not act completely independently.) So normal saline is isotonic with blood.

Freezing Point Depression and Boiling Point Elevation

Dissolved particles get in the way of water molecules joining an ice crystal, so a solution freezes at a lower temperature than pure water (freezing point depression). They also hold onto water molecules (ion–dipole and hydrogen bonds) and lower the vapor pressure, so a solution boils at a higher temperature (boiling point elevation). Both changes are proportional to the molality of particles:

\( \Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m \qquad \Delta T_b = i\,K_b\,m \)

For water, Kf = 1.86 °C/m and Kb = 0.512 °C/m. Subtract ΔTf from 0.00 °C to get the new freezing point; add ΔTb to 100.00 °C to get the new boiling point.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Road salt

Problem. Calcium chloride is spread on icy Sierra highways. What is the freezing point of a 0.650 m CaCl2 solution?

Solution. CaCl2 gives 3 ions, so i = 3.

\( \Delta T_f = (3)(1.86\ {}^\circ\text{C}/m)(0.650\ m) = 3.63\ {}^\circ\text{C} \qquad T_f = 0.00 - 3.63 = -3.63\ {}^\circ\text{C} \)

Check. Three particles per formula unit make CaCl2 more effective per mole than NaCl (i = 2), which is one reason it is used when roads are very cold.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Boiling point of a sugar solution

Problem. What is the boiling point of a 1.50 m glucose solution at 1 atm?

Solution. Glucose is a nonelectrolyte, i = 1.

\( \Delta T_b = (1)(0.512\ {}^\circ\text{C}/m)(1.50\ m) = 0.768\ {}^\circ\text{C} \qquad T_b = 100.00 + 0.768 = 100.77\ {}^\circ\text{C} \)

Check. Boiling point elevation is small compared with freezing point depression, because Kb is much smaller than Kf.

Why Does a Solute Raise the Boiling Point?

Ask yourself two questions. When a liquid boils, what has to happen? Molecules have to leave the liquid and enter the gas phase, and to do that each one needs enough kinetic energy to break away from its neighbors. So does a higher boiling point mean it is easier or harder for water molecules to escape? Harder.

In pure water, the molecules are held back by hydrogen bonds. Dissolve salt, and the water molecules that crowd around each Na+ and Cl− are now held by ion–dipole attractions, which are stronger than hydrogen bonds (Section 9.6). The ions hang on to the water like a kid at a sleepover who does not want to go home. Water molecules now need more kinetic energy to break free, and that means a higher temperature before the solution boils. The more dissolved particles there are, the more water is held back, which is why ΔTb grows with i × m. (Salting pasta water raises its boiling point, but only a little: about 20 g of salt in 4 kg of water raises it by less than 0.1 °C. The salt is mostly there for flavor.)

Comparing Solutions: Count Particles First

Many questions ask which of several solutions has the lowest freezing point, the highest boiling point, or the greatest osmotic pressure. Use this checklist:

  1. Count particles. Write each dissolving equation and find i. Ionic solutes give 2, 3, or 4 particles; molecular solutes give 1.
  2. If the molalities are the same, the larger i wins: an ionic solute beats a molecular one, and FeCl3 (4) beats CaCl2 (3), which beats NaCl (2).
  3. If i is the same, the larger molality wins.
  4. If both differ, multiply: the larger i × m has the larger effect. Do not rank by i alone. 0.10 m NaCl (2 × 0.10 = 0.20 m of particles) has a smaller effect than 0.30 m glucose (0.30 m of particles).

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Same molality, different particles

Problem. Rank 0.10 m solutions of glucose, NaCl, Ca(C2H3O2)2, and FeCl3 from lowest to highest boiling point, and find the boiling point of the FeCl3 solution.

Solution. The molalities are equal, so count particles: glucose i = 1, NaCl i = 2, Ca(C2H3O2)2 i = 3, FeCl3 i = 4.

glucose (lowest) < NaCl < Ca(C2H3O2)2 < FeCl3 (highest)

\( \Delta T_b = (4)(0.512\ {}^\circ\text{C}/m)(0.10\ m) = 0.20\ {}^\circ\text{C} \qquad T_b = 100.00 + 0.20 = 100.20\ {}^\circ\text{C} \)

Check. Four particles per formula unit give four times glucose's elevation (0.051 °C). Two significant figures, from 0.10 m.

Osmosis and Tonicity

A semipermeable membrane, such as a cell membrane, lets water pass but blocks many solutes. Osmosis is the net flow of water across such a membrane from the side with lower solute (particle) concentration to the side with higher solute concentration, as if the solutes were pulling water toward them. The pressure needed to stop that flow is the osmotic pressure; it is larger when the difference in particle concentration is larger.

Build the idea in two steps. First, picture pure water on both sides of the membrane. Water molecules cross in both directions at the same rate, a dynamic equilibrium, so neither level changes. Now dissolve salt on one side. Water molecules are attracted to the ions and cluster around them, so more water crosses toward the salty side than leaves it, and the level on that side rises. The taller column of liquid pushes back harder and harder; when its pressure is large enough to make the two flows equal again, the level stops rising. That pressure is the osmotic pressure.

Your intestines work like a semipermeable membrane. Water you drink has far fewer dissolved particles than your blood and tissues, so it moves into the body. Salty seawater is the reverse: in the intestine it is a "thirsty" solution with more particles than body fluids, so it pulls water out of the body (the Biology Connection below explains why the kidneys cannot keep up).

  • Isotonic: same particle concentration as the cell; no net water flow.
  • Hypotonic: lower particle concentration outside; water flows in, and red blood cells swell and can burst (hemolysis).
  • Hypertonic: higher particle concentration outside; water flows out, and cells shrink (crenation).

Pickling is osmosis in the kitchen. A cucumber or jalapeño sitting in a salty brine is in a hypertonic solution, so water moves out of its cells into the brine, and the vegetable's texture and flavor change. Road salt (freezing point depression), antifreeze (both freezing point depression and boiling point elevation), and pickling (osmosis) are the three everyday faces of colligative properties.

Clinical Connection: Nursing and IV Fluids

IV fluids are chosen by tonicity. Isotonic fluids such as 0.9% NaCl (normal saline) and lactated Ringer's expand the volume of the blood without moving much water into or out of cells. Hypotonic fluids such as 0.45% NaCl move water into cells and are used to rehydrate cells in some kinds of dehydration. Hypertonic fluids such as 3% NaCl pull water out of cells and are used in specific situations such as swelling of the brain. 5% dextrose (D5W) is a special case: in the bag it is about isotonic, but once the body uses the glucose, what is left behaves like pure water. Giving the wrong tonicity can swell or shrink cells dangerously, so IV fluids are always given exactly as ordered and monitored. (Context only; not treatment advice.)

Biology Connection: Kidneys and Serum Osmolality

Your body holds serum osmolality within a narrow range. Sensors in the hypothalamus detect a rise in osmolality, for example after a salty meal or heavy sweating, and trigger thirst and the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). ADH makes the kidneys return more water to the blood, producing a smaller volume of more concentrated urine. When osmolality falls, ADH drops and the kidneys excrete dilute urine. Drinking seawater fails because seawater's osmolality, roughly 1000 mOsm/kg, is far higher than the most concentrated urine the human kidney can make, about 1200 mOsm/kg at best, so getting rid of the salt costs more water than the seawater provides.

Valley Connection: Salty Soils on the Westside

Plant roots take up water by osmosis, which works only when the water in the soil has a lower solute concentration than the fluid inside the root cells. On parts of the San Joaquin Valley's Westside, years of irrigation with water that carries dissolved salts, plus a shallow, poorly drained water table, have left salts building up in the soil. When the soil water becomes too salty, osmosis slows or even reverses, and crops wilt even in wet ground. Growers respond by leaching salts below the root zone with extra water, installing drainage, and planting more salt-tolerant crops; researchers at the USDA's salinity laboratory in Riverside and at the University of California rate crops by how much soil salinity they can tolerate.

Sources: USDA Agricultural Research Service, U.S. Salinity Laboratory (ars.usda.gov); University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources, salinity management in the San Joaquin Valley (ucanr.edu).

Professor Tip

For any colligative property, count particles first. Write the dissociation (CaCl2 → Ca2+ + 2 Cl−) to find i, then multiply by the molality. When comparing solutions, the one with the larger i × m freezes lowest, boils highest, and has the highest osmotic pressure. For osmosis, remember "water follows solute": it flows toward the side with more dissolved particles.

Common Mistakes

  • Using liters of solution for molality: molality is per kilogram of solvent.
  • Forgetting i for ionic solutes (NaCl gives 2 particles, CaCl2 gives 3).
  • Ranking by i alone when the molalities differ: multiply i × m.
  • Adding ΔTf to the freezing point: freezing points go down.
  • Reversing osmosis: water moves toward the higher solute concentration.

Summary

  • Molality m = mol solute / kg solvent; osmolality = i × m.
  • ΔTf = iKfm (Kf = 1.86 °C/m); ΔTb = iKbm (Kb = 0.512 °C/m). Dissolved ions hold water molecules with ion–dipole attractions, so a solution needs a higher temperature to boil. Any dissolved particle has an effect; more particles, bigger effect.
  • To compare solutions, count particles first; at equal molality the larger i wins, and in general the larger i × m wins.
  • Osmosis moves water toward higher particle concentration; isotonic, hypotonic, and hypertonic solutions keep, swell, or shrink cells.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

5.85 g of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) is dissolved in 500.0 g of water. Find the molality and the osmolality in mOsm/kg.

Answer

5.85 ÷ 58.44 = 0.1001 mol; ÷ 0.5000 kg = 0.200 m. Osmolality = 2 × 0.2002 = 0.400 Osm/kg = 400. mOsm/kg (hypertonic compared with blood).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which has the lower freezing point, 0.10 m NaCl or 0.15 m glucose? Explain.

Answer

0.10 m NaCl: its particle concentration is 2 × 0.10 = 0.20 m, more than glucose's 0.15 m.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Red blood cells are placed in 3% NaCl. What happens to them, and what is this solution called relative to the cells?

Answer

The solution is hypertonic; water flows out of the cells and they shrink (crenation).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Give the van 't Hoff factor i for (a) K3PO4; (b) Mg(NO3)2; (c) urea, CO(NH2)2.

Answer

(a) 4: 3 K+ + PO43−. (b) 3: Mg2+ + 2 NO3−. (c) 1: urea is molecular and stays whole.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Which has the higher boiling point, 0.10 m FeCl3 or 0.15 m CaCl2?

Answer

0.15 m CaCl2. FeCl3 has more particles per formula unit, but the molalities differ, so multiply: FeCl3 4 × 0.10 = 0.40 m of particles; CaCl2 3 × 0.15 = 0.45 m.
