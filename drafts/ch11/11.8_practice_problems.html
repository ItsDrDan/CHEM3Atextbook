11.1 Solutions and How They Form

Problem 11.1

Which substance is most soluble in water?

A. Hexane, C6H14
B. Carbon tetrachloride, CCl4
C. Glycerol, C3H8O3 (HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH), used in cough syrups and skin lotions
D. Iodine, I2

Hint

Like dissolves like: water is polar and hydrogen bonds. Check each molecule: is it polar, and can it hydrogen bond with water?

Answer

C. Glycerol has three O–H groups, and each one hydrogen bonds with water. Like dissolves like: a polar, hydrogen-bonding solute mixes with a polar, hydrogen-bonding solvent. Glycerol and water are miscible in all proportions, which is why glycerol is easy to blend into water-based syrups and lotions.

  • A is a hydrocarbon. It has only C and H, so it is nonpolar with only dispersion forces, and water squeezes it out into a separate layer.
  • B is a common trap. Each C–Cl bond is polar (ΔEN = 0.5), but the four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron and cancel (Chapter 6), so the molecule is nonpolar and barely dissolves in water.
  • D is nonpolar (two identical atoms), so it dissolves only slightly in water; it dissolves much better in nonpolar solvents.

Problem 11.2

Identify the solvent and the solute(s) in each solution, and give the state of the solution: (a) dry air, about 78% N2, 21% O2, and small amounts of argon and CO2; (b) sterling silver, 92.5% silver and 7.5% copper by mass; (c) club soda, carbon dioxide dissolved in water; (d) a 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate oral rinse (ignore the flavorings).

Hint

The solvent is the component present in the largest amount, whatever its state. The state of the solution matches the state of the solvent.

Answer

(a) Solvent N2 (the largest component); solutes O2, Ar, and CO2. A gaseous solution.

(b) Solvent silver; solute copper. A solid solution (an alloy), like the dental amalgam in Section 11.1.

(c) Solvent water; solute CO2, a gas dissolved in a liquid. A liquid (aqueous) solution.

(d) Solvent water; solute chlorhexidine gluconate, only 0.12% of the rinse. A liquid (aqueous) solution.

A solution can be a gas, liquid, or solid; the solvent is whichever component is present in the largest amount.

Problem 11.3

Each substance below dissolves in water. Which solution conducts electricity well?

A. Glucose, C6H12O6
B. Ethanol, CH3CH2OH
C. Potassium bromide, KBr
D. Urea, CH4N2O

Hint

A solution conducts only if it contains ions. Which choice is ionic (a metal with a nonmetal) rather than molecular?

Answer

C. KBr is ionic. When it dissolves it separates into K+ and Br− ions, and moving ions carry current, so KBr is a strong electrolyte.

  • A dissolves because its O–H groups hydrogen bond with water, but the molecules stay whole. With no ions, glucose is a nonelectrolyte.
  • B is also a molecular compound that dissolves by hydrogen bonding without forming ions. The O–H in ethanol does not break off as an ion in water.
  • D is a molecular compound (a waste product in urine). It dissolves well by hydrogen bonding, but as whole molecules, so it does not conduct.

Problem 11.4

(a) Describe how water molecules arrange themselves around a K+ ion and around a Br− ion in a KBr solution, and name the attraction involved. (b) Explain why KBr does not dissolve in hexane, C6H14.

Hint

(a) Which end of a water molecule is partly negative and which is partly positive? Each ion attracts the opposite end. (b) Does hexane have any partial charges?

Answer

(a) Around K+, water molecules turn their partially negative (δ−) oxygen ends toward the ion. Around Br−, they turn their partially positive (δ+) hydrogen ends toward the ion. These are ion–dipole attractions; each ion ends up surrounded by water molecules (hydrated).

(b) Hexane is nonpolar and has only dispersion forces. It has no partial charges to attract the ions, so it cannot make up for the strong attractions between K+ and Br− in the crystal. The ions stay together as a solid. Ionic solutes need a polar solvent.

Problem 11.5

The solubility of KCl is 34.2 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C. At 20 °C, a student stirs 17.1 g of KCl into 50.0 g of water, and all of it dissolves with nothing left over. How should the solution be classified?

A. Unsaturated, because 17.1 g is less than 34.2 g
B. Saturated
C. Supersaturated
D. It cannot be classified without the volume of the solution

Hint

Solubility is per 100 g of water, but only 50.0 g of water was used. Scale the amount dissolved to 100 g of water before comparing.

Answer

B. The solubility is given per 100 g of water, but the student used only 50.0 g, half as much. Scale the amount dissolved to 100 g of water:

\( \dfrac{17.1\ \text{g KCl}}{50.0\ \text{g water}} \times 100\ \text{g water} = 34.2\ \text{g KCl per 100 g water} \)

That equals the solubility, so the solution holds the maximum: it is saturated.

  • A compares 17.1 g with 34.2 g without matching the amounts of water. Half as much water holds only half as much KCl, 17.1 g.
  • C would need more than 34.2 g per 100 g of water, held in solution by careful cooling.
  • D is wrong because solubility tables are written per mass of water; the mass of water and the temperature are all you need.

Problem 11.6

A painter cleans brushes used with oil-based paint in mineral spirits, a mixture of nonpolar hydrocarbons, but rinses brushes used with water-based latex paint under the tap. Use "like dissolves like" to explain both choices. Why is water useless for the oil-based brushes?

Hint

Like dissolves like. Is oil-based paint polar or nonpolar? Are mineral spirits? How strongly do water molecules hold on to each other?

Answer

Oil-based paint is made of nonpolar oils and resins held together by dispersion forces. Mineral spirits are also nonpolar, so the paint and solvent attract each other about as strongly as they attract themselves, and the paint dissolves: like dissolves like. In water-based latex paint, the liquid carrying the paint is water, so water rinses it out before it dries.

Water molecules hydrogen bond strongly to one another. Nonpolar paint cannot form attractions with water that strong, so water simply squeezes the oil out and beads off the brush, the same reason salad oil floats on vinegar.

Problem 11.8

Problem 11.9

Chemists purify solids by recrystallization. A student dissolves 60.0 g of KNO3 in 50.0 g of water at 80 °C, then cools the solution to 20 °C and filters off the crystals. Use the solubility table above. (a) Does all of the KNO3 dissolve at 80 °C? (b) What mass of KNO3 crystallizes at 20 °C? (c) What percent of the KNO3 is recovered as crystals? (d) Why can the recovery never reach 100%?

Hint

Scale every solubility to 50.0 g of water (half the table value). (b) Amount dissolved minus what stays dissolved at 20 °C. (c) Crystals ÷ starting mass × 100%.

Answer

(a) In 50.0 g of water at 80 °C, the most that dissolves is 50.0 g × (169 g / 100 g) = 84.5 g. Since 60.0 g is less than 84.5 g, yes, all of it dissolves (the solution is unsaturated).

(b) At 20 °C, 50.0 g of water holds only 50.0 g × (31.6 g / 100 g) = 15.8 g. The rest crystallizes: 60.0 g − 15.8 g = 44.2 g.

(c) \( \dfrac{44.2\ \text{g}}{60.0\ \text{g}} \times 100\% = 73.7\% \)

(d) The cold solution is still saturated: 15.8 g of KNO3 stays dissolved in the 50.0 g of water and passes through the filter. Recovery is 73.7% at best. (Impurities present in small amounts stay dissolved too, which is the point of recrystallization.)

Problem 11.10

On hot, still afternoons in late summer, fish sometimes die in shallow farm ponds in the Valley. Which statement best explains why, chemically?

A. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, because the solubility of a gas decreases as temperature rises.
B. Warm water holds more dissolved oxygen, and too much oxygen poisons the fish.
C. Oxygen is nonpolar, so no oxygen at all can dissolve in water once it warms up.
D. Heat raises the air pressure above the pond, which forces oxygen out of the water.

Hint

How does the solubility of a gas change as water warms (Chapter 11)? Check each choice for the right trend and a correct reason.

Answer

A. Gases are always less soluble in warmer water: faster-moving gas molecules escape from the liquid more easily. A warm, shallow pond with no wind to mix in fresh air can drop below the dissolved oxygen fish need, especially at night and when algae die and decompose.

  • B applies the rule for most solids (more soluble when hot) to a gas. Gases behave the opposite way.
  • C goes too far. Oxygen is nonpolar, so it is only slightly soluble, but a few milligrams per liter do dissolve, and fish depend on that small amount at every temperature.
  • D has the pressure effect backward and the cause wrong: warming does not raise the air pressure over a pond, and a higher O2 pressure would dissolve more oxygen (Henry's law).

Problem 11.11

At 25 °C, water in contact with pure oxygen at 1.00 atm dissolves 40.3 mg of O2 per liter. At sea level, the partial pressure of O2 in air is 0.210 atm. What is the solubility of O2 in water in contact with air at 25 °C?

A. 8.46 mg/L
B. 192 mg/L
C. 40.3 mg/L
D. 31.8 mg/L

Hint

Henry's law: S2 = S1 × P2/P1. Use only the O2 partial pressure; a lower pressure means less dissolved gas.

Answer

A. Henry's law at constant temperature:

\( S_2 = S_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = 40.3\ \tfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{0.210\ \text{atm}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 8.46\ \tfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \)

A lower O2 pressure means less dissolved O2. This value is close to the dissolved oxygen measured in clean, well-mixed water at 25 °C.

  • B divides by the pressure ratio instead of multiplying. Lowering the pressure cannot raise the solubility.
  • C assumes pressure does not matter. For gases it matters a great deal.
  • D uses 0.790 atm, the fraction of air that is not oxygen (mostly N2). Only the O2 partial pressure controls how much O2 dissolves.

Problem 11.12

At the surface, where the partial pressure of N2 is 0.781 atm, a diver's blood holds about 12.5 mg of dissolved N2 per liter (a round value for practice). (a) She descends to a depth where the air she breathes from her tank is at a total pressure of 3.20 atm and is 78.1% N2. What is the partial pressure of N2? (b) Once her blood comes to equilibrium at that depth, how much N2 is dissolved per liter? (c) Use Henry's law to explain why rising to the surface too quickly can cause decompression sickness.

Hint

(a) Partial pressure = mole fraction × total pressure. (b) Henry's law, S1/P1 = S2/P2. (c) What happens to the solubility as the N2 pressure drops?

Answer

(a) PN2 = 0.781 × 3.20 atm = 2.50 atm (2.4992 atm unrounded).

(b) \( S_2 = S_1 \times \dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = 12.5\ \tfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{2.4992\ \text{atm}}{0.781\ \text{atm}} = 40.0\ \tfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \)

(b) 40.0 mg/L, 3.20 times the surface value, because the N2 pressure is 3.20 times higher.

(c) As she rises, the N2 pressure drops, so the blood can hold less N2. Rising slowly lets the extra gas leave through the lungs a little at a time. Rising fast is like opening a shaken soda: the gas comes out of solution as bubbles in the blood and tissues. (Context only; dive limits come from training agencies and dive computers.)

Problem 11.13

At 0 °C, CO2 dissolves in water to 3.35 g/L when the CO2 pressure above the water is 1.00 atm. (a) A soda is bottled ice cold under a CO2 pressure of 2.40 atm. How much CO2 does each liter hold? (b) After the bottle is opened, the soda is in contact with air, where the CO2 partial pressure is only about 4.0 × 10−4 atm. How much CO2 could a liter hold once it reaches equilibrium with the air at 0 °C? (c) An open soda takes hours to go completely flat, and a warm one goes flat faster. Explain both.

Hint

(a), (b) Henry's law: S2 = S1 × P2/P1. (c) Henry's law says how much stays dissolved, not how fast it leaves; how does warmth change both?

Answer

(a) \( S_2 = 3.35\ \tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{2.40\ \text{atm}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 8.04\ \tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{L}} \)

(b) \( S_2 = 3.35\ \tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{4.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{atm}}{1.00\ \text{atm}} = 1.3 \times 10^{-3}\ \tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{L}} \)

(a) 8.04 g/L; (b) 1.3 × 10−3 g/L (two significant figures, set by 4.0 × 10−4 atm), almost none.

(c) Henry's law tells how much CO2 the soda holds at equilibrium, not how fast it gets there. The extra CO2 escapes only at the surface and as bubbles, which takes time. In a warm soda, CO2 is less soluble to begin with and its faster molecules escape more quickly, so it goes flat sooner.

11.3 Concentration: Percent, Parts per Million, and Molarity

Problem 11.14

A sports-drink mix is made by dissolving 12.4 g of sugar in 187.6 g of water. What is the mass percent of sugar?

A. 6.20%
B. 6.61%
C. 12.4%
D. 0.0620%

Hint

The denominator is the whole solution: sugar plus water. Then multiply the fraction by 100%.

Answer

A. The mass of solution is 12.4 g + 187.6 g = 200.0 g.

\( \%\,(\text{m/m}) = \dfrac{12.4\ \text{g sugar}}{200.0\ \text{g solution}} \times 100\% = 6.20\% \)

  • B divides by the mass of water (the solvent), 187.6 g, instead of the mass of the whole solution.
  • C treats the grams of sugar as the percent, which is true only when the solution has a mass of exactly 100 g.
  • D stops at the fraction, 0.0620, and then writes a percent sign without multiplying by 100%.

Problem 11.15

How many grams of NaCl are in a 1.00 L bag of 0.45% (m/v) NaCl, often called half-normal saline?

A. 0.0045 g
B. 4.5 g
C. 450 g
D. 0.45 g

Hint

0.45% (m/v) means 0.45 g per 100 mL. Change the liters to mL, then use the percent as a conversion factor.

Answer

B. 0.45% (m/v) means 0.45 g of NaCl per 100 mL of solution. Convert the volume to mL first: 1.00 L = 1000. mL.

\( 1000.\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{0.45\ \text{g NaCl}}{100\ \text{mL}} = 4.5\ \text{g NaCl} \)

Two significant figures, set by 0.45%. A check: normal saline (0.9%) has 9 g per liter, and half-normal has half as much.

  • A plugs in 1.00 (liters) where the percent needs milliliters.
  • C forgets to divide by 100 mL; that would be a bag that is nearly half salt.
  • D reads 0.45% as "0.45 g per bag" (or per liter). The percent is per 100 mL.

Problem 11.16

An IV order calls for D5W, 5% dextrose (glucose) in water; take it as 5.00% (m/v). (a) What volume of D5W contains 30.0 g of glucose? (b) How many moles of glucose (C6H12O6, 180.156 g/mol) is that? (c) What is the molarity of D5W?

Hint

(a) Start with the grams of glucose and flip the percent (100 mL on top). (b) Divide by the molar mass. (c) Moles ÷ liters of solution.

Answer

(a) \( 30.0\ \text{g glucose} \times \dfrac{100\ \text{mL}}{5.00\ \text{g glucose}} = 600.\ \text{mL} \)

(b) \( 30.0\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{180.156\ \text{g}} = 0.167\ \text{mol} \)

(c) \( M = \dfrac{0.16652\ \text{mol}}{0.600\ \text{L}} = 0.278\ M \)

(a) 600. mL (the decimal point shows three significant figures); (b) 0.167 mol; (c) 0.278 M. A check for (c): 5.00 g per 100 mL is 50.0 g per liter, and 50.0 g ÷ 180.156 g/mol = 0.278 mol in each liter. (Context only; IV fluids are given exactly as ordered.)

Problem 11.17

Read each label as a conversion factor. (a) A chlorhexidine gluconate oral rinse is 0.12% (m/v). How many milligrams of chlorhexidine gluconate are in a 15.0 mL dose? What volume of isopropyl alcohol does it contain?

Write each label as a fraction with full units, start with the volume given, and let units cancel: (a) g per 100 mL, then g to mg; (b) mg per mL; (c) mL per 100 mL.

(a) \( 15.0\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{0.12\ \text{g}}{100\ \text{mL}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} = 18\ \text{mg} \)

Problem 11.18

Many community water systems add fluoride to a level of 0.70 mg/L to help prevent tooth decay. (a) Express this in ppm. (b) How many milligrams of fluoride does a person drinking 2.0 L of this water take in? (c) An adult toothpaste contains 1.45 × 103 ppm fluoride by mass (mg of fluoride per kg of paste). How many milligrams of fluoride are in a 1.00 g ribbon of paste? (d) A fluoride varnish contains 2.26 × 104 ppm fluoride. How many times more concentrated is it than the toothpaste?

Hint

(a) In water, 1 mg/L ≈ 1 ppm. (b) Volume × concentration. (c) ppm by mass is mg per kg; change grams to kg. (d) Divide the two ppm values.

Answer

(a) In dilute water solutions 1 mg/L ≈ 1 ppm, so 0.70 mg/L = 0.70 ppm.

(b) 2.0 L × 0.70 mg/L = 1.4 mg of fluoride.

(c) \( 1.00\ \text{g paste} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1.45 \times 10^{3}\ \text{mg F}}{1\ \text{kg paste}} = 1.45\ \text{mg F} \)

(d) 2.26 × 104 ppm ÷ 1.45 × 103 ppm = 15.6 times as concentrated.

(a) 0.70 ppm; (b) 1.4 mg; (c) 1.45 mg, about the same as a day's drinking water, which is why children are taught to spit, not swallow; (d) 15.6, which is why varnish is applied by a hygienist in a thin, measured layer. (Context only; follow the dentist's orders and product directions.)

Problem 11.19

A private well in the Valley tests at 18 ppb arsenic. What is this concentration in mg/L?

A. 0.018 mg/L
B. 18 mg/L
C. 1.8 × 10−5 mg/L
D. 1.8 × 104 mg/L

Hint

In water, 1 ppb ≈ 1 μg/L. Then convert μg to mg (1 mg = 1000 μg): a larger unit means a smaller number.

Answer

A. In water, 18 ppb ≈ 18 μg/L, and 1 mg = 1000 μg:

\( 18\ \dfrac{\mu\text{g}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mg}}{1000\ \mu\text{g}} = 0.018\ \dfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \)

The well is above the 10 ppb drinking water limit for arsenic.

  • B treats ppb as mg/L. In water, mg/L is ppm; ppb is a thousand times smaller.

Problem 11.20

A lab report for a small Valley water system lists nitrate at 38.5 mg/L (as nitrate) and 1,2,3-trichloropropane (1,2,3-TCP) at 0.000012 mg/L. The limits are 45 mg/L for nitrate (as nitrate) and 5 ppt for 1,2,3-TCP. (a) Express the nitrate result in ppm. Does it meet the limit? (b) What is the molarity of nitrate ion (NO3−, 62.004 g/mol)? (c) Express the 1,2,3-TCP result in ppt. How many times the limit is it? (d) How many nanograms of 1,2,3-TCP are in 2.0 L of this water?

(a) 1 mg/L ≈ 1 ppm; compare with the limit. (b) mg to g to moles, per liter. (c) mg/L to ng/L (1 mg = 106 ng), and 1 ng/L ≈ 1 ppt. (d) Liters × ng/L.

Answer

(a) 38.5 mg/L ≈ 38.5 ppm. It is below 45 mg/L, so it meets the nitrate limit.

(b) \( 38.5\ \dfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{mg}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{62.004\ \text{g}} = 6.21 \times 10^{-4}\ M \)

(c) \( 0.000012\ \dfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{10^{6}\ \text{ng}}{1\ \text{mg}} = 12\ \dfrac{\text{ng}}{\text{L}} = 12\ \text{ppt} \)

(c) 12 ppt, which is 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 times the limit, so this water needs treatment for 1,2,3-TCP. (d) 2.0 L × 12 ng/L = 24 ng, a tiny mass, but the limit is set that low because of the long-term health risk.

Problem 11.22

The hydrogen peroxide sold as a first-aid antiseptic is 3.00% (m/m) H2O2 (34.014 g/mol), and the solution has a density of 1.01 g/mL. What is its molarity?

Hint

Assume 100. g of solution: the percent gives grams of H2O2 (change to moles), and the density gives the volume of solution (change to liters).

Answer

Assume 100. g of solution. It contains 3.00 g of H2O2:

\( 3.00\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{34.014\ \text{g}} = 0.08820\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}_2 \)

Use the density to find the volume of 100. g of solution:

\( 100.\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mL}}{1.01\ \text{g}} = 99.01\ \text{mL} = 0.09901\ \text{L} \qquad M = \dfrac{0.08820\ \text{mol}}{0.09901\ \text{L}} = 0.891\ M \)

Problem 11.48

Fresh cucumbers soaked in a strong salt brine for pickling become limp and shriveled. Which explanation is correct?

A. Water moves out of the cucumber cells into the brine by osmosis, toward the higher solute concentration.
B. Water moves from the brine into the cucumber cells by osmosis, toward the lower solute concentration.
C. The salt dissolves the cell walls, so the cucumber collapses.
D. No water moves, because cell membranes block water; the cucumber shrinks because the salt is heavy.

Hint

Answer

A. The brine is hypertonic to the cucumber's cells.   • A skips the mole ratio (1 : 1). Each Ca(OH)2 has two OH− and neutralizes two HCl.
  • C flips the mole ratio (1/2 instead of 2/1).
  • D divides grams by molarity, skipping the conversion of grams to moles; it is also far too large a volume for a fraction of a gram of base.

Problem 11.61

Which aqueous solution has the lowest freezing point?

A. 0.20 m glucose
B. 0.10 m Ca(C2H3O2)2
C. 0.12 m NaCl
D. 0.080 m FeCl3

Hint

Count particles: find i for each solute (acetate stays together) and multiply by its molality. The largest i × m freezes lowest.

Answer

D. Count particles, then multiply by molality: FeCl3 → Fe3+ + 3 Cl−, i = 4, and 4 × 0.080 = 0.32 m of particles, the most of the four.

  • A: glucose stays whole (i = 1), so 0.20 m of particles. Its molality is the largest, but its particle count is the smallest.
  • B: Ca(C2H3O2)2 gives Ca2+ + 2 C2H3O2−, i = 3: 0.30 m, close but less than 0.32 m.
  • C: NaCl gives 2 ions: 0.24 m.

Problem 11.62

The solubility of KNO3 is 110 g per 100 g of water at 60 °C and 31.6 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C. A student dissolves 55.0 g of KNO3 in 50.0 g of water at 60 °C. (a) Is the solution unsaturated, saturated, or supersaturated at 60 °C? (b) It cools to 20 °C without being disturbed, and no crystals form. Label it now. (c) A seed crystal is added at 20 °C. What mass of KNO3 crystallizes?

Hint

The solubilities are per 100 g of water, so scale them to 50.0 g of water first, then compare with 55.0 g at each temperature. (c) Dissolved minus the new limit.

Answer

The solubilities are per 100 g of water, and this solution has only 50.0 g of water, so halve them: 55.0 g at 60 °C and 15.8 g at 20 °C.

(a) 55.0 g dissolved, and 55.0 g is the limit: saturated (on the curve). (b) 55.0 g is still dissolved, but the limit is now 15.8 g: supersaturated (above the curve). (c) 55.0 − 15.8 = 39.2 g crystallizes, leaving a saturated solution.

Problem 11.63

A student titrates HCl with NaOH using phenolphthalein. Which statement is correct?

A. At the endpoint, the flask contains no ions.
B. The endpoint comes just after the equivalence point, when a slight excess of OH− turns the indicator pink.
C. The equivalence point is reached when equal volumes of acid and base have been mixed.
D. A starting buret reading of 0.50 mL means the buret holds only 0.50 mL of NaOH.

Hint

Think through H+ + OH− → H2O. Which ions are left in the flask? What makes phenolphthalein change color? What does a buret reading measure?

Answer

B. The reaction in the flask is H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l). At the equivalence point the moles of OH− added match the moles of H+; the next fraction of a drop leaves OH− in excess, and the indicator changes color. That color change is the endpoint.

  • A: Na+ and Cl− are still dissolved; only H+ and OH− combine to make water.
  • C: the moles match by the balanced equation; the volumes are equal only if the two molarities happen to be equal (for a 1 : 1 reaction).
  • D: a buret tells how much has been dispensed. 0.50 mL is how far the level sits below the zero mark.
