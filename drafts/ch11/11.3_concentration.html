Learning Objectives

  • Calculate mass percent, mass/volume percent, and parts per million or billion.
  • Use a percent as a conversion factor in either direction, including the mass or volume of solution that holds a given amount of solute.
  • Calculate molarity from the mass of solute and the volume of solution.
  • Convert a mass percent to molarity using the solution's density.

"0.9% sodium chloride," "5% dextrose," "10 parts per billion arsenic," "0.100 M HCl": these labels all answer the same question, how much solute is in how much solution, in different units. Each unit is chosen for a reason: percents are easy to read on a label, parts per million or billion fit tiny amounts of contaminants, and molarity counts particles for chemical reactions.

Percent Concentration

A percent concentration is the amount of solute in 100 units of solution (solute + solvent):

\( \%\,(\text{m/m}) = \dfrac{\text{g solute}}{\text{g solution}} \times 100\% \qquad \%\,(\text{m/v}) = \dfrac{\text{g solute}}{\text{mL solution}} \times 100\% \)

  • Mass percent, %(m/m), compares masses and is common in the lab.
  • Mass/volume percent, %(m/v), is grams of solute per 100 mL of solution and is used for IV fluids: 0.9% NaCl contains 0.9 g NaCl in every 100 mL; 5% dextrose (D5W) contains 5 g of glucose in every 100 mL.
  • Volume percent, %(v/v), is used for liquids mixed with liquids, such as 70% isopropyl alcohol.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Mass percent

Problem. A student dissolves 15.0 g of KCl in 135.0 g of water. What is the mass percent of KCl?

Solution. The mass of solution is 15.0 + 135.0 = 150.0 g.

\( \%\,(\text{m/m}) = \dfrac{15.0\;\text{g}}{150.0\;\text{g}} \times 100\% = 10.0\% \)

Check. The denominator is the whole solution, not just the water. Dividing by 135.0 g would give 11.1%, a common mistake.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Grams in an IV bag

Problem. How many grams of glucose are in a 500. mL bag of 5.00% (m/v) dextrose?

Solution. 5.00% (m/v) means 5.00 g per 100 mL, a conversion factor:

\( {500.\;\text{mL}} \times \dfrac{5.00\;\text{g glucose}}{100\;\text{mL}} = {25.0\;\text{g glucose}} \)

Check. Half a liter of a 5% solution holds 25 g; a full liter holds 50 g.

Using a Percent Backward

A percent works as a conversion factor in either direction. Example 2 went from solution to solute. Just as often you know how much solute you need and must find how much solution delivers it, the same question a nurse asks about a medication dose. Write the percent as a fraction with full units, then start with the term by itself: the given amount with a single unit. A percent is a fraction, so it could be written either way up; the given amount cannot be flipped, so it is always the safe place to start. Then put the percent in the setup upside down so the solute unit cancels.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): How much solution holds the solute you need?

Problem. (a) A solution is 12.0% KCl by mass. How many grams of the solution contain 4.50 g of KCl? (b) How many milliliters of 5.00% (m/v) dextrose contain 12.5 g of glucose?

Given and Desire. (a) Given 4.50 g KCl; desire ??? g solution; the percent means 12.0 g KCl = 100 g solution. (b) Given 12.5 g glucose; desire ??? mL solution; the percent means 5.00 g glucose = 100 mL solution.

Solution. Start with the term by itself and flip the percent so the solute cancels:

\( \text{(a)}\;{4.50\;\text{g KCl}} \times \dfrac{100\;\text{g solution}}{12.0\;\text{g KCl}} = {37.5\;\text{g solution}} \qquad \text{(b)}\;{12.5\;\text{g glucose}} \times \dfrac{100\;\text{mL}}{5.00\;\text{g glucose}} = {250.\;\text{mL}} \)

Check. (a) The KCl is about one-eighth of the solution, and 8 × 4.5 is about 36, so 37.5 g is right. (b) Each 100 mL holds 5 g, and 12.5 g is two and a half of those 5 g portions, so 250 mL. If you multiplied by the percent instead of dividing, you would get an answer smaller than the solute itself, which is impossible. (Context only; doses are prepared exactly as ordered.)

Parts per Million and Parts per Billion

For very dilute solutions, such as pollutants in drinking water or trace elements in blood, percents become awkward. Parts per million (ppm) and parts per billion (ppb) work the same way as percent, with 106 or 109 in place of 100. In dilute water solutions, 1 L has a mass of about 1 kg, which gives handy equivalences:

\( {1\;\text{ppm}} \approx {1\;\text{mg/L}} \qquad {1\;\text{ppb}} \approx 1\;\mu\text{g/L} \qquad {1\;\text{ppt}} \approx {1\;\text{ng/L}} \)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Arsenic in well water

Problem. A rural well contains 0.0124 mg of arsenic per liter. Express this in ppb. Does it meet the drinking water limit of 10 ppb?

Solution.

\( 0.0124\;\dfrac{\text{mg}}{\text{L}} \times \dfrac{1000\;\mu\text{g}}{1\;\text{mg}} = 12.4\;\mu\text{g/L} = {12.4\;\text{ppb}} \)

No: 12.4 ppb is above the 10 ppb limit.

Check. Converting mg to μg multiplies by 1000, and 1 μg/L in water is 1 ppb.

Molarity

For chemical reactions we need to count particles, so the most important unit in chemistry is molarity (M): moles of solute per liter of solution.

\( M = \dfrac{\text{mol solute}}{\text{L solution}} \)

A 1.00 M solution contains 1.00 mol of solute in every liter of solution. "[NaCl]" in square brackets means "the molarity of NaCl." Note that the denominator is liters of solution, not liters of water: to make a solution of known molarity, dissolve the solute and then add water up to the mark on a volumetric flask.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Molarity of a saline stock

Problem. A student dissolves 5.25 g of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) in water and fills a 50.00 mL volumetric flask to the line. What is the molarity?

Solution.

\( \text{mol NaCl} = \dfrac{5.25\;\text{g}}{58.44\;\text{g/mol}} = {0.08984\;\text{mol}} \qquad M = \dfrac{0.08984\;\text{mol}}{0.05000\;\text{L}} = 1.80\;M \)

Check. mL was converted to L before dividing. About 0.09 mol in a twentieth of a liter is about 1.8 mol per liter.

Small molarities are often written in millimolar (mM, read "millimolar"), where 1 M = 1000 mM. A dilute saline sample with 0.898 g of NaCl in 525 mL of solution is 0.0293 M, which is the same as 29.3 mM: multiply by 1000. Clinical labs report many blood values in millimoles per liter (mmol/L), which is the same unit as mM, and you will use mM in dilutions in Section 11.4.

Converting Percent to Molarity

To convert a mass percent to molarity, assume 100 g of solution: the percent gives the grams of solute (convert to moles), and the density gives the volume of the solution (convert to liters).

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): A glucose solution

Problem. A 10.0% (m/m) glucose solution has a density of 1.04 g/mL. What is its molarity? (C6H12O6 180.156 g/mol)

Solution. In 100. g of solution there are 10.0 g of glucose = 0.05551 mol. The volume of 100. g of solution is 100. g ÷ 1.04 g/mL = 96.15 mL = 0.09615 L.

\( M = \dfrac{0.05551\;\text{mol}}{0.09615\;\text{L}} = 0.577\;M \)

Check. The density converts mass of solution to volume of solution; the answer has 3 significant figures, set by 10.0% and 1.04 g/mL.

Which Unit When?

Each concentration unit has its own home:

  • Percent is what you will read on IV bags, medications, and consumer products, so it is the unit health-science careers use most.
  • ppm and ppb are for trace amounts, such as contaminants in water.
  • Molarity is the most common unit in chemistry and in most labs, because it counts moles. When a problem asks only for "the concentration," it almost always means molarity unless it says otherwise.
  • Molality (Section 11.6) is used much less often, mainly for freezing points, boiling points, and osmolality.

All of them do double duty: each one is a concentration and a conversion factor that bridges the mole (or grams) to liters or kilograms.

Professor Tip: Calculator Habits

When the denominator is a sum, put it in parentheses. For Example 1, type 15.0 ÷ (15.0 + 135.0) × 100 = 10.0. Without the parentheses the calculator divides first and then adds, 15.0 ÷ 15.0 + 135.0 × 100, and you get 13,501, a number that makes no sense for a percent. For a percent-to-molarity conversion you can chain the whole Example 6 on a TI-36X Pro without writing anything down: 10.0 ÷ 100 × 1.04 × 1000 ÷ 180.156 = 0.577 M (percent as a fraction, times density in g/mL, times 1000 mL/L, divided by the molar mass).

Professor Tip

Every concentration is "amount of solute over amount of solution," so treat it as a conversion factor. Write the units out in full (g glucose / 100 mL solution, mol NaCl / L solution) and let them cancel. If a problem gives mL, convert to L before using molarity.

Valley Connection: Parts per Trillion in Valley Wells

Many San Joaquin Valley communities drink groundwater, and some wells carry contaminants measured in parts per billion or even parts per trillion. Arsenic occurs naturally in some Valley aquifers, and its drinking water limit is 10 ppb. 1,2,3-Trichloropropane (1,2,3-TCP), a contaminant left in groundwater from soil fumigants used on farms decades ago, is regulated far more strictly: in 2017 California set its maximum contaminant level at 5 parts per trillion, 0.000005 mg/L. Most of the affected wells are in the Central San Joaquin Valley, including Kern, Fresno, Tulare, and Merced counties, and many water systems have installed granular activated carbon filters to bring it below the limit. Five parts per trillion is about a quarter of a single drop of water spread through an Olympic-size swimming pool.

Sources: California State Water Resources Control Board, Division of Drinking Water, 1,2,3-Trichloropropane (waterboards.ca.gov); U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, arsenic drinking water standard (epa.gov).

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Dental products are labeled with concentrations you can now read. Chlorhexidine gluconate oral rinse, prescribed after periodontal therapy, is 0.12%: 0.12 g per 100 mL. Fluoride varnish is commonly 5% sodium fluoride, which corresponds to 22,600 ppm of fluoride ion, while most adult toothpastes contain about 1,000 to 1,500 ppm fluoride. Comparing the units shows why varnish is applied by the hygienist in a thin, measured layer rather than used at home. (Context only; follow the dentist's orders and product directions.)

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Iodinated contrast agents for CT and angiography are labeled by their iodine concentration in milligrams of iodine per milliliter (mg I/mL), such as 300 or 350 mg I/mL. A more concentrated agent makes vessels brighter on the image but is more viscous and delivers more iodine per mL, so technologists check the concentration, the volume, and the injection rate on every order and warm the contrast to body temperature to lower its viscosity (Section 9.7). (Context only; follow the protocol and orders.)

Common Mistakes

  • Dividing by the mass of solvent instead of the mass of solution in a percent.
  • Typing a sum in the denominator without parentheses, which gives a "crazy number."
  • Starting a backward percent problem with the percent and flipping it the wrong way: start with the given amount and let the units cancel.
  • Using mL in molarity: convert to liters.
  • Using liters of water instead of liters of solution.
  • Using grams instead of moles in molarity.

Summary

  • %(m/m) = g solute / g solution × 100%; %(m/v) = g solute / mL solution × 100%.
  • In dilute water solutions, 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L and 1 ppb ≈ 1 μg/L.
  • A percent is a conversion factor both ways: g solution = g solute × (100 g solution / g solute in the percent).
  • Molarity M = mol solute / L solution (1 M = 1000 mM). Density converts a mass percent into molarity.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What is the mass percent of sugar in a drink made from 26.0 g of sugar and 224.0 g of water?

Hint

The denominator is the whole solution: sugar plus water. Divide the grams of sugar by that total and multiply by 100%.

Answer

26.0 ÷ 250.0 × 100% = 10.4%.

Mass percent divides the solute by the total mass of solution (26.0 g + 224.0 g = 250.0 g), not by the water alone.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Nitrate in a Valley well is measured at 52 mg/L. The drinking water limit is 45 mg/L (as nitrate). Express the measurement in ppm and decide whether it meets the limit.

Hint

In dilute water solutions, 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg/L. Convert, then compare with the limit.

Answer

52 mg/L ≈ 52 ppm, above the 45 ppm limit.

A liter of dilute water solution has a mass of about 1 kg, so milligrams per liter equal milligrams per kilogram, which is parts per million; 52 ppm is more than the 45 ppm limit.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What is the molarity of a solution made by dissolving 2.50 g of KCl (74.55 g/mol) in enough water to make 125.0 mL of solution?

Hint

Our goal is the mole: grams of KCl to moles, then divide by liters of solution (change mL to L first).

Answer

2.50 ÷ 74.55 = 0.03353 mol; ÷ 0.1250 L = 0.268 M.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A sugar syrup is 15.0% sugar by mass. How many grams of syrup contain 30.0 g of sugar?

Hint

Start with the term by itself, the grams of sugar, and write the percent upside down (g syrup on top) so grams of sugar cancel.

Answer

30.0 g sugar × (100 g syrup / 15.0 g sugar) = 200. g syrup.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Express 0.00450 M glucose in millimolar.

Hint

1 M = 1000 mM. Moving to the smaller unit, should the number get bigger or smaller?

Answer

0.00450 M × 1000 mM/M = 4.50 mM.

Since 1 M = 1000 mM, multiplying by 1000 mM/M cancels M and moves the decimal point three places to the right.
