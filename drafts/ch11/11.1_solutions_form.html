Learning Objectives

  • Identify the solute and solvent in a solution.
  • Explain how intermolecular forces decide whether a substance dissolves ("like dissolves like").
  • Write dissolving equations that show an ionic solute separating into ions and a molecular solute staying whole.
  • Describe saturated, unsaturated, and supersaturated solutions and use a solubility to classify a solution.

Salt disappears in soup, sugar disappears in iced tea, but salad oil floats on vinegar no matter how hard you shake it. Whether two substances mix depends on the same intermolecular forces you met in Chapter 9.

Solutes and Solvents

A solution is a homogeneous mixture (Chapter 1). The component present in the larger amount is the solvent; the substance dissolved in it is the solute. A solution with water as the solvent is an aqueous solution, written (aq). Solutions are not only liquids: air is a gaseous solution, and brass and dental amalgam are solid solutions of metals.

How Solutions Form

For a solute to dissolve, its particles must separate from each other, the solvent particles must make room, and the solute and solvent must attract each other. A substance dissolves well when the new solute–solvent attractions are about as strong as the attractions they replace.

  • Ionic solutes in water: the δ− oxygen ends of water molecules surround cations, and the δ+ hydrogen ends surround anions. These ion–dipole attractions (Section 9.6) pull ions out of the crystal. Soluble ionic compounds (green-sheet solubility rules, Section 7.4) are strong electrolytes: their solutions conduct electricity because they contain ions.
  • Polar molecular solutes in water: molecules such as sugar, ethanol, and urea dissolve because they form hydrogen bonds or dipole–dipole attractions with water. They stay as whole molecules, so they are nonelectrolytes.
  • Nonpolar solutes, such as oil, fats, and gasoline, cannot form strong attractions with water. Water molecules stay hydrogen bonded to each other and squeeze the nonpolar molecules out, so the two separate into layers. Nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents.

The short rule is "like dissolves like": polar and ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents; nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents. Two liquids that mix in all proportions, such as water and ethanol, are miscible; liquids that form separate layers, such as water and oil, are immiscible.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Will it dissolve?

Problem. Predict whether each is soluble in water, and explain: (a) KBr; (b) methanol, CH3OH; (c) octane, C8H18; (d) iodine, I2. In which solvent would iodine dissolve better, water or hexane (C6H14)?

Solution. (a) Soluble: ionic, ion–dipole attractions (and soluble by the green-sheet rules). (b) Soluble (miscible): polar, with an O–H that hydrogen bonds with water. (c) Insoluble: nonpolar hydrocarbon. (d) Only slightly soluble: nonpolar. Iodine dissolves much better in nonpolar hexane.

Check. Find the strongest intermolecular force of the solute and of the solvent (Section 9.6). If they match in kind, the substances mix.

Writing Dissolving Equations

A dissolving equation (also called a solubility or dissociation equation) shows what a solute becomes once it is in water. Write the pure solute on the left and, on the right, whatever is actually spread through the water, each labeled (aq).

  • Ionic solutes come apart into their ions: they dissociate. Each ion ends up surrounded by water molecules, as in Figure 1. The subscript that tells how many of an ion are in the formula becomes a coefficient in front of that ion, and a polyatomic ion stays together as one unit.
  • Molecular solutes stay whole. Glucose, C6H12O6, dissolves because its many O–H groups hydrogen bond with water, but no bonds inside the molecule break, so the formula on the right is the same as on the left. It is still dissolved: you cannot see it, and the mixture is homogeneous.

NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq)
Na3PO4(s) → 3 Na+(aq) + PO43−(aq)
C6H12O6(s) → C6H12O6(aq)

The 3 in Na3PO4 moved from a subscript to a coefficient: one formula unit releases three separate sodium ions, each surrounded by water. The 4 in PO43− stays a subscript, because the four oxygens stay bonded to the phosphorus. Check each equation the way you check any equation: the atoms balance, and the charges on the right add up to zero. Counting the particles on the right side is the skill behind the concentration of each ion (Section 11.4) and behind freezing point depression (Section 11.6).

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Writing dissolving equations

Problem. Write the dissolving equation for each substance in water, and give the number of dissolved particles that one formula unit or molecule produces: (a) CaCl2; (b) (NH4)2SO4; (c) ethanol, C2H5OH, a liquid.

Solution.

(a) CaCl2(s) → Ca2+(aq) + 2 Cl−(aq)
(b) (NH4)2SO4(s) → 2 NH4+(aq) + SO42−(aq)
(c) C2H5OH(l) → C2H5OH(aq)

(a) 3 particles. (b) 3 particles: the 2 outside the parentheses becomes the coefficient of the ammonium ion, and ammonium and sulfate each stay together as polyatomic ions. (c) 1 particle: ethanol is molecular; its O–H group hydrogen bonds with water, but it does not form ions.

Check. Charges on the right add to zero: (a) +2 + 2(−1) = 0; (b) 2(+1) + (−2) = 0. Both salts are soluble by the green-sheet rules (Section 7.4).

Saturated, Unsaturated, and Supersaturated

There is a limit to how much solute a given amount of solvent can hold. The solubility of a substance is the maximum amount that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature, often reported in grams per 100 g of water. For example, about 36 g of NaCl dissolves in 100 g of water at room temperature.

Think of saturation as a Goldilocks problem, like sweetening a glass of iced tea. Not sweet enough yet? You can stir in more sugar, and it dissolves: the tea is unsaturated. Just right, and any more sugar would sit at the bottom: saturated. Too much, and yet somehow all of it is still dissolved: supersaturated. There is a limit because there are only so many water molecules available to surround the solute particles.

Three kinds of solution, compared with the solubility
SolutionAmount dissolvedAdd a little more solute and it…Iced-tea version
UnsaturatedLess than the maximumdissolvesNot sweet enough
SaturatedThe maximum (the solubility)stays undissolved on the bottomJust right
SupersaturatedMore than the maximumsets off crystallization: the solution is unstableToo much, yet still dissolved

A supersaturated solution is made by dissolving solute in hot solvent and cooling it carefully, without disturbing it (Section 11.2 shows this on a solubility curve). It is unstable: stirring or adding a seed crystal makes the extra solute crystallize ("crash out"). In a classic demonstration, a flask of clear supersaturated sodium acetate solution sits quietly until a single tiny crystal on the tip of a scoopula touches it. Crystals spread from that point like frost forming, and within seconds the whole flask turns solid. Nothing was added but one crystal: the extra solute was already in the flask. Reusable heat packs hold the same compound; clicking the metal disk starts the crystallization, which releases heat (Section 9.3).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Classifying a solution

Problem. The solubility of KCl is 34.2 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C. At 20 °C, is each solution unsaturated, saturated, or supersaturated? (a) 25.0 g KCl in 100. g water, all dissolved; (b) 34.2 g in 100. g water, all dissolved; (c) 38.0 g in 100. g water, all dissolved after cooling a hot solution.

Solution. (a) Unsaturated (25.0 < 34.2). (b) Saturated. (c) Supersaturated (38.0 > 34.2, held in solution only because it was cooled carefully).

Check. Compare the amount dissolved with the solubility for the same amount of water and temperature.

Professor Tip

When you are asked "will it dissolve?", you are really being asked "what are the intermolecular forces?" Draw on what you already know from Chapter 9: ionic → ion–dipole with water; O–H or N–H → hydrogen bonds with water; only C and H → dispersion only, so it stays out of water. Then apply "like dissolves like."

Biology Connection: Water-Soluble and Fat-Soluble Vitamins

"Like dissolves like" explains how your body handles vitamins. The B vitamins and vitamin C are polar, with many O–H and N–H groups, so they dissolve in blood plasma, and any excess is filtered by the kidneys into the urine. Vitamins A, D, E, and K are mostly nonpolar, so they dissolve in fats: they are absorbed along with dietary fat and stored in fatty tissue and the liver. That is why the fat-soluble vitamins can build up to harmful levels if taken in very large doses, while most excess water-soluble vitamins are excreted. The same rule explains why cell membranes, made of lipids with nonpolar tails, keep most ions and polar molecules from simply leaking through.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking that dissolving breaks covalent bonds: sugar molecules stay whole; only ionic compounds separate into ions.
  • Calling every molecule with H "able to hydrogen bond with water": it needs O–H, N–H, or lone pairs on N, O, or F.
  • Comparing amounts at different temperatures when deciding whether a solution is saturated.
  • Keeping a subscript on a separated ion: Na3PO4 gives 3 Na+, not "Na3+." The other mistake is splitting a polyatomic ion: SO42− stays together.

Summary

  • Solute dissolves in solvent; aqueous solutions have water as the solvent.
  • "Like dissolves like": polar/ionic in polar, nonpolar in nonpolar.
  • Dissolving equations: ionic solutes dissociate, and the subscript becomes a coefficient (Na3PO4 → 3 Na+ + PO43−); molecular solutes stay whole (C6H12O6(aq)).
  • Unsaturated < saturated (the solubility) < supersaturated (unstable; a seed crystal makes the extra crash out).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Identify the solute and the solvent: (a) 0.9% NaCl in water; (b) a 70% isopropyl alcohol rubbing solution (70% alcohol, 30% water).

Hint

The solvent is the component present in the larger amount; the solute is what is dissolved in it. In (b), which liquid makes up the larger share?

Answer

(a) Solute NaCl, solvent water. (b) Solvent isopropyl alcohol (the larger amount), solute water.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which is more soluble in water, ethanol (CH3CH2OH) or propane (CH3CH2CH3)? Explain.

Hint

What is the strongest attraction each can form with water? Look for an O–H group; a molecule made of only C and H has dispersion forces only.

Answer

Ethanol. Its O–H group hydrogen bonds with water; propane is nonpolar and has only dispersion forces.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A clear solution of sodium acetate suddenly fills with crystals when a single crystal is dropped in. What kind of solution was it?

Hint

A stable solution would dissolve the crystal or leave it sitting there. What does it mean that extra solute came out all at once?

Answer

Supersaturated. The seed crystal let the excess solute crystallize.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Write the dissolving equation for (a) K2CO3 and (b) sucrose, C12H22O11. How many dissolved particles does one formula unit or molecule give?

Hint

(a) K2CO3 is ionic: the subscript on K becomes a coefficient, and carbonate stays together. (b) Is sucrose ionic or molecular? Count the particles on the right.

Answer

(a) K2CO3(s) → 2 K+(aq) + CO32−(aq): 3 particles. (b) C12H22O11(s) → C12H22O11(aq): 1 particle, because sucrose is molecular and stays whole.
