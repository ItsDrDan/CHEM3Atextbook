Patient Case: Mateo and the Smoke Week

The week that sent Mateo Herrera (Chapters 2–5) to the emergency department began with a wildfire in the Sierra foothills. Smoke settled over the Valley for days. Burning wood and brush is a combustion reaction: carbon-based fuel reacting with oxygen. When there is plenty of oxygen, combustion produces mostly carbon dioxide and water. In a smoldering fire, there is not enough oxygen to finish the job, and the reaction also makes carbon monoxide (CO), soot, and tiny particles called PM2.5 that reach deep into the lungs. For someone with asthma, those particles inflame the airways, which is why Mateo's chest tightened every afternoon that week.

Carbon monoxide is dangerous for a different reason: it binds to hemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, and a standard pulse oximeter cannot tell the difference (Chapters 3 and 4). In this chapter you will write and balance equations like these, classify reactions by their patterns, predict what happens when solutions are mixed, and follow electrons as they move in oxidation–reduction reactions, the same kind of reaction as burning, rusting, and the cellular respiration that powers your body. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Recognize evidence of a chemical change and read a chemical equation, including phase labels (Section 7.1).
  • Balance chemical equations using conservation of mass (Section 7.2).
  • Classify reactions as combination, decomposition, single displacement, double displacement, or combustion, and predict products (Section 7.3).
  • Classify electrolytes and use solubility rules (Section 7.4).
  • Write molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations for precipitation reactions (Section 7.5).
  • Predict products of acid–base neutralization and gas-evolution reactions, and recognize when no reaction occurs (Section 7.6).
  • Assign oxidation numbers, identify what is oxidized and reduced, write simple half-reactions, and name oxidizing and reducing agents (Section 7.7).
  • Use a short activity series to predict whether a metal will displace another (Section 7.8).

Why This Chapter Matters

Reactions are what chemistry is about. Antacids neutralizing stomach acid, kidney stones forming in urine, bleach killing bacteria, iron rusting, and your cells turning glucose into energy are all reactions you will be able to write and classify by the end of this chapter. Chapter 8 then uses balanced equations to calculate how much of each substance reacts or forms.
