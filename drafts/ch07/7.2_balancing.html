Learning Objectives

  • Balance chemical equations by adjusting coefficients.
  • Balance polyatomic ions as units when they appear on both sides.
  • Check that a balanced equation uses the lowest whole-number coefficients.

A balanced equation is a recipe that obeys conservation of mass: the same atoms, in the same numbers, go in and come out. Balancing is a skill of careful counting, and a good method makes it routine.

The Method

  1. Write the skeleton equation with correct formulas and phase labels. Get the formulas right first (Chapter 4); they never change after this step.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on each side. Make a small table.
  3. Add coefficients, one substance at a time, to make the counts equal. Start with an element that appears in only one substance on each side; save elements that appear by themselves (O2, H2, metals) for last.
  4. Treat polyatomic ions as units when the same ion (such as SO42− or NO3−) appears unchanged on both sides.
  5. Check every element again, and make sure the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers (divide by any common factor).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Hydrogen and oxygen

Problem. Balance H2(g) + O2(g) → H2O(l).

Solution. O: 2 on the left, 1 on the right, so put 2 in front of H2O: H2 + O2 → 2 H2O. Now H: 2 left, 4 right, so put 2 in front of H2:

2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l)

Check. H 4 = 4; O 2 = 2. You could not "fix" it by writing H2O2, because that is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Polyatomic ions as units

Problem. Balance NaOH(aq) + Mg(NO3)2(aq) → NaNO3(aq) + Mg(OH)2(s).

Solution. Count units: OH, 1 left and 2 right; NO3, 2 left and 1 right. A 2 in front of NaOH fixes OH, and a 2 in front of NaNO3 fixes NO3 (and Na):

2 NaOH(aq) + Mg(NO3)2(aq) → 2 NaNO3(aq) + Mg(OH)2(s)

Check. Na 2 = 2; OH 2 = 2; Mg 1 = 1; NO3 2 = 2.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A combustion

Problem. Balance the combustion of ethane: C2H6(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g).

Solution. C: 2 CO2. H: 6 H means 3 H2O. O on the right: 2(2) + 3 = 7, which would need 7/2 O2. Fractions are not allowed in the final answer, so double everything:

2 C2H6(g) + 7 O2(g) → 4 CO2(g) + 6 H2O(g)

Check. C 4 = 4; H 12 = 12; O 14 = 8 + 6 = 14.

Professor Tip

For combustion, balance in the order C, then H, then O, because oxygen appears in both products and O2 stands alone. If oxygen ends up odd, double all the coefficients. And never touch a subscript: Br2 (one molecule) and 2 Br (two atoms) are different things.

Major Connection: Engineering

Engine and furnace designers use balanced combustion equations to set the air-to-fuel ratio. For gasoline (approximated as octane, C8H18), the balanced equation 2 C8H18 + 25 O2 → 16 CO2 + 18 H2O says each fuel molecule needs 12.5 oxygen molecules. Too little air leaves unburned fuel and produces carbon monoxide; too much wastes energy heating extra air. An oxygen sensor in the exhaust lets a car's computer keep the mixture near the balanced ratio.

Common Mistakes

  • Changing subscripts instead of coefficients.
  • Forgetting the coefficient multiplies the whole formula: 3 Ca(NO3)2 has 6 N and 18 O.
  • Leaving fractions or a common factor (4, 2, 4 should be 2, 1, 2).

Summary

  • Balance by changing coefficients only; count every element on both sides.
  • Balance polyatomic ions as units; balance lone elements last; for combustion, C then H then O.
  • Final coefficients are the smallest whole numbers.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Balance: Al(s) + O2(g) → Al2O3(s).

Answer

4 Al(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Al2O3(s). Al 4 = 4; O 6 = 6.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Balance: Zn(s) + HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g).

Answer

Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Balance the combustion of propane: C3H8(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g).

Answer

C3H8(g) + 5 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 4 H2O(g). O: 10 = 6 + 4.
