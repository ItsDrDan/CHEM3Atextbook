Learning Objectives

  • Balance chemical equations by adjusting coefficients.
  • Balance polyatomic ions as units when they appear on both sides.
  • Check that a balanced equation uses the lowest whole-number coefficients.
  • Use a counting table, the zig-zag method, "even the odds," and "lonely elements last" to balance harder equations.

A balanced equation is a recipe that obeys conservation of mass: the same atoms, in the same numbers, go in and come out. Balancing is a skill of careful counting, and a good method makes it routine.

The Method

  1. Write the skeleton equation with correct formulas and phase labels. Get the formulas right first (Chapter 4); they never change after this step.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on each side. Make a small table.
  3. Add coefficients, one substance at a time, to make the counts equal. Start with an element that appears in only one substance on each side; save elements that appear by themselves (O2, H2, metals) for last.
  4. Treat polyatomic ions as units when the same ion (such as SO42− or NO3−) appears unchanged on both sides.
  5. Check every element again, and make sure the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers (divide by any common factor).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Hydrogen and oxygen

Problem. Balance H2(g) + O2(g) → H2O(l).

Solution. O: 2 on the left, 1 on the right, so put 2 in front of H2O: H2 + O2 → 2 H2O. Now H: 2 left, 4 right, so put 2 in front of H2:

2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l)

Check. H 4 = 4; O 2 = 2. You could not "fix" it by writing H2O2, because that is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance.

Picture it. Draw each molecule as a pair of balls: two H–H pairs and one O–O pair on the left, two bent H–O–H molecules on the right. Four H balls and two O balls go in; four H balls and two O balls come out. The coefficient 2 means two whole water molecules, and you can read the equation as "2 mol H2 react with 1 mol O2 to make 2 mol H2O."

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Polyatomic ions as units

Problem. Balance NaOH(aq) + Mg(NO3)2(aq) → NaNO3(aq) + Mg(OH)2(s).

Solution. Count units: OH, 1 left and 2 right; NO3, 2 left and 1 right. A 2 in front of NaOH fixes OH, and a 2 in front of NaNO3 fixes NO3 (and Na):

2 NaOH(aq) + Mg(NO3)2(aq) → 2 NaNO3(aq) + Mg(OH)2(s)

Check. Na 2 = 2; OH 2 = 2; Mg 1 = 1; NO3 2 = 2.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A combustion

Problem. Balance the combustion of ethane: C2H6(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g).

Solution. C: 2 CO2. H: 6 H means 3 H2O. O on the right: 2(2) + 3 = 7, which would need 7/2 O2. Fractions are not allowed in the final answer, so double everything:

2 C2H6(g) + 7 O2(g) → 4 CO2(g) + 6 H2O(g)

Check. C 4 = 4; H 12 = 12; O 14 = 8 + 6 = 14.

Keep Count: A Counting Table

Draw an imaginary line down the arrow. Under each side, list every element (or every polyatomic unit) and write its count right under the formula it comes from. Each time you add a coefficient, cross out the old count and write the new one. This keeps your counting honest, and it is how the counts are shown in lecture.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Counting table for HI(aq) + Ba(OH)2(aq) → H2O(l) + BaI2(aq), before and after balancing
UnitLeft, unbalancedRight, unbalancedLeft, balancedRight, balanced
H (not counting the H in OH)1122
I1222
Ba1111
OH2122

The balanced equation is 2 HI(aq) + Ba(OH)2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + BaI2(aq). Notice the trick in the table: water was counted as HOH, one H plus one OH unit. Writing water as HOH while you balance lets hydroxide stay a unit on both sides. Write it as H2O again in the final answer.

The Zig-Zag Method for Polyatomic Ions

Equations full of polyatomic ions look the hardest, but they hide an easy route. Because the same polyatomic ions appear on both sides, you can balance by zig-zagging: start with one polyatomic ion, then jump across the arrow to its partner in the same formula, then back across to that partner's partner, and so on. Each coefficient you add fixes the next count, and the equation usually balances itself.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Zig-zag with phosphate and hydroxide

Problem. Balance Ba(OH)2(aq) + H3PO4(aq) → Ba3(PO4)2(s) + H2O(l).

Solution. Rewrite water as HOH so you can count H and OH separately. Units to count: Ba, OH, H, PO4.

  1. Start with phosphate. 2 PO4 on the right, 1 on the left: put 2 in front of H3PO4.
  2. Zig to its partner, H. 2 H3PO4 has 6 H; the right has 1 H (in HOH): put 6 in front of HOH.
  3. Zag to H's partner, OH. 6 HOH has 6 OH; the left has 2 OH in Ba(OH)2: put 3 in front of Ba(OH)2.
  4. Zig to OH's partner, Ba. 3 Ba on the left, 3 Ba in Ba3(PO4)2. Already balanced.

3 Ba(OH)2(aq) + 2 H3PO4(aq) → Ba3(PO4)2(s) + 6 H2O(l)

Check. Ba 3 = 3; PO4 2 = 2; H 6 + OH 6 on the left, and 6 HOH on the right; total O 6 + 8 = 14 = 8 + 6. The coefficients 3, 2, 1, 6 share no common factor.

Example 2 above is a zig-zag too: start with OH (2 NaOH), zig to its partner Na (2 NaNO3), and nitrate is then already balanced. One more shortcut: in a double displacement where every ion has a charge of +1 or −1 (for example AgNO3 + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO3), the swapped equation is already balanced. Always check anyway; this shortcut does not apply to redox equations (Section 7.7).

Even the Odds, and Leave Lonely Elements for Last

Two more habits handle most of the rest.

  • Even the odds. If an element has an even count on one side and an odd count on the other, use the least common multiple to make both sides the same even number. Two O on one side and three on the other? Make it six on each side.
  • Leave lonely elements for last. An element standing by itself (Fe, Al, O2, H2) can be changed without disturbing anything else. Save it as your "kicker," the last coefficient you write.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Rust, using both habits

Problem. Balance Fe(s) + O2(g) → Fe2O3(s).

Solution. Fe stands alone, so leave it for last. Oxygen is 2 on the left and 3 on the right. The least common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6, so even the odds: 3 O2 (6 O) and 2 Fe2O3 (6 O). Now 2 Fe2O3 holds 4 Fe, so the kicker is 4 Fe:

4 Fe(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Fe2O3(s)

Check. Fe 4 = 4; O 6 = 6. Writing the lonely Fe first would have forced you to change it again later.

Professor Tip

For combustion, balance in the order C, then H, then O, because oxygen appears in both products and O2 stands alone (it is your kicker). After C and H, add up the oxygen on the right and subtract any O already in the fuel (ethanol, C2H5OH, brings one). That is the O that must come from O2. If it is even, finish with O2. If it is odd, do not fight it with fractions: start over and put a 2 in front of the fuel right away, then balance C, H, and O again. Save yourself the pain. And never touch a subscript: Br2 (one molecule) and 2 Br (two atoms) are different things.

Major Connection: Engineering

Engine and furnace designers use balanced combustion equations to set the air-to-fuel ratio. For gasoline (approximated as octane, C8H18), the balanced equation 2 C8H18 + 25 O2 → 16 CO2 + 18 H2O says each fuel molecule needs 12.5 oxygen molecules. Too little air leaves unburned fuel and produces carbon monoxide; too much wastes energy heating extra air. An oxygen sensor in the exhaust lets a car's computer keep the mixture near the balanced ratio.

Common Mistakes

  • Changing subscripts instead of coefficients.
  • Forgetting the coefficient multiplies the whole formula: 3 Ca(NO3)2 has 6 N and 18 O.
  • Leaving fractions or a common factor (4, 2, 4 should be 2, 1, 2).
  • Doing math on a coefficient. 2 HCl means two HCl molecules; it is not H2Cl2. The coefficient counts molecules and never merges into the formula.
  • Splitting polyatomic ions you could keep whole. If sulfate, nitrate, phosphate or hydroxide appears unchanged on both sides, count it as one unit.
  • Balancing a lonely element first. You will only have to change it again; save it for last.

Summary

  • Balance by changing coefficients only; count every element on both sides.
  • Balance polyatomic ions as units; balance lone elements last; for combustion, C then H then O.
  • Final coefficients are the smallest whole numbers.
  • Keep a counting table under each side. Write water as HOH to keep OH as a unit, and zig-zag between polyatomic partners.
  • Even the odds with the least common multiple; for combustion, add up the oxygen on the right and subtract any O already in the fuel; if the O that must come from O2 is odd, double the fuel.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Balance: Al(s) + O2(g) → Al2O3(s).

Hint

Start with O: 2 in O2 and 3 in Al2O3. Find the smallest number both divide into, then balance Al last.

Answer

4 Al(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Al2O3(s). Al 4 = 4; O 6 = 6.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Balance: Zn(s) + HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g).

Hint

Count Cl and H on each side. Balance with a coefficient on HCl, never by changing a subscript.

Answer

Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g).

ZnCl2 and H2 each need 2 Cl and 2 H, so a coefficient of 2 on HCl balances both while Zn is already 1 = 1.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Balance the combustion of propane: C3H8(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g).

Hint

Balance C, then H, then O. Count the O on the right after C and H; if it is even, divide by 2 for O2.

Answer

C3H8(g) + 5 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 4 H2O(g). O: 10 = 6 + 4.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Use the zig-zag method: KOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → K2SO4(aq) + H2O(l).

Hint

Write water as HOH. Start with sulfate, zig to its partner H, zag to OH, then zig to K.

Answer

2 KOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → K2SO4(aq) + 2 H2O(l). Write water as HOH. Sulfate is 1 = 1. Zig to its partner H: 2 H on the left, so 2 HOH. Zag to OH: 2 OH on the right, so 2 KOH. Zig to K: 2 = 2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Balance the combustion of butane: C4H10(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g). Is the oxygen total odd or even after you balance C and H?

Hint

Balance C, then H, and count the O on the right. If the total is odd, double the fuel and rebalance.

Answer

With 1 C4H10: 4 CO2 and 5 H2O give 8 + 5 = 13 O, which is odd. Double the hydrocarbon: 2 C4H10, 8 CO2, 10 H2O, 16 + 10 = 26 O, so 13 O2. 2 C4H10(g) + 13 O2(g) → 8 CO2(g) + 10 H2O(g). Check: C 8 = 8; H 20 = 20; O 26 = 26.
