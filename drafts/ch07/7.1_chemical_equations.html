Learning Objectives

  • List the common signs that a chemical reaction has occurred.
  • Identify reactants, products, coefficients, and phase labels in a chemical equation.
  • State the law of conservation of mass and use it to check an equation.

In Chapter 1 you learned that a chemical change makes a new substance, while a physical change only changes form. A chemical reaction is the process of a chemical change: atoms rearrange, old bonds break, and new bonds form. Chemists describe reactions with a compact sentence written in formulas, the chemical equation.

Signs of a Chemical Reaction

You usually cannot see atoms rearrange, but you can see the results: a gas forms (bubbles that are not just boiling), a solid forms from clear solutions (a precipitate), a color changes, or energy is released or absorbed as heat, light, or cold. Each is a clue, not proof: you still need to ask whether a new substance has formed.

Reading a Chemical Equation

A chemical equation shows the reactants (starting substances) on the left, an arrow meaning "react to form," and the products on the right:

2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l)

  • Coefficients, the numbers in front of formulas, count molecules or formula units (or moles of them, Chapter 8). A coefficient of 1 is not written.
  • Subscripts are part of each formula and are never changed.
  • Phase labels give the physical state: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, and (aq) aqueous, dissolved in water.
  • A Δ (delta) over the arrow means the reactants are heated; a formula over the arrow names a catalyst.

Read the equation above as "two molecules of hydrogen gas react with one molecule of oxygen gas to form two molecules of liquid water."

Conservation of Mass

Atoms are not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction; they are only rearranged. This is the law of conservation of mass, first shown by careful weighing in the 1700s: the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. In an equation, it means every kind of atom must appear in the same number on both sides. An equation that meets that test is balanced (Section 7.2).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Reading and checking an equation

Problem. For 2 Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2 NaCl(s), identify the reactants, the products, and the phases, and check that atoms are conserved.

Solution. Reactants: solid sodium and chlorine gas. Product: solid sodium chloride. Atoms: left 2 Na and 2 Cl; right 2 Na and 2 Cl. Conserved.

Check. The coefficient 2 in front of NaCl multiplies both Na and Cl.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Mass in, mass out

Problem. A student heats 10.00 g of calcium carbonate until it completely decomposes into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide gas. The calcium oxide left in the crucible has a mass of 5.60 g. What mass of carbon dioxide escaped?

Solution. By conservation of mass, 10.00 g = 5.60 g + mass of CO2, so the CO2 has a mass of 4.40 g.

Check. The solid lost mass because a gas left, not because matter disappeared; in a sealed container, the total mass would not change.

Professor Tip

When a word problem describes a reaction, translate it into an equation before doing anything else. Names become formulas (Chapter 4), and remember the seven diatomic elements, H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2: "oxygen gas" is O2(g), never O(g). Add phase labels as you go.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating every bubble as a reaction. Boiling and fizzing from a dissolved gas leaving are physical changes.
  • Writing O instead of O2 for oxygen gas (and similarly for the other diatomic elements).
  • Thinking mass is lost when a gas escapes. It left the container; it was not destroyed.

Summary

  • Signs of reaction: gas, precipitate, color change, energy change.
  • Reactants → products; coefficients count units; subscripts are fixed; phase labels (s), (l), (g), (aq).
  • Mass and atoms are conserved, so equations must be balanced.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which are evidence of a chemical change: (a) ice melting; (b) a cut apple turning brown; (c) a copper roof turning green; (d) sugar dissolving in tea?

Hint

For each, ask whether a new substance forms. A change of state or dissolving keeps the same substance.

Answer

(b) and (c): new substances form (color changes). (a) and (d) are physical changes.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write an equation, with phase labels, for: "Solid magnesium burns in oxygen gas to form solid magnesium oxide." (You may leave it unbalanced.)

Hint

Turn each name into a formula with a phase label: oxygen gas is diatomic, and Mg2+ with O2− gives the oxide.

Answer

Mg(s) + O2(g) → MgO(s). Balanced: 2 Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2 MgO(s).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

In a sealed flask, 4.0 g of hydrogen reacts completely with 32.0 g of oxygen. What is the mass of water formed?

Hint

Use the law of conservation of mass: in a sealed flask, total mass of products equals total mass of reactants.

Answer

4.0 g + 32.0 g = 36.0 g of water (conservation of mass).

Mass is conserved in a sealed flask, so the water's mass equals the total mass of hydrogen and oxygen that reacted.
