Learning Objectives

  • Predict whether a precipitate forms when two ionic solutions are mixed.
  • Write molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations.
  • Identify spectator ions and recognize when there is no reaction.
  • Follow the full routine from names to net ionic equation, carrying coefficients into the ions.

Mix two clear solutions and a cloud of solid suddenly appears: a precipitation reaction, a double displacement in which one product is insoluble. Precipitation is how kidney stones form, how hard water leaves scale in kettles, and how labs test for particular ions.

Predicting a Precipitate

  1. Write the formulas of the reactants and identify their ions.
  2. Swap partners (cation of one with anion of the other) and write neutral product formulas from charges.
  3. Use the solubility rules (Section 7.4) to label each product (aq) or (s).
  4. Balance. If both products are aqueous, write no reaction.

Three Ways to Write the Equation

  • The molecular equation writes every substance as a neutral formula: AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq).
  • The complete ionic equation splits every aqueous strong electrolyte into its ions; solids, liquids, gases, and weak electrolytes stay together:
    Ag+(aq) + NO3−(aq) + Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s) + Na+(aq) + NO3−(aq)
  • Spectator ions appear unchanged on both sides (here Na+ and NO3−). Cancel them to get the net ionic equation, which shows only what actually changes: Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s).

Think of spectators at a sporting event: they are in the stadium the whole time, but they watch; they do not play. Spectator ions are in the beaker the whole time, but they do not take part. That gives a second way to say what the net ionic equation is: only the reactant ions needed to build the product that is not aqueous (the solid, liquid, or gas), and that product.

The Full Routine, Step by Step

A net ionic problem uses almost every skill from Chapters 4 and 7 in a row. Work it in order and do not skip steps.

  1. Names to formulas (Chapter 4), with the ion charges written above each ion.
  2. Predict the pattern. About 99% of reactions between two aqueous ionic compounds are double displacements, so use AB + CD → AD + CB (outside–inside, Section 7.3).
  3. Swap-drop the charges to write neutral product formulas. If the charges are +1 and −1, the formula needs no subscripts.
  4. Balance the molecular equation, starting with polyatomic ions and zig-zagging (Section 7.2).
  5. Label phases with the solubility table: (s) or (aq). This gives the molecular equation.
  6. Write the complete ionic equation. Split each (aq) compound into ions and carry its coefficient into every ion: 3 NaNO3(aq) becomes 3 Na+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq).
  7. Cancel the spectators. What is left is the net ionic equation. If everything cancels, there is no reaction.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): From names to net ionic

Problem. Solutions of cobalt(II) nitrate and sodium phosphate are mixed. Write the molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations.

Solution.

  1. Formulas: cobalt(II) is Co2+ and nitrate is NO3−, so Co(NO3)2; sodium is Na+ and phosphate is PO43−, so Na3PO4.
  2. Pattern: double displacement. Outside ions Co2+ and PO43− pair; inside ions NO3− and Na+ pair (cation first: NaNO3).
  3. Swap-drop: Co2+ and PO43− give Co3(PO4)2; Na+ and NO3− give NaNO3.
  4. Balance by zig-zag: 2 PO4 on the right, so 2 Na3PO4; zig to Na (6), so 6 NaNO3; zag to NO3 (6), so 3 Co(NO3)2; zig to Co: 3 = 3.
  5. Phases: Co(NO3)2 and Na3PO4 are soluble (nitrate; Group 1); Co3(PO4)2 is insoluble (phosphate, and Co2+ is not an exception); NaNO3 is soluble.

Molecular: 3 Co(NO3)2(aq) + 2 Na3PO4(aq) → Co3(PO4)2(s) + 6 NaNO3(aq)

Complete ionic (coefficients carried into the ions): 3 Co2+(aq) + 6 NO3−(aq) + 6 Na+(aq) + 2 PO43−(aq) → Co3(PO4)2(s) + 6 Na+(aq) + 6 NO3−(aq)

Net ionic (cancel 6 Na+ and 6 NO3−): 3 Co2+(aq) + 2 PO43−(aq) → Co3(PO4)2(s)

Check. Atoms: Co 3 = 3, P 2 = 2, O 8 = 8. Charge: 3(2+) + 2(3−) = 0 on the left, 0 on the right. The net ionic equation holds exactly the reactant ions needed to build the solid.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Lead(II) acetate and potassium sulfate

Problem. Write the molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations for mixing K2SO4(aq) and Pb(C2H3O2)2(aq).

Solution. Swap: K+ + C2H3O2− → KC2H3O2 (soluble: Group 1); Pb2+ + SO42− → PbSO4 (insoluble sulfate exception).

Molecular: K2SO4(aq) + Pb(C2H3O2)2(aq) → PbSO4(s) + 2 KC2H3O2(aq)

Complete ionic: 2 K+(aq) + SO42−(aq) + Pb2+(aq) + 2 C2H3O2−(aq) → PbSO4(s) + 2 K+(aq) + 2 C2H3O2−(aq)

Net ionic: Pb2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) → PbSO4(s)

Check. The net ionic equation is balanced in atoms and in charge (2+ and 2− on the left, 0 on the right).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): No reaction

Problem. What happens when NaNO3(aq) and NH4Cl(aq) are mixed?

Solution. The swapped products, NaCl and NH4NO3, are both soluble. Every ion is a spectator, so there is no reaction (NR); the ions simply mix.

Check. No precipitate, gas, or water forms, so nothing "leaves" the solution.

Professor Tip

Only split (aq) strong electrolytes in a complete ionic equation. Never split a solid, a liquid, a gas, water, or a weak acid. And when you cancel spectators, cancel only ions that are identical on both sides, in kind, charge, and phase. Finally, reduce the net ionic coefficients if they share a factor.

Professor Tip: When the Phases Are Given

On an exam, a reaction is sometimes written with the phases already shown, for example 2 AgNO3(aq) + K2SO4(aq) → Ag2SO4(s) + 2 KNO3(aq). Then you do not need the solubility table: the (s) tells you what precipitates. The net ionic equation must build that solid from its ions, so read the ions straight out of its formula and balance: 2 Ag+(aq) + SO42−(aq) → Ag2SO4(s). The same works for a liquid or gas product.

In the Lab: Experiment 10a, Types of Reactions

In this lab you mix pairs of solutions and observe what happens: a cloudy precipitate (for example, 2 NaCl(aq) + Pb(NO3)2(aq) → PbCl2(s) + 2 NaNO3(aq)), a temperature change from neutralization, bubbles from a gas-evolution reaction, the signs of a redox reaction, or no change at all. For each, you classify the reaction and write the balanced equation, connecting what you see in the test tube to the equations in Sections 7.3 to 7.7.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Barium sulfate contrast (Section 4.3) is a precipitate on purpose. The solubility rules list BaSO4 as insoluble (sulfate with Ba2+), so when a patient swallows a BaSO4 suspension, almost no Ba2+ ions dissolve to be absorbed; the solid simply coats the digestive tract and blocks X-rays. Manufacturers make it by precipitation from soluble barium compounds, and its insolubility is what makes it safe to use, while soluble barium salts are toxic.

Biology Connection: Physiology

About 75 to 80% of kidney stones are mostly calcium oxalate, CaC2O4, an insoluble salt (oxalate is in the "insoluble" row of the green sheet). When urine contains high concentrations of Ca2+ and C2O42− (from oxalic acid in foods such as spinach and nuts, and from metabolism), the net ionic reaction Ca2+(aq) + C2O42−(aq) → CaC2O4(s) can occur in the kidney. Drinking more water dilutes the ions and makes the precipitate less likely to form, which is why fluid intake is a common recommendation for people who form stones.

Common Mistakes

  • Splitting the precipitate into ions in the complete ionic equation.
  • Wrong ion counts: 2 NaCl(aq) gives 2 Na+ and 2 Cl−.
  • Keeping spectators in the net ionic equation, or cancelling ions that are not identical.
  • Inventing a reaction when both products are soluble: write NR.
  • Dropping coefficients when splitting: 3 Co(NO3)2(aq) gives 3 Co2+ and 6 NO3−, not 1 and 2.

Summary

  • A precipitation reaction is a double displacement that forms an insoluble solid.
  • Molecular → complete ionic (split aqueous strong electrolytes) → net ionic (cancel spectators).
  • If all products are aqueous, there is no reaction.
  • Full routine: names → formulas → predict (double displacement) → swap-drop → balance → phases → complete ionic (carry coefficients) → cancel spectators.
  • The net ionic equation contains only the reactant ions needed to make the product that is not aqueous. If the phases are given, build that product from its ions.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the net ionic equation for mixing BaCl2(aq) and Na2SO4(aq).

Hint

Swap partners and find the insoluble product with the green sheet. Write only the ions that build that solid.

Answer

Ba2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) → BaSO4(s). Spectators: Na+, Cl−.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Will a precipitate form when Sr(NO3)2(aq) and KF(aq) are mixed? If so, write the net ionic equation.

Hint

Swap partners: check the fluoride row of the green sheet for Sr2+. If a solid forms, write only the ions that build it.

Answer

Yes: SrF2 is insoluble (fluoride with Sr2+). Sr2+(aq) + 2 F−(aq) → SrF2(s).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Write the molecular equation for mixing FeCl3(aq) and NaOH(aq).

Hint

Swap partners and swap-drop the charges. Check the hydroxide row for Fe3+, then balance with chloride and hydroxide as units.

Answer

FeCl3(aq) + 3 NaOH(aq) → Fe(OH)3(s) + 3 NaCl(aq) (a rusty-brown precipitate).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Solutions of aluminum chloride and silver nitrate are mixed. Write the molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations, and name the spectator ions.

Hint

Names to formulas, swap partners, balance, label phases. Split the aqueous compounds into ions, cancel the spectators, and reduce the coefficients if you can.

Answer

Molecular: AlCl3(aq) + 3 AgNO3(aq) → 3 AgCl(s) + Al(NO3)3(aq). Complete ionic: Al3+(aq) + 3 Cl−(aq) + 3 Ag+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq) → 3 AgCl(s) + Al3+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq). Net ionic: 3 Ag+(aq) + 3 Cl−(aq) → 3 AgCl(s), which reduces to Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s). Spectators: Al3+ and NO3−.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Without using the solubility table, write the net ionic equation for Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2 KNO3(aq).

Hint

The product marked (s) is the only one not aqueous. Build it from its ions; everything else is a spectator.

Answer

The (s) marks PbI2 as the product that is not aqueous, so build it from its ions: Pb2+(aq) + 2 I−(aq) → PbI2(s). K+ and NO3− are spectators.
