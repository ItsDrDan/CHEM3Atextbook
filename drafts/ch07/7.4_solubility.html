Learning Objectives

  • Classify substances as strong electrolytes, weak electrolytes, or nonelectrolytes.
  • Write the ions formed when a soluble ionic compound dissolves.
  • Use the solubility rules on the green sheet to predict whether an ionic compound dissolves in water.
  • Recall the always-soluble ions (AANA) and the exception memory aids, and choose the right phase label for elements, water, and salts.

Sports-drink ads promise to "replace your electrolytes." Those electrolytes are dissolved salts, the same kind of ions that carry the electrical signals in your nerves and heart. Most reactions in your body, and many in the lab, happen in water. When an ionic compound dissolves, its ions separate and move freely, surrounded by water molecules: the slightly negative O ends of water molecules crowd around each cation and the slightly positive H ends crowd around each anion. Enough water molecules working together surround and conquer the crystal, pulling it apart ion by ion. Whether a compound dissolves, and whether its solution conducts electricity, decides what happens when two solutions are mixed (Sections 7.5 and 7.6).

Electrolytes

An electrolyte is a substance that forms ions in water, so its solution conducts electricity.

  • Strong electrolytes break apart completely into ions: soluble ionic compounds (NaCl, KNO3), strong acids (HCl, HNO3, H2SO4; the list is on the green sheet), and strong bases (NaOH, KOH).
  • Weak electrolytes form only a few ions; most of the substance stays as molecules: weak acids such as acetic acid (vinegar) and the weak base ammonia.
  • Nonelectrolytes dissolve as whole molecules and form no ions: sugar (glucose, sucrose), ethanol, urea.

When a soluble ionic compound dissolves, write its ions with (aq) labels and the correct counts: CaCl2(s) → Ca2+(aq) + 2 Cl−(aq); Na2SO4(s) → 2 Na+(aq) + SO42−(aq). Polyatomic ions stay together: they are like a family, always together. The subscript outside the parentheses becomes the count of ions: Ca(NO3)2(s) → Ca2+(aq) + 2 NO3−(aq). A particle drawing of calcium nitrate solution must therefore show whole NO3− ions, twice as many as Ca2+ ions.

Classifying an Electrolyte, Step by Step

Ask three questions about the formula, in this order.

  1. Is it ionic? A metal (or NH4+) with an anion, that is, a cation plus an anion. A soluble ionic compound is a strong electrolyte.
  2. Is it an acid or a base? An H in front of the formula (HF, HNO3) marks an acid; an OH in back (KOH, Ca(OH)2) marks a base. Check the green-sheet list of strong acids and strong bases. On the list: strong electrolyte. Not on the list: weak acid or weak base, so a weak electrolyte.
  3. Neither? A molecular compound made of nonmetals, usually carbon-based, with H and O that are not in the acid or base positions: a nonelectrolyte. It may dissolve, but it does not form ions.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Strong, weak, or nonelectrolyte?

Problem. Classify each: (a) K2SO4; (b) HNO2; (c) NH4Cl; (d) Ba(OH)2; (e) C2H6O (ethanol).

Solution.

  • (a) K+ and SO42−: ionic and soluble, so strong.
  • (b) H in front: an acid. Nitrous acid, HNO2, is not on the strong-acid list (nitric acid, HNO3, is), so it is a weak acid and a weak electrolyte.
  • (c) NH4+ and Cl−: ionic (ammonium is the cation), and soluble, so strong.
  • (d) OH in back: a base. Ba(OH)2 is on the strong-base list, so strong.
  • (e) Nonmetals only, carbon-based, no H in front and no OH in back as a hydroxide ion: a nonelectrolyte.

Check. Ethanol's formula has O and H, but in a molecule, not as an OH− ion attached to a metal. Only one compound here, HNO2, needed the green-sheet list to settle it.

Solubility Rules

Not every ionic compound dissolves. If the attraction between the ions is stronger than their attraction to water, the compound is insoluble and stays a solid. The table below follows the CHEM 3A green sheet, which you may use on exams.

Solubility rules for ionic compounds in water (CHEM 3A green sheet)

Group 1 (Li+, Na+, K+, ...), NH4+, NO3−, C2H3O2−, ClO4−, ClO3−solublenone (Li+ is only slightly soluble with CO32−, PO43−, F−)
Cl−, Br−, I−solublewith Ag+, Hg22+, Pb2+
F−solublewith Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+, Pb2+
SO42−, SO32−solublewith Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+, Ag+, Pb2+
CO32−, PO43−, CrO42−, C2O42−insolublesoluble with Group 1 and NH4+
O2−, S2−insolublesoluble with Group 1, NH4+, Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+
OH−insolublesoluble with Group 1 and NH4+; slightly soluble with Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+

Two habits make the table easy to use. First, check the cation: anything with a Group 1 ion or ammonium is soluble (the table's only footnote: a few Li+ compounds, with CO32−, PO43−, or F−, are only slightly soluble). Second, if neither ion appears in a "soluble" row, call the compound insoluble ("anything that's not on the list isn't soluble"). Iron(III) carbonate, for example, has neither ion in a soluble row, so it is insoluble.

Professor Tip: AANA and Three Memory Aids

  • AANA, always soluble: Alkali metals (Group 1), Ammonium, Nitrate, Acetate (perchlorate and chlorate too). If you see any one of these, the compound dissolves. No exceptions to check, apart from the table's note on a few slightly soluble Li+ compounds.
  • "What's the H.A.P. with the heavy halides?" Hg22+, Ag+, and Pb2+ make insoluble chlorides, bromides, and iodides.
  • "Sulfate stays home to watch CBS and PBS." Ca2+, Ba2+, Sr2+, and Pb2+ (plumbous), and Ag+ (the table lists it too), make insoluble sulfates.
  • "Hydroxide and sulfide take Ca.B.S. to the party." Hydroxides and sulfides are mostly insoluble, but Ca2+, Ba2+, and Sr2+ are soluble exceptions (their hydroxides only slightly).

The first two aids name insoluble exceptions to soluble rows; the "party" aid names soluble exceptions to insoluble rows.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Soluble or insoluble?

Problem. Predict whether each is soluble in water: (a) K3PO4; (b) AgBr; (c) Ca(NO3)2; (d) BaSO4; (e) Fe(OH)3; (f) (NH4)2S.

Solution. (a) soluble: K+ is Group 1. (b) insoluble: bromide with Ag+. (c) soluble: all nitrates. (d) insoluble: sulfate with Ba2+. (e) insoluble: hydroxide, and Fe3+ is not an exception. (f) soluble: ammonium.

Check. Look at both ions every time; one "always soluble" ion (Group 1, NH4+, nitrate, acetate) settles it. You can also check the cation's row and the anion's row and confirm they agree. For K2S: the K+ row says soluble, and the sulfide row says insoluble except with Group 1. Both rows agree: soluble.

Using the Table With Judgment

The solubility table is for salts, ionic compounds of a cation and an anion. Not every species in an equation is a salt, so label the others by what they are:

  • Diatomic elements such as H2, O2, and Cl2 are gases at room temperature: write (g), never (aq). (Br2 is a liquid and I2 a solid as pure substances; when they form in a water solution, write Br2(aq) and I2(aq).)
  • Water is (l), not (aq). "Aqueous" means dissolved in water; water dissolved in water makes no sense.
  • A metal written alone, such as Ag or Zn, is the neutral element, a solid: Ag(s). It is not the ion Ag+(aq). No charge means a charge of zero.
  • Strong acids and strong bases on the green-sheet list are strong electrolytes: in solution, HBr(aq) is really H+(aq) + Br−(aq).

Professor Tip

Keep the green sheet open and make the lookup a routine: (1) Is either ion Group 1, ammonium, nitrate, or acetate (AANA)? Soluble. (2) Otherwise, find the anion's row and check the exceptions for your cation. (3) Not listed anywhere? Assume insoluble. Do not try to memorize every compound; learn the lookup.

Biology Connection: Physiology

Your body fluids are electrolyte solutions. Plasma is about 0.9% dissolved salts, mostly Na+ and Cl−, with K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, HCO3−, and HPO42−. Because these ions carry charge, nerves and the heart can send electrical signals by moving them across membranes. Glucose dissolves in blood just as well, but as whole molecules: it is a nonelectrolyte. Solubility matters too: calcium phosphate is insoluble, which is exactly why it can form the hard mineral of bone and teeth (Section 6.1), while the sodium and potassium salts in your blood stay dissolved.

Common Mistakes

  • Breaking polyatomic ions apart when dissolving: Na2SO4 gives SO42−, not S and O ions.
  • Forgetting the counts: CaCl2 gives 2 Cl−.
  • Calling all ionic compounds soluble. Many carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides, and sulfides are not.
  • Calling sugar an electrolyte because it dissolves. Dissolving is not the same as forming ions.
  • Calling every acid strong. Only the acids on the green-sheet list are strong; HF and HNO2 are weak.
  • Using the table for everything. H2O is (l), O2 and Cl2 are (g), and a lone metal such as Ag is (s).

Summary

  • Strong electrolytes (soluble ionic compounds, strong acids and bases) fully ionize; weak electrolytes partly; nonelectrolytes not at all.
  • Always soluble (AANA): Alkali metals, Ammonium, Nitrate, Acetate. Use the green sheet for halides (H.A.P.), sulfates (CBS and PBS, and Ag+; the table lists it too), and the mostly insoluble anions (hydroxide and sulfide take Ca.B.S. to the party).
  • Classify electrolytes in order: ionic? acid (H in front) or base (OH in back) on the strong list? otherwise a molecular nonelectrolyte.
  • The table is for salts: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2 are (g), water is (l), lone metals are (s).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify as strong, weak, or nonelectrolyte: (a) KBr; (b) C12H22O11 (sucrose); (c) HC2H3O2; (d) HNO3.

Hint

Ask the three questions: is it ionic? Is it an acid or a base on the strong list? Neither means nonelectrolyte.

Answer

(a) strong (soluble ionic). (b) nonelectrolyte. (c) weak (weak acid). (d) strong (strong acid).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the ions formed when Al(NO3)3 dissolves.

Hint

Keep the nitrate ion whole; the subscript after the parentheses gives how many nitrate ions form.

Answer

Al(NO3)3(s) → Al3+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq).

Aluminum nitrate is a soluble ionic compound, so it separates into one Al3+ and three NO3− ions, which keep their own covalent bonds.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which are insoluble: PbCl2, Na2CO3, CaCO3, NH4Br, Mg(OH)2?

Hint

Check each against the green sheet: AANA compounds (including Group 1 and ammonium) are always soluble. Look up each anion's row and its exceptions.

Answer

PbCl2, CaCO3, and Mg(OH)2. Na2CO3 (Group 1) and NH4Br (ammonium) are soluble.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Four drawings claim to show the particles in a solution of magnesium nitrate, Mg(NO3)2(aq). Drawing A shows separate Mg2+ ions, N atoms and O atoms. Drawing B shows 3 Mg2+ ions and 6 NO3− ions. Drawing C shows 3 Mg2+ ions and 3 NO3− ions. Drawing D shows 3 intact Mg(NO3)2 units. Which is correct?

Hint

Is magnesium nitrate a soluble strong electrolyte? Then check two things: are polyatomic ions kept whole, and is the ion ratio the one in the formula?

Answer

B. Mg(NO3)2 is soluble (nitrate) and a strong electrolyte, so it separates into ions: Mg2+(aq) + 2 NO3−(aq), a 1 : 2 ratio, and 3 : 6 is 1 : 2. A breaks the polyatomic ion apart; C has the wrong ratio (1 : 1, which is not neutral); D shows no ions at all, as if it were a nonelectrolyte.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Classify as strong, weak, or nonelectrolyte: (a) LiOH; (b) HF; (c) (NH4)3PO4; (d) C3H8O3 (glycerol).

Hint

Ask the three questions: ionic? Acid (H in front) or base (OH in back) on the strong list? Neither means nonelectrolyte.

Answer

(a) strong: OH in back, and Group 1 hydroxides are strong bases. (b) weak: H in front, but HF is not on the strong-acid list. (c) strong: ionic (ammonium phosphate) and soluble (ammonium). (d) nonelectrolyte: a carbon-based molecule of nonmetals.
