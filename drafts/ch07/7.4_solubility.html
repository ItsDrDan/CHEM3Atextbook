Learning Objectives

  • Classify substances as strong electrolytes, weak electrolytes, or nonelectrolytes.
  • Write the ions formed when a soluble ionic compound dissolves.
  • Use the solubility rules on the green sheet to predict whether an ionic compound dissolves in water.

Most reactions in your body, and many in the lab, happen in water. When an ionic compound dissolves, its ions separate and move freely, surrounded by water molecules. Whether a compound dissolves, and whether its solution conducts electricity, decides what happens when two solutions are mixed (Sections 7.5 and 7.6).

Electrolytes

An electrolyte is a substance that forms ions in water, so its solution conducts electricity.

  • Strong electrolytes break apart completely into ions: soluble ionic compounds (NaCl, KNO3), strong acids (HCl, HNO3, H2SO4; the list is on the green sheet), and strong bases (NaOH, KOH).
  • Weak electrolytes form only a few ions; most of the substance stays as molecules: weak acids such as acetic acid (vinegar) and the weak base ammonia.
  • Nonelectrolytes dissolve as whole molecules and form no ions: sugar (glucose, sucrose), ethanol, urea.

When a soluble ionic compound dissolves, write its ions with (aq) labels and the correct counts: CaCl2(s) → Ca2+(aq) + 2 Cl−(aq); Na2SO4(s) → 2 Na+(aq) + SO42−(aq). Polyatomic ions stay together.

Solubility Rules

Not every ionic compound dissolves. If the attraction between the ions is stronger than their attraction to water, the compound is insoluble and stays a solid. The table below follows the CHEM 3A green sheet, which you may use on exams.

Group 1 (Li+, Na+, K+, ...), NH4+, NO3−, C2H3O2−, ClO4−, ClO3−solublenone (Li+ is only slightly soluble with CO32−, PO43−, F−)
Cl−, Br−, I−solublewith Ag+, Hg22+, Pb2+
F−solublewith Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+, Pb2+
SO42−, SO32−solublewith Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+, Ag+, Pb2+
CO32−, PO43−, CrO42−, C2O42−insolublesoluble with Group 1 and NH4+
O2−, S2−insolublesoluble with Group 1, NH4+, Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+
OH−insolublesoluble with Group 1 and NH4+; slightly soluble with Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+

Two habits make the table easy to use. First, check the cation: anything with a Group 1 ion or ammonium is soluble, full stop. Second, if neither ion appears in a "soluble" row, call the compound insoluble. Lecture memory aids: "What's the H.A.P. with the heavy halides?" (Hg22+, Ag+, Pb2+ make insoluble chlorides, bromides, and iodides), and "Sulfate stays home to watch CBS and PBS" (Ca, Ba, Sr, and Pb(II) sulfates are insoluble).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Soluble or insoluble?

Problem. Predict whether each is soluble in water: (a) K3PO4; (b) AgBr; (c) Ca(NO3)2; (d) BaSO4; (e) Fe(OH)3; (f) (NH4)2S.

Solution. (a) soluble: K+ is Group 1. (b) insoluble: bromide with Ag+. (c) soluble: all nitrates. (d) insoluble: sulfate with Ba2+. (e) insoluble: hydroxide, and Fe3+ is not an exception. (f) soluble: ammonium.

Check. Look at both ions every time; one "always soluble" ion (Group 1, NH4+, nitrate, acetate) settles it.

Professor Tip

Keep the green sheet open and make the lookup a routine: (1) Is either ion Group 1, ammonium, nitrate, or acetate? Soluble. (2) Otherwise, find the anion's row and check the exceptions for your cation. (3) Not listed anywhere? Assume insoluble. Do not try to memorize every compound; learn the lookup.

Biology Connection: Physiology

Your body fluids are electrolyte solutions. Plasma is about 0.9% dissolved salts, mostly Na+ and Cl−, with K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, HCO3−, and HPO42−. Because these ions carry charge, nerves and the heart can send electrical signals by moving them across membranes. Glucose dissolves in blood just as well, but as whole molecules: it is a nonelectrolyte. Solubility matters too: calcium phosphate is insoluble, which is exactly why it can form the hard mineral of bone and teeth (Section 6.1), while the sodium and potassium salts in your blood stay dissolved.

Common Mistakes

  • Breaking polyatomic ions apart when dissolving: Na2SO4 gives SO42−, not S and O ions.
  • Forgetting the counts: CaCl2 gives 2 Cl−.
  • Calling all ionic compounds soluble. Many carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides, and sulfides are not.
  • Calling sugar an electrolyte because it dissolves. Dissolving is not the same as forming ions.

Summary

  • Strong electrolytes (soluble ionic compounds, strong acids and bases) fully ionize; weak electrolytes partly; nonelectrolytes not at all.
  • Always soluble: Group 1, NH4+, NO3−, acetate. Use the green sheet for halides, sulfates, and the mostly insoluble anions.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify as strong, weak, or nonelectrolyte: (a) KBr; (b) C12H22O11 (sucrose); (c) HC2H3O2; (d) HNO3.

Answer

(a) strong (soluble ionic). (b) nonelectrolyte. (c) weak (weak acid). (d) strong (strong acid).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the ions formed when Al(NO3)3 dissolves.

Answer

Al(NO3)3(s) → Al3+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which are insoluble: PbCl2, Na2CO3, CaCO3, NH4Br, Mg(OH)2?

Answer

PbCl2, CaCO3, and Mg(OH)2. Na2CO3 (Group 1) and NH4Br (ammonium) are soluble.
