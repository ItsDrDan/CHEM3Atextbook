Learning Objectives

  • Recognize chemical change, read equations, and balance them.
  • Classify reactions by pattern and predict products.
  • Use solubility rules; write molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations.
  • Predict neutralization and gas-evolution products, and recognize no reaction.
  • Assign oxidation numbers; identify oxidation, reduction, and agents; write simple half-reactions; use the activity series.

How to Use This Guide

Read the Key Ideas after the sections, use the video moments for anything still unclear, work the examples with the answers covered, and then go to the Practice Problems. Keep the green sheet beside you for solubility and strong acids. Each Key Idea ends with the section where it is taught.

Key Ideas

1. Equations follow conservation of mass

Evidence of reaction: gas, precipitate, color change, energy change. Write correct formulas with phase labels, then balance with coefficients only; polyatomic ions as units; combustion C, then H, then O; lowest whole numbers. (Sections 7.1 and 7.2)

2. Five patterns

Combination A + B → AB; decomposition AB → A + B; single displacement A + BC → AC + B; double displacement AB + CD → AD + CB; combustion fuel + O2 → CO2 + H2O (incomplete: also CO and soot). (Section 7.3)

3. Ions in water

Soluble ionic compounds, strong acids, and strong bases are strong electrolytes. Always soluble: Group 1, NH4+, NO3−, acetate; check the green sheet for the rest. (Section 7.4)

4. Something must leave the solution

A double displacement occurs if it forms a precipitate, water, or a gas. Molecular → complete ionic (split only aqueous strong electrolytes) → net ionic (cancel spectators). H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O; H2SO3 → SO2 + H2O; NH4OH → NH3 + H2O. Otherwise: no reaction. (Sections 7.5 and 7.6)

5. Follow the electrons

LEO goes GER (OIL RIG). Oxidation numbers by the rules in order. Oxidation number up = oxidized (the reducing agent); down = reduced (the oxidizing agent). Balance electrons in half-reactions. A metal displaces ions of metals below it in the activity series. (Sections 7.7 and 7.8)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): From names to a net ionic equation

Problem. Aqueous solutions of copper(II) chloride and potassium hydroxide are mixed. Write the molecular and net ionic equations.

Solution. CuCl2(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s) + 2 KCl(aq) (hydroxide with Cu2+ is insoluble). Spectators K+ and Cl−. Net ionic: Cu2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s) (a pale blue solid).

Check. Charge on the left: +2 + 2(−1) = 0, equal to the right.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Which kind of reaction?

Problem. Predict the products and type for K2CO3(aq) + 2 HBr(aq).

Solution. Swap: 2 KBr(aq) + H2CO3; carbonic acid decomposes: K2CO3(aq) + 2 HBr(aq) → 2 KBr(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g), a gas-evolution (double displacement) reaction. Net ionic: CO32−(aq) + 2 H+(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(g).

Check. Not redox: every element keeps its oxidation number.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Redox bookkeeping

Problem. For Fe(s) + 2 AgNO3(aq) → Fe(NO3)2(aq) + 2 Ag(s), identify the oxidized element, the oxidizing agent, the half-reactions, and electrons transferred. Is it consistent with the activity series?

Solution. Fe 0 → +2: oxidized (Fe is the reducing agent). Ag +1 → 0: reduced (AgNO3, or Ag+, is the oxidizing agent). Fe → Fe2+ + 2 e−; 2 Ag+ + 2 e− → 2 Ag. 2 electrons transferred. Fe is above Ag in the series, so the reaction occurs.

Check. NO3− is a spectator: N stays +5.

Common Mistakes

  • Never change subscripts to balance; reduce coefficients to lowest whole numbers.
  • Predict product formulas from ion charges, not by copying subscripts.
  • Split only aqueous strong electrolytes; cancel only identical spectators.
  • Replace H2CO3, H2SO3, NH4OH with gas + water.
  • Oxidizing agent is reduced; reducing agent is oxidized. Free elements are 0.
