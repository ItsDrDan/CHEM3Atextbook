Learning Objectives

  • Recognize chemical change, read equations, and balance them.
  • Classify reactions by pattern and predict products with the three-step routine (letters, swap-drop, balance).
  • Use solubility rules; write molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations.
  • Predict neutralization and gas-evolution products, and recognize no reaction.
  • Assign oxidation numbers; identify oxidation, reduction, and agents; write simple half-reactions; tell which patterns are redox; use the activity series.

Key Ideas

1. Equations follow conservation of mass

Evidence of reaction: gas, precipitate, color change, energy change. Write correct formulas with phase labels, then balance with coefficients only; keep a counting table; polyatomic ions as units, zig-zagging between partners (write water as HOH); even the odds with the least common multiple; lonely elements last as the "kicker"; combustion C, then H, then O: add up the oxygen on the right and subtract any O already in the fuel; if the O that must come from O2 is odd, double the fuel; lowest whole numbers. (Sections 7.1 and 7.2)

2. Five patterns

Combination A + B → AB; decomposition AB → A + B; single displacement A + BC → AC + B; double displacement AB + CD → AD + CB; combustion fuel + O2 → CO2 + H2O (incomplete: also CO and soot). Predict in three steps: assign letters and match the pattern; write product formulas by swap-drop (ions) or bonding rules (nonmetals); balance. In single displacement, a metal replaces the cation (including H+ in an acid) and a halogen replaces the anion. In double displacement, the outside ions pair and the inside ions pair. (Section 7.3)

3. Ions in water

Soluble ionic compounds, strong acids, and strong bases are strong electrolytes. Classify by asking: ionic? acid (H in front) or base (OH in back) on the strong list? If neither, a molecular nonelectrolyte. Always soluble (AANA): Alkali metals, Ammonium, Nitrate, Acetate; check the green sheet for the rest (H.A.P. halides; sulfate watches CBS and PBS, and Ag+ (the table lists it too); hydroxide and sulfide take Ca.B.S. to the party). The table is for salts: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2 are (g) (Br2 and I2 formed in water are (aq)), water is (l), lone metals are (s). (Section 7.4)

4. Something must leave the solution

A double displacement occurs if it forms a precipitate, water, or a gas. Molecular → complete ionic (split only aqueous strong electrolytes) → net ionic (cancel spectators). H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O; H2SO3 → SO2 + H2O; NH4OH → NH3 + H2O; balance before you break them down. Otherwise: no reaction. Carry coefficients into the ions; the net ionic equation holds only the reactant ions needed to build the product that is not aqueous (spectators are fans in the stands). Gas evolution: H+ + HCO3− → H2O + CO2. (Sections 7.5 and 7.6)

5. Follow the electrons

LEO goes GER (OIL RIG). Oxidation numbers by the rules in order. Oxidation number up = oxidized (the reducing agent); down = reduced (the oxidizing agent). Oxidation numbers are bookkeeping charges, not real ion charges (NaH: H is −1; FeCl3: Fe is +3 by reverse swap-drop). Balance electrons in half-reactions for single displacement, combination, and decomposition, and check that charge balances, not just atoms. Double displacement is never redox; combination and decomposition sometimes; single displacement and combustion always. A metal displaces ions of metals below it in the activity series; the reverse does not happen on its own. (Sections 7.7 and 7.8)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): From names to a net ionic equation

Problem. Aqueous solutions of copper(II) chloride and potassium hydroxide are mixed. Write the molecular and net ionic equations.

Solution. CuCl2(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s) + 2 KCl(aq) (hydroxide with Cu2+ is insoluble). Spectators K+ and Cl−. Net ionic: Cu2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq) → Cu(OH)2(s) (a pale blue solid).

Check. Charge on the left: +2 + 2(−1) = 0, equal to the right.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Which kind of reaction?

Problem. Predict the products and type for K2CO3(aq) + 2 HBr(aq).

Solution. Swap: 2 KBr(aq) + H2CO3; carbonic acid decomposes: K2CO3(aq) + 2 HBr(aq) → 2 KBr(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g), a gas-evolution (double displacement) reaction. Net ionic: CO32−(aq) + 2 H+(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(g).

Check. Not redox: every element keeps its oxidation number.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Redox bookkeeping

Problem. For Fe(s) + 2 AgNO3(aq) → Fe(NO3)2(aq) + 2 Ag(s), identify the oxidized element, the oxidizing agent, the half-reactions, and electrons transferred. Is it consistent with the activity series?

Solution. Fe 0 → +2: oxidized (Fe is the reducing agent). Ag +1 → 0: reduced (AgNO3, or Ag+, is the oxidizing agent). Fe → Fe2+ + 2 e−; 2 Ag+ + 2 e− → 2 Ag. 2 electrons transferred. Fe is above Ag in the series, so the reaction occurs.

Check. NO3− is a spectator: N stays +5.

Common Mistakes

  • Never change subscripts to balance; reduce coefficients to lowest whole numbers.
  • Predict product formulas from ion charges, not by copying subscripts.
  • Split only aqueous strong electrolytes; cancel only identical spectators.
  • Replace H2CO3, H2SO3, NH4OH with gas + water.
  • Oxidizing agent is reduced; reducing agent is oxidized. Free elements are 0.
  • A halogen replaces the anion, not the cation; label charges before you swap-drop.
  • Carry coefficients into the ions; balance charge as well as atoms in redox equations.
