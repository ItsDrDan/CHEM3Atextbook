Learning Objectives

  • Define oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons.
  • Assign oxidation numbers using the rules in order.
  • Identify the species oxidized and reduced, the oxidizing and reducing agents, and the number of electrons transferred.
  • Write simple half-reactions for single-displacement, combination, and decomposition reactions, and check that both atoms and charge balance.
  • Predict whether a reaction pattern is never, sometimes, or always redox.
  • Recognize corrosion and combustion as redox reactions.

Rusting iron, tarnishing silver, a burning match, a charging phone, and every breath your cells use to "burn" glucose have one thing in common: electrons move from one substance to another. These are oxidation–reduction reactions, or redox for short. Photosynthesis, batteries, and the reactions that keep you alive are redox too, and redox reactions are often exothermic: they release energy as heat or light.

Losing and Gaining Electrons

  • Oxidation is the loss of electrons.
  • Reduction is the gain of electrons (the charge is "reduced," made more negative).

Why does gaining something count as reduction? Because electrons are negative. Picture the charge on a number line: each electron added moves the charge one step down, toward the negative end. Cl (0) gaining one electron becomes Cl− (−1); Fe3+ gaining one electron becomes Fe2+. The charge is literally reduced. Losing electrons moves the charge up: Zn (0) becomes Zn2+.

Two memory aids: LEO the lion goes GER (Lose Electrons Oxidation, Gain Electrons Reduction) and OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain). Oxidation and reduction always happen together, and the electrons lost by one substance equal the electrons gained by the other.

Oxidation Numbers

Not every redox reaction involves obvious ions (think of CH4 burning). Oxidation numbers (oxidation states) are bookkeeping charges that let you track electrons in any substance. Apply these rules in order; when two rules conflict, the earlier one wins:

  1. An atom in a pure element has oxidation number 0: Fe, Cu, O2, Cl2, S8.
  2. The oxidation numbers in a formula add up to its overall charge: 0 for a neutral compound, the ion's charge for an ion. (For a monatomic ion, the oxidation number is its charge.)
  3. Group 1 metals are +1; Group 2 metals are +2.
  4. Fluorine is −1.
  5. Hydrogen is +1 (with nonmetals).
  6. Oxygen is −2.
  7. Other halogens (Cl, Br, I) are −1.

If only one atom is left unassigned, find it by arithmetic using rule 2.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Assigning oxidation numbers

Problem. Find the oxidation number of the named element: (a) S in SO42−; (b) N in NH3; (c) Cr in Cr2O72−; (d) C in CO2.

Solution.

  • (a) S + 4(−2) = −2, so S = +6.
  • (b) N + 3(+1) = 0, so N = −3.
  • (c) 2 Cr + 7(−2) = −2, so 2 Cr = +12, Cr = +6.
  • (d) C + 2(−2) = 0, so C = +4.

Check. Each sum equals the species' charge.

Professor Tip: Oxidation Numbers Are Not Ion Charges

An ion charge is real and measurable: Na+ in salt water really carries a +1 charge. An oxidation number is an imaginary charge, assigned as if every bond were completely ionic, even in a molecule like CO2 where there are no ions at all. C in CO2 is +4 on paper only. For a monatomic ion the two numbers happen to match, which is why the rules feel familiar.

Two habits from lecture make the rules quick. Follow the priority order. In sodium hydride, NaH, the Group 1 rule (rule 3) comes before the hydrogen rule (rule 5), so Na is +1 and H must be −1 to make the sum 0. Reverse swap-drop for transition metals: in FeCl3, three Cl− give 3−, so Fe must be +3; in CuO, one O2− means Cu is +2.

What Was Oxidized? What Was Reduced?

Assign oxidation numbers to every element on both sides. An element whose oxidation number increases lost electrons and was oxidized; one whose oxidation number decreases gained electrons and was reduced. The reactant containing the element that is reduced is the oxidizing agent (it causes the oxidation); the reactant containing the element that is oxidized is the reducing agent.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Burning methane

Problem. In CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, identify the element oxidized, the element reduced, and the agents.

Solution. C goes from −4 (in CH4) to +4 (in CO2): oxidized. O goes from 0 (in O2) to −2: reduced. H stays +1. Reducing agent: CH4. Oxidizing agent: O2.

Check. Every combustion is a redox reaction, with O2 as the oxidizing agent.

Half-Reactions for Single Displacement

A redox reaction can be split into two half-reactions, one showing the electrons lost and one showing the electrons gained. For single-displacement reactions:

  1. Write the oxidation half-reaction for the metal that becomes an ion, with electrons on the right: Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2 e−.
  2. Write the reduction half-reaction for the ion that becomes a metal, with electrons on the left: Cu2+(aq) + 2 e− → Cu(s).
  3. If the electron counts differ, multiply each half-reaction so that electrons lost = electrons gained, then add and cancel the electrons.

In CHEM 3A you balance redox only for simple reactions like these; reactions that need H2O, H+, or OH− to balance are on the Going Further page (7.12).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Aluminum and copper(II)

Problem. Write the half-reactions and the balanced net equation for Al(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Al3+(aq) + Cu(s). How many electrons are transferred?

Solution. Oxidation: Al → Al3+ + 3 e−. Reduction: Cu2+ + 2 e− → Cu. The least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so multiply the oxidation by 2 and the reduction by 3:

2 Al → 2 Al3+ + 6 e−    3 Cu2+ + 6 e− → 3 Cu

2 Al(s) + 3 Cu2+(aq) → 2 Al3+(aq) + 3 Cu(s), with 6 electrons transferred.

Check. Atoms balance, and charge balances: 3(+2) = +6 on the left, 2(+3) = +6 on the right.

Check charge, not just atoms

A redox equation can have the same atoms on both sides and still be wrong. Look at Ag+(aq) + Cu(s) → Ag(s) + Cu2+(aq). One Ag and one Cu on each side, so the atoms balance, but the charge is +1 on the left and +2 on the right. Electrons cannot just vanish or appear: copper loses 2 electrons, and each Ag+ gains only 1, so two silver ions are needed. The balanced equation is 2 Ag+(aq) + Cu(s) → 2 Ag(s) + Cu2+(aq), with +2 on each side. Balancing the electrons with half-reactions takes care of this automatically.

Half-Reactions for Combination and Decomposition

Single displacement is not the only simple redox reaction. Whenever a free element combines into a compound, or a compound breaks down into free elements, electrons move, and you can write half-reactions the same way. Assign oxidation numbers on both sides, write one half-reaction for each element that changes, and keep diatomic elements whole (Cl2, O2), which means the electron count doubles.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Copper turning to copper(II) oxide

Problem. Heated in air, copper forms black copper(II) oxide: 2 Cu(s) + O2(g) → 2 CuO(s). Write the half-reactions, label each, and find the electrons transferred.

Solution. Oxidation numbers: Cu 0 and O 0 on the left (free elements); in CuO, O is −2, so by reverse swap-drop Cu is +2.

  • Oxidation (Cu loses electrons, 0 → +2): Cu → Cu2+ + 2 e−.
  • Reduction (O gains electrons, 0 → −2; O2 stays whole, so two O atoms gain 2 each): O2 + 4 e− → 2 O2−.

Multiply the copper half-reaction by 2 so that 4 electrons are lost and 4 gained. Adding gives 2 Cu + O2 → 2 CuO, with 4 electrons transferred. Cu is the reducing agent; O2 is the oxidizing agent.

Check. Each half-reaction balances in charge: 0 = +2 + 2(−1); 0 + 4(−1) = 2(−2). The same oxidation of copper by air, slowly and with water and CO2, makes the green patina on copper roofs and statues.

Decomposition into elements runs the other way. When potassium oxide is broken down, 2 K2O → 4 K + O2, potassium goes from +1 to 0 (reduced: K+ + e− → K, four times) and oxide goes from −2 to 0 (oxidized: 2 O2− → O2 + 4 e−), so 4 electrons are transferred.

Which Reactions Are Redox?

You can often tell from the reaction pattern (Section 7.3) alone:

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Reaction patterns that are never, sometimes, or always redox
Redox?PatternsWhy
NeverDouble displacement: precipitation, neutralization, gas evolutionIons only swap partners; every ion keeps its charge.
SometimesCombination, decompositionRedox when a free element is a reactant or product (2 Mg + O2 → 2 MgO; 2 HgO → 2 Hg + O2); not redox when only compounds are involved (CaCO3 → CaO + CO2).
AlwaysSingle displacement, combustionA free element becomes part of a compound (or the reverse), so oxidation numbers change.

Professor Tip

A quick way to spot a redox reaction: is there a free element (charge 0) on one side that is part of a compound on the other? If so, it is redox. Check CaCO3 → CaO + CO2: Ca stays +2, C stays +4, O stays −2. Nothing changes, so this decomposition is not redox.

Corrosion: redox you can see

  • Rust: iron is oxidized by oxygen in the presence of water, forming hydrated iron(III) oxide (simplified: 4 Fe + 3 O2 → 2 Fe2O3).
  • Silver tarnish: silver is oxidized by sulfur compounds in air, forming black silver sulfide, Ag2S.
  • Copper patina: copper roofs and statues oxidize to green copper compounds.
  • Rust that flakes versus oxide that protects: iron rust is crumbly and flakes off, exposing fresh iron, so rusting keeps going. Aluminum forms a thin, tight oxide layer and copper a tight patina, each sealing the metal underneath. One common way to protect iron is to coat it with zinc (galvanized steel, used for fencing and irrigation pipe): the zinc is oxidized instead of the iron (Section 7.8).
  • In the body: metal implants and dental fillings sit in a warm, salty, wet environment, so they are made from metals and alloys that corrode very slowly: titanium and certain stainless steels for implants, and (in older fillings) amalgam, an alloy of mercury with silver, tin, and copper.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth-whitening products use oxidizing agents. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), or carbamide peroxide, which releases hydrogen peroxide, oxidizes the large colored molecules trapped in enamel and dentin, breaking them into smaller, colorless pieces. In H2O2, oxygen has the unusual oxidation number −1; as the peroxide reacts, oxygen is reduced to −2 (in water), while the stain molecules are oxidized. The same oxidizing power is why peroxide can irritate gums and why office-strength products are applied with a protective barrier on the soft tissue.

Biology Connection: Cellular Respiration

Your cells "burn" glucose in a controlled series of redox reactions with the same overall equation as combustion: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O. Carbon is oxidized (from 0 on average in glucose to +4 in CO2) and oxygen is reduced (0 to −2). Instead of releasing the energy as a flame, cells pass the electrons along carriers such as NAD+ and the electron transport chain in the mitochondria, capturing the energy as ATP. White blood cells use redox chemistry as a weapon too: their "oxidative burst" makes reactive oxygen compounds that destroy engulfed bacteria.

Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up the agents. The oxidizing agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidized.
  • Putting electrons on the wrong side. Oxidation: electrons are products. Reduction: electrons are reactants.
  • Forgetting subscripts when assigning oxidation numbers: in Cr2O72−, 2 Cr share +12.
  • Giving O2 an oxidation number of −2. Free elements are 0.
  • Ignoring the rule order. In NaH, H is −1, because the Group 1 rule comes first.
  • Balancing atoms but not charge. Ag+ + Cu → Ag + Cu2+ has equal atoms but unequal charge; it needs 2 Ag+.
  • Calling every reaction redox. Precipitation, neutralization, and gas evolution never are.

Summary

  • Oxidation = loss of electrons (oxidation number increases); reduction = gain (oxidation number decreases).
  • Assign oxidation numbers with the rules in order: elements 0; sum = charge; Group 1 +1, Group 2 +2; F −1; H +1; O −2; other halogens −1.
  • Oxidizing agent is reduced; reducing agent is oxidized. Half-reactions balance electrons lost and gained.
  • Combustion, corrosion, and cellular respiration are redox reactions.
  • Oxidation numbers are bookkeeping charges, not real ion charges. Follow the rule order (NaH: H is −1); use reverse swap-drop for metals (FeCl3: Fe is +3).
  • Double displacement is never redox; combination and decomposition sometimes; single displacement and combustion always.
  • Half-reactions also work for combination and decomposition (2 Cu + O2 → 2 CuO, 4 e−). A balanced redox equation balances both atoms and charge.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the oxidation number of Mn in KMnO4 and of S in H2S.

Hint

Apply the rules in order: K is +1, H is +1, O is −2. Find the last atom by arithmetic so the sum equals zero.

Answer

KMnO4: +1 + Mn + 4(−2) = 0, Mn = +7. H2S: 2(+1) + S = 0, S = −2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

In 2 Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2 NaCl(s), what is oxidized, what is reduced, and how many electrons are transferred in the balanced equation?

Hint

Assign oxidation numbers before and after: an increase is oxidation, a decrease is reduction. Count electrons per atom × number of atoms.

Answer

Na (0 → +1) is oxidized; Cl (0 → −1) is reduced. 2 Na each lose 1 electron: 2 electrons transferred.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Write the half-reactions and net equation for Mg(s) + Ag+(aq) → Mg2+(aq) + Ag(s).

Hint

Oxidation: Mg loses electrons (on the right). Reduction: Ag+ gains electrons (on the left). Multiply so the electrons match, then add.

Answer

Mg → Mg2+ + 2 e−; Ag+ + e− → Ag (×2). Net: Mg(s) + 2 Ag+(aq) → Mg2+(aq) + 2 Ag(s).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Is each half-reaction an oxidation or a reduction? (a) Pb → Pb2+ + 2 e−; (b) Fe3+ + e− → Fe2+; (c) Br2 + 2 e− → 2 Br−.

Hint

Look at which side the electrons are on: lost electrons (on the right) mean oxidation; gained electrons (on the left) mean reduction.

Answer

(a) oxidation: electrons are lost (products), and the charge goes up, 0 to +2. (b) reduction: an electron is gained, and the charge goes down, +3 to +2. (c) reduction: electrons are gained, and each Br goes from 0 to −1.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

For 2 Ca(s) + O2(g) → 2 CaO(s), write both half-reactions and give the number of electrons transferred. Then give the oxidation number of H in CaH2.

Hint

Ca loses electrons; O2 gains them. Match the electrons, then use the rule order for CaH2: the Group 2 rule comes before the hydrogen rule.

Answer

Oxidation: Ca → Ca2+ + 2 e− (×2). Reduction: O2 + 4 e− → 2 O2−. 4 electrons transferred. In CaH2, the Group 2 rule makes Ca +2, so each H is −1 (+2 + 2(−1) = 0).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

Classify each as redox or not redox: (a) AgNO3(aq) + KBr(aq) → AgBr(s) + KNO3(aq); (b) 2 Ag2O(s) → 4 Ag(s) + O2(g); (c) MgO(s) + CO2(g) → MgCO3(s).

Hint

Assign oxidation numbers to every element on both sides and look for any change. If nothing changes, no electrons moved.

Answer

(a) Not redox: a precipitation (double displacement), and every ion keeps its charge. (b) Redox: a decomposition into elements; Ag goes +1 → 0 (reduced) and O goes −2 → 0 (oxidized), 4 electrons transferred. (c) Not redox: a combination of two compounds; Mg stays +2, C stays +4, O stays −2.
