Learning Objectives

  • Define oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons.
  • Assign oxidation numbers using the rules in order.
  • Identify the species oxidized and reduced, the oxidizing and reducing agents, and the number of electrons transferred.
  • Write simple half-reactions for single-displacement reactions.
  • Recognize corrosion and combustion as redox reactions.

Rusting iron, tarnishing silver, a burning match, a charging phone, and every breath your cells use to "burn" glucose have one thing in common: electrons move from one substance to another. These are oxidation–reduction reactions, or redox for short.

Losing and Gaining Electrons

  • Oxidation is the loss of electrons.
  • Reduction is the gain of electrons (the charge is "reduced," made more negative).

Two memory aids: LEO the lion goes GER (Lose Electrons Oxidation, Gain Electrons Reduction) and OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain). Oxidation and reduction always happen together, and the electrons lost by one substance equal the electrons gained by the other.

Oxidation Numbers

Not every redox reaction involves obvious ions (think of CH4 burning). Oxidation numbers (oxidation states) are bookkeeping charges that let you track electrons in any substance. Apply these rules in order; when two rules conflict, the earlier one wins:

  1. An atom in a pure element has oxidation number 0: Fe, Cu, O2, Cl2, S8.
  2. The oxidation numbers in a formula add up to its overall charge: 0 for a neutral compound, the ion's charge for an ion. (For a monatomic ion, the oxidation number is its charge.)
  3. Group 1 metals are +1; Group 2 metals are +2.
  4. Fluorine is −1.
  5. Hydrogen is +1 (with nonmetals).
  6. Oxygen is −2.
  7. Other halogens (Cl, Br, I) are −1.

If only one atom is left unassigned, find it by arithmetic using rule 2.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Assigning oxidation numbers

Problem. Find the oxidation number of the named element: (a) S in SO42−; (b) N in NH3; (c) Cr in Cr2O72−; (d) C in CO2.

Solution.

  • (a) S + 4(−2) = −2, so S = +6.
  • (b) N + 3(+1) = 0, so N = −3.
  • (c) 2 Cr + 7(−2) = −2, so 2 Cr = +12, Cr = +6.
  • (d) C + 2(−2) = 0, so C = +4.

Check. Each sum equals the species' charge.

What Was Oxidized? What Was Reduced?

Assign oxidation numbers to every element on both sides. An element whose oxidation number increases lost electrons and was oxidized; one whose oxidation number decreases gained electrons and was reduced. The reactant containing the element that is reduced is the oxidizing agent (it causes the oxidation); the reactant containing the element that is oxidized is the reducing agent.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Burning methane

Problem. In CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, identify the element oxidized, the element reduced, and the agents.

Solution. C goes from −4 (in CH4) to +4 (in CO2): oxidized. O goes from 0 (in O2) to −2: reduced. H stays +1. Reducing agent: CH4. Oxidizing agent: O2.

Check. Every combustion is a redox reaction, with O2 as the oxidizing agent.

Half-Reactions for Single Displacement

A redox reaction can be split into two half-reactions, one showing the electrons lost and one showing the electrons gained. For single-displacement reactions:

  1. Write the oxidation half-reaction for the metal that becomes an ion, with electrons on the right: Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2 e−.
  2. Write the reduction half-reaction for the ion that becomes a metal, with electrons on the left: Cu2+(aq) + 2 e− → Cu(s).
  3. If the electron counts differ, multiply each half-reaction so that electrons lost = electrons gained, then add and cancel the electrons.

In CHEM 3A you balance redox only for simple reactions like these; reactions that need H2O, H+, or OH− to balance are on the Going Further page (7.12).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Aluminum and copper(II)

Problem. Write the half-reactions and the balanced net equation for Al(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Al3+(aq) + Cu(s). How many electrons are transferred?

Solution. Oxidation: Al → Al3+ + 3 e−. Reduction: Cu2+ + 2 e− → Cu. The least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so multiply the oxidation by 2 and the reduction by 3:

2 Al → 2 Al3+ + 6 e−    3 Cu2+ + 6 e− → 3 Cu

2 Al(s) + 3 Cu2+(aq) → 2 Al3+(aq) + 3 Cu(s), with 6 electrons transferred.

Check. Atoms balance, and charge balances: 3(+2) = +6 on the left, 2(+3) = +6 on the right.

Professor Tip

A quick way to spot a redox reaction: is there a free element (charge 0) on one side that is part of a compound on the other? Single displacement, combination with an element, decomposition into an element, and combustion are all redox. Double displacement (precipitation, neutralization, gas evolution) never is: every ion keeps its charge.

Corrosion: redox you can see

  • Rust: iron is oxidized by oxygen in the presence of water, forming hydrated iron(III) oxide (simplified: 4 Fe + 3 O2 → 2 Fe2O3).
  • Silver tarnish: silver is oxidized by sulfur compounds in air, forming black silver sulfide, Ag2S.
  • Copper patina: copper roofs and statues oxidize to green copper compounds.
  • In the body: metal implants and dental fillings sit in a warm, salty, wet environment, so they are made from metals and alloys that corrode very slowly: titanium and certain stainless steels for implants, and (in older fillings) amalgam, an alloy of mercury with silver, tin, and copper.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth-whitening products use oxidizing agents. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), or carbamide peroxide, which releases hydrogen peroxide, oxidizes the large colored molecules trapped in enamel and dentin, breaking them into smaller, colorless pieces. In H2O2, oxygen has the unusual oxidation number −1; as the peroxide reacts, oxygen is reduced to −2 (in water), while the stain molecules are oxidized. The same oxidizing power is why peroxide can irritate gums and why office-strength products are applied with a protective barrier on the soft tissue.

Biology Connection: Cellular Respiration

Your cells "burn" glucose in a controlled series of redox reactions with the same overall equation as combustion: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O. Carbon is oxidized (from 0 on average in glucose to +4 in CO2) and oxygen is reduced (0 to −2). Instead of releasing the energy as a flame, cells pass the electrons along carriers such as NAD+ and the electron transport chain in the mitochondria, capturing the energy as ATP. White blood cells use redox chemistry as a weapon too: their "oxidative burst" makes reactive oxygen compounds that destroy engulfed bacteria.

Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up the agents. The oxidizing agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidized.
  • Putting electrons on the wrong side. Oxidation: electrons are products. Reduction: electrons are reactants.
  • Forgetting subscripts when assigning oxidation numbers: in Cr2O72−, 2 Cr share +12.
  • Giving O2 an oxidation number of −2. Free elements are 0.

Summary

  • Oxidation = loss of electrons (oxidation number increases); reduction = gain (oxidation number decreases).
  • Assign oxidation numbers with the rules in order: elements 0; sum = charge; Group 1 +1, Group 2 +2; F −1; H +1; O −2; other halogens −1.
  • Oxidizing agent is reduced; reducing agent is oxidized. Half-reactions balance electrons lost and gained.
  • Combustion, corrosion, and cellular respiration are redox reactions.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the oxidation number of Mn in KMnO4 and of S in H2S.

Answer

KMnO4: +1 + Mn + 4(−2) = 0, Mn = +7. H2S: 2(+1) + S = 0, S = −2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

In 2 Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2 NaCl(s), what is oxidized, what is reduced, and how many electrons are transferred in the balanced equation?

Answer

Na (0 → +1) is oxidized; Cl (0 → −1) is reduced. 2 Na each lose 1 electron: 2 electrons transferred.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Write the half-reactions and net equation for Mg(s) + Ag+(aq) → Mg2+(aq) + Ag(s).

Answer

Mg → Mg2+ + 2 e−; Ag+ + e− → Ag (×2). Net: Mg(s) + 2 Ag+(aq) → Mg2+(aq) + 2 Ag(s).
