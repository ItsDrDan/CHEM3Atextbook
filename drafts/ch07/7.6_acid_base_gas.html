Learning Objectives

  • Predict the products of an acid–base neutralization and write its net ionic equation.
  • Recognize the unstable products that decompose into gases.
  • Write equations for gas-evolution reactions and decide when no reaction occurs.
  • Write molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations for neutralization and gas-evolution reactions.

Two more kinds of double displacement complete the set. In one, an acid and a base cancel each other and make water. In the other, a product is unstable and falls apart into a gas, which bubbles out of the solution. Both are everyday chemistry: antacids, baking soda in cooking, and the fizz in a soda.

Acid–Base Neutralization

An acid releases H+ ions in water (Section 4.5), and a base such as a metal hydroxide releases OH− ions (Section 6.1). When they react, the H+ and OH− combine into water, and the remaining ions form a salt:

acid + base → water + salt    HA(aq) + BOH(aq) → H2O(l) + BA(aq)

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Neutralization equations

Problem. Write the molecular and net ionic equations for HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq), and the molecular equation for H2SO4(aq) + KOH(aq).

Solution. HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → H2O(l) + NaCl(aq). Na+ and Cl− are spectators, so the net ionic equation is H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l). For sulfuric acid, each molecule has two H+, so it needs two OH−: H2SO4(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + K2SO4(aq).

Check. Every strong acid + strong base neutralization has the same net ionic equation, H+ + OH− → H2O. If the salt is insoluble, as with H2SO4 + Ba(OH)2 → 2 H2O + BaSO4(s), it is a neutralization and a precipitation at once (Example 2).

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Sulfuric acid with two different bases

Problem. Write the molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations for H2SO4(aq) with (a) KOH(aq) and (b) Ba(OH)2(aq).

Solution. Both are strong bases (green sheet), so both split into ions; H2SO4 is a strong acid and is written as 2 H+ + SO42−.

(a) Molecular: H2SO4(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + K2SO4(aq).
Complete ionic: 2 H+(aq) + SO42−(aq) + 2 K+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + 2 K+(aq) + SO42−(aq).
Net ionic: 2 H+ + 2 OH− → 2 H2O, which reduces to H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l). Spectators: K+ and SO42−.

(b) Molecular: H2SO4(aq) + Ba(OH)2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + BaSO4(s).
Complete ionic: 2 H+(aq) + SO42−(aq) + Ba2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + BaSO4(s).
Net ionic: the same as the complete ionic equation. BaSO4 is insoluble (sulfate with Ba2+), so every ion ends up in a product that is not aqueous, and there are no spectators.

Check. Charges in (b): left 2(+1) + (−2) + (+2) + 2(−1) = 0; right 0. Changing only the base changed the net ionic equation, because the salt in (b) precipitates.

Gas-Evolution Reactions

Some double-displacement "products" never last. Whenever you would write one of these, replace it with what it actually becomes:

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Baking soda and vinegar

Problem. Write the balanced equation for sodium bicarbonate reacting with acetic acid.

Solution. Swap partners: NaHCO3(aq) + HC2H3O2(aq) → NaC2H3O2(aq) + H2CO3(aq). Replace the carbonic acid:

NaHCO3(aq) + HC2H3O2(aq) → NaC2H3O2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

Check. The CO2 is the fizz; atoms balance (Na 1, H 5, C 3, O 5 on each side).

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): An ammonium salt and a base

Problem. Predict the products of NH4Cl(aq) + NaOH(aq).

Solution. Swap: NaCl(aq) + NH4OH(aq). Replace NH4OH: NH4Cl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + NH3(g). The sharp smell of ammonia is the sign.

Check. N 1, H 5, Cl 1, Na 1, O 1 on each side.

Professor Tip: Balance Before You Break It Down

Balance the equation while the unstable product is still written in, then replace it. For HCl + Na2CO3, swap to NaCl + H2CO3, balance by zig-zag (carbonate 1 = 1; zig to Na, so 2 NaCl; zag to Cl, so 2 HCl), and only then rewrite H2CO3: 2 HCl(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). The equation stays balanced, because H2CO3 and H2O + CO2 contain the same atoms. To remember the gas, subtract water: H2CO3 − H2O = CO2; H2SO3 − H2O = SO2; NH4OH − H2O = NH3. In the lab, the bubbles in the test tube are that gas.

Net ionic equations for gas evolution

Gas-evolution reactions have net ionic equations too, written by the same routine as precipitation (Section 7.5). The gas and the water are not aqueous, so they stay whole; the net ionic equation shows the ions needed to build them.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Three gas-evolution net ionic equations

Problem. Write the molecular and net ionic equations when an acid is added to each solution: (a) HCl(aq) with potassium bicarbonate; (b) HNO3(aq) with potassium bisulfite; (c) HCl(aq) with sodium sulfide.

Solution.

  • (a) HCl(aq) + KHCO3(aq) → KCl(aq) + H2CO3, which becomes KCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Bicarbonate, HCO3−, is a polyatomic ion and stays together. Spectators: K+, Cl−. Net ionic: H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(g).
  • (b) HNO3(aq) + KHSO3(aq) → KNO3(aq) + H2O(l) + SO2(g). Spectators: K+, NO3−. Net ionic: H+(aq) + HSO3−(aq) → H2O(l) + SO2(g).
  • (c) 2 HCl(aq) + Na2S(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + H2S(g). H2S does not break down; it is already the gas. Spectators: Na+, Cl−. Net ionic: 2 H+(aq) + S2−(aq) → H2S(g).

Check. Charge balances in each net ionic equation: (a) +1 − 1 = 0 on the left, 0 on the right; (b) the same; (c) +2 − 2 = 0, and 0. Notice that here, the acid's anion is a spectator: any strong acid would give the same net ionic equation.

Does a reaction happen?

A double displacement occurs when something leaves the dissolved ions: a precipitate (solid), water (or another weak electrolyte), or a gas. If the products are only soluble ionic compounds, the answer is no reaction.

Professor Tip

Three products should never be your final answer: H2CO3, H2SO3, and NH4OH. Train your eye to spot them right after you swap partners, and rewrite them as gas + water. Then check the other product's solubility as usual.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many over-the-counter antacids are bases or carbonates that react with stomach acid (HCl). Magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia) neutralizes it: Mg(OH)2(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + 2 H2O(l). Calcium carbonate tablets do a gas-evolution reaction: CaCO3(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). The CO2 is why carbonate antacids can cause belching and bloating, a side effect nurses may hear about when reviewing a patient's over-the-counter medicines. (Context only; this is not treatment advice.)

Common Mistakes

  • Leaving H2CO3, H2SO3, or NH4OH as products.
  • Forgetting to match H+ and OH−: H2SO4 needs 2 OH−.
  • Splitting water into ions in an ionic equation. H2O(l) stays together, and so do the gases CO2, SO2, NH3, and H2S.
  • Breaking bicarbonate or bisulfite apart. HCO3− and HSO3− are polyatomic ions; in a net ionic equation they appear whole.
  • Assuming every neutralization has the net ionic equation H+ + OH− → H2O. If the salt is insoluble (BaSO4), it belongs in the net ionic equation too.

Summary

  • Acid + base → water + salt; strong acid + strong base net ionic: H+ + OH− → H2O.
  • H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O; H2SO3 → SO2 + H2O; NH4OH → NH3 + H2O; H2S is a gas.
  • A double displacement needs a precipitate, water, or a gas; otherwise, no reaction.
  • Balance with the unstable product written in, then replace it. Gas-evolution net ionic equations: H+ + HCO3− → H2O + CO2; H+ + HSO3− → H2O + SO2; 2 H+ + S2− → H2S.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the balanced equation for HNO3(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq).

Hint

Acid + base → water + salt. Match H+ to OH−: Ca(OH)2 has two OH.

Answer

2 HNO3(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + Ca(NO3)2(aq).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the balanced equation for Na2SO3(aq) + 2 HCl(aq), and name the gas.

Hint

Swap partners, then use the unstable-products table: what does H2SO3 become?

Answer

Na2SO3(aq) + 2 HCl(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + SO2(g); sulfur dioxide.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Does a reaction occur when NaCl(aq) and KNO3(aq) are mixed?

Hint

Swap partners and check: does a precipitate, water, or a gas form?

Answer

No. The swapped products, NaNO3 and KCl, are both soluble, and no water or gas forms.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Write the molecular and net ionic equations for HBr(aq) + K2CO3(aq).

Hint

Balance with H2CO3 written in, then replace it with what it becomes. Cancel the spectators for the net ionic equation.

Answer

Balance first with H2CO3 written in: 2 HBr + K2CO3 → 2 KBr + H2CO3. Then replace it: 2 HBr(aq) + K2CO3(aq) → 2 KBr(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Spectators K+ and Br− cancel: 2 H+(aq) + CO32−(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(g).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Write the net ionic equation for (NH4)2SO4(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → K2SO4(aq) + 2 H2O(l) + 2 NH3(g).

Hint

Write the complete ionic equation (water and the gas stay whole), cancel the spectators, and reduce the coefficients.

Answer

Complete ionic: 2 NH4+ + SO42− + 2 K+ + 2 OH− → 2 K+ + SO42− + 2 H2O(l) + 2 NH3(g). Cancel K+ and SO42− and reduce by 2: NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l) + NH3(g).
