Learning Objectives

  • Predict the products of an acid–base neutralization and write its net ionic equation.
  • Recognize the unstable products that decompose into gases.
  • Write equations for gas-evolution reactions and decide when no reaction occurs.

Two more kinds of double displacement complete the set. In one, an acid and a base cancel each other and make water. In the other, a product is unstable and falls apart into a gas, which bubbles out of the solution. Both are everyday chemistry: antacids, baking soda in cooking, and the fizz in a soda.

Acid–Base Neutralization

An acid releases H+ ions in water (Section 4.5), and a base such as a metal hydroxide releases OH− ions (Section 6.1). When they react, the H+ and OH− combine into water, and the remaining ions form a salt:

acid + base → water + salt    HA(aq) + BOH(aq) → H2O(l) + BA(aq)

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Neutralization equations

Problem. Write the molecular and net ionic equations for HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq), and the molecular equation for H2SO4(aq) + KOH(aq).

Solution. HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → H2O(l) + NaCl(aq). Na+ and Cl− are spectators, so the net ionic equation is H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l). For sulfuric acid, each molecule has two H+, so it needs two OH−: H2SO4(aq) + 2 KOH(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + K2SO4(aq).

Check. Every strong acid + strong base neutralization has the same net ionic equation, H+ + OH− → H2O. If the salt is insoluble, as with H2SO4 + Ba(OH)2 → 2 H2O + BaSO4(s), it is a neutralization and a precipitation at once.

Gas-Evolution Reactions

Some double-displacement "products" never last. Whenever you would write one of these, replace it with what it actually becomes:

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Baking soda and vinegar

Problem. Write the balanced equation for sodium bicarbonate reacting with acetic acid.

Solution. Swap partners: NaHCO3(aq) + HC2H3O2(aq) → NaC2H3O2(aq) + H2CO3(aq). Replace the carbonic acid:

NaHCO3(aq) + HC2H3O2(aq) → NaC2H3O2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

Check. The CO2 is the fizz; atoms balance (Na 1, H 5, C 3, O 5 on each side).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): An ammonium salt and a base

Problem. Predict the products of NH4Cl(aq) + NaOH(aq).

Solution. Swap: NaCl(aq) + NH4OH(aq). Replace NH4OH: NH4Cl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + NH3(g). The sharp smell of ammonia is the sign.

Check. N 1, H 5, Cl 1, Na 1, O 1 on each side.

Does a reaction happen?

A double displacement occurs when something leaves the dissolved ions: a precipitate (solid), water (or another weak electrolyte), or a gas. If the products are only soluble ionic compounds, the answer is no reaction.

Professor Tip

Three products should never be your final answer: H2CO3, H2SO3, and NH4OH. Train your eye to spot them right after you swap partners, and rewrite them as gas + water. Then check the other product's solubility as usual.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many over-the-counter antacids are bases or carbonates that react with stomach acid (HCl). Magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia) neutralizes it: Mg(OH)2(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + 2 H2O(l). Calcium carbonate tablets do a gas-evolution reaction: CaCO3(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). The CO2 is why carbonate antacids can cause belching and bloating, a side effect nurses may hear about when reviewing a patient's over-the-counter medicines. Common Mistakes

  • Leaving H2CO3, H2SO3, or NH4OH as products.
  • Forgetting to match H+ and OH−: H2SO4 needs 2 OH−.
  • Splitting water into ions in an ionic equation. H2O(l) stays together.

Summary

  • Acid + base → water + salt; strong acid + strong base net ionic: H+ + OH− → H2O.
  • H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O; H2SO3 → SO2 + H2O; NH4OH → NH3 + H2O; H2S is a gas.
  • A double displacement needs a precipitate, water, or a gas; otherwise, no reaction.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the balanced equation for HNO3(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq).

Answer

2 HNO3(aq) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + Ca(NO3)2(aq).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the balanced equation for Na2SO3(aq) + 2 HCl(aq), and name the gas.

Answer

Na2SO3(aq) + 2 HCl(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + SO2(g); sulfur dioxide.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Does a reaction occur when NaCl(aq) and KNO3(aq) are mixed?

Answer

No. The swapped products, NaNO3 and KCl, are both soluble, and no water or gas forms.
