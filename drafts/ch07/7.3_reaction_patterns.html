Learning Objectives

  • Classify reactions as combination, decomposition, single displacement, double displacement, or combustion.
  • Predict the products of each pattern and balance the equation.
  • Distinguish complete from incomplete combustion.

Millions of reactions are known, but a handful of patterns cover most of the ones in this course. Recognizing the pattern tells you what the products will probably be, the same way recognizing a sentence's grammar tells you where the verb goes.

Combination (Synthesis)

Two or more reactants form one product: A + B → AB. A metal and a nonmetal combine into an ionic compound whose formula you predict from the ion charges (Section 4.2): 2 Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2 NaCl(s); 2 Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2 MgO(s).

Decomposition

One reactant breaks into two or more simpler substances: AB → A + B, usually with heat, light, or electricity. Two patterns to know: heating a metal carbonate gives a metal oxide and CO2 (CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)), and hydrogen peroxide breaks down into water and oxygen: 2 H2O2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + O2(g).

Single Displacement

An element replaces one partner in a compound: A + BC → AC + B. A metal replaces a metal (or hydrogen) cation, keeping the new compound neutral: Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s). Many metals react with acids this way, releasing hydrogen gas: Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). Whether a single displacement actually happens depends on the activity series (Section 7.8).

Double Displacement

Two ionic compounds swap partners: AB + CD → AD + CB. Keep each cation first, give it the other anion, and rebuild neutral formulas: AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq). A double displacement happens only if something "leaves" the solution as a product that is not dissolved ions: a precipitate (Section 7.5), water (neutralization), or a gas (Section 7.6). If all the products are aqueous ions, there is no reaction.

Combustion

A carbon-based fuel reacts rapidly with oxygen, releasing heat and light. Complete combustion (enough O2) of a fuel containing C, H, and possibly O gives CO2 and H2O: CH4(g) + 2 O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2 H2O(g). Incomplete combustion (too little O2) also forms carbon monoxide and soot (carbon): 2 CH4(g) + 3 O2(g) → 2 CO(g) + 4 H2O(g). That is why gas heaters and generators must never run in closed spaces.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Classify and predict

Problem. Classify each reaction and predict the products, then balance: (a) K(s) + Br2(l); (b) the combustion of glucose, C6H12O6; (c) Pb(NO3)2(aq) + KI(aq); (d) heating MgCO3(s).

Solution.

  • (a) Combination: K+ and Br− make KBr. 2 K(s) + Br2(l) → 2 KBr(s).
  • (b) Combustion: C6H12O6(s) + 6 O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 6 H2O(l), the same overall equation as cellular respiration.
  • (c) Double displacement: Pb2+ pairs with I− (PbI2), K+ with NO3−. Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2 KNO3(aq) (bright yellow precipitate).
  • (d) Decomposition of a carbonate: MgCO3(s) → MgO(s) + CO2(g).

Check. Count atoms in each: all balanced. Ions recombine with the correct charges.

Professor Tip

Use the shape of the reactant side to classify. Two elements: combination. One compound alone (often heated): decomposition. An element plus a compound: single displacement. Two ionic compounds (often both aqueous): double displacement. Anything with O2 and a C–H fuel: combustion. Then write the product formulas from ion charges, not by copying subscripts from the reactants.

Biology Connection: Microbiology

The decomposition of hydrogen peroxide is the basis of the catalase test, one of the first tests used to sort unknown bacteria. Many bacteria, such as Staphylococcus, make the enzyme catalase, which speeds up 2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2. A small amount of the culture is mixed with a drop of 3% hydrogen peroxide: immediate bubbling (O2 gas) means catalase-positive; no bubbles means catalase-negative, as for Streptococcus. Your own cells use catalase too, to destroy the peroxide made during metabolism before it can damage proteins and DNA.

Valley Connection: Burning Versus Chipping Old Orchards

When an almond orchard reaches the end of its productive life, after about 20 to 25 years, the trees must be removed. For decades, the common practice was to pile and burn them. Open burning is incomplete combustion on a huge scale: besides CO2 and water, it releases carbon monoxide, soot, fine particles (PM2.5), and nitrogen oxides that help form ozone (Chapter 6), all of which the Valley's geography traps. California law passed in 2003 required the San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District to phase out most open burning of agricultural waste, and the district now funds alternatives. One alternative studied by UC Cooperative Extension researchers is whole orchard recycling: grinding the old trees into chips and working them into the soil, where microorganisms break them down slowly. Studies have found this can improve soil organic matter and water-holding capacity, an important benefit in a region where water is precious.

Sources: San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District, Agricultural Burning and alternatives programs (valleyair.org); California Senate Bill 705 (2003); UC Agriculture and Natural Resources, whole orchard recycling research (ucanr.edu).

Common Mistakes

  • Copying subscripts from reactants into products. In Pb(NO3)2 + KI, the product is PbI2 (from charges), not PbI.
  • Forgetting diatomic elements as products: H2(g), O2(g).
  • Calling every reaction with O2 combustion. 2 Mg + O2 → 2 MgO is a combination (and a redox reaction), but Mg is not a carbon fuel.

Summary

  • Combination: A + B → AB. Decomposition: AB → A + B. Single displacement: A + BC → AC + B. Double displacement: AB + CD → AD + CB. Combustion: fuel + O2 → CO2 + H2O.
  • Incomplete combustion also produces CO and soot.
  • Predict product formulas from ion charges, then balance.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify: (a) 2 KClO3(s) → 2 KCl(s) + 3 O2(g); (b) Fe(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → FeCl2(aq) + H2(g); (c) 4 Fe(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Fe2O3(s).

Answer

(a) decomposition. (b) single displacement. (c) combination (rusting).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of ethanol, C2H5OH(l).

Answer

C2H5OH(l) + 3 O2(g) → 2 CO2(g) + 3 H2O(g). O: 1 + 6 = 7 = 4 + 3.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Predict the products and balance: CaCl2(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) → ?

Answer

Double displacement: CaCl2(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) → CaCO3(s) + 2 NaCl(aq).
