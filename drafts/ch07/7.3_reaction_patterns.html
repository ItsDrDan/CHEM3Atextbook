Learning Objectives

  • Classify reactions as combination, decomposition, single displacement, double displacement, or combustion.
  • Predict the products of each pattern and balance the equation.
  • Distinguish complete from incomplete combustion.
  • Use the three-step routine: assign letters to match a pattern, write product formulas from charges (swap-drop) or bonding rules, then balance.
  • Predict which partner a free element replaces: metals replace cations; halogens replace anions.

Millions of reactions are known, but a handful of patterns cover most of the ones in this course. Recognizing the pattern tells you what the products will probably be, the same way recognizing a sentence's grammar tells you where the verb goes.

Combination (Synthesis)

Two or more reactants form one product: A + B → AB. A metal and a nonmetal combine into an ionic compound whose formula you predict from the ion charges (Section 4.2): 2 Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2 NaCl(s); 2 Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2 MgO(s).

Aluminum and iodine combine the same way. Al forms Al3+ and I forms I−, so the product is AlI3: 2 Al(s) + 3 I2(s) → 2 AlI3(s). The reaction gives off so much heat that you can see it, as the photos below show.

Decomposition

One reactant breaks into two or more simpler substances: AB → A + B, usually with heat, light, or electricity. A triangle (Δ) written over the arrow means the reactant is heated; for example, solid mercury(II) oxide heated in a test tube gives liquid mercury and oxygen gas: 2 HgO(s) → 2 Hg(l) + O2(g) (with Δ over the arrow). Two patterns to know: heating a metal carbonate gives a metal oxide and CO2 (CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)), and hydrogen peroxide breaks down into water and oxygen: 2 H2O2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + O2(g).

Single Displacement

An element replaces one partner in a compound: A + BC → AC + B. To predict which partner it replaces, ask what kind of ion the free element would make. It replaces the partner with the same kind of charge.

  • A metal forms a cation, so it replaces the cation (a metal ion or H+), keeping the new compound neutral: Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s).
  • A halogen (Cl2, Br2, I2) forms an anion, so it replaces the anion. Chlorine bubbled through sodium bromide solution takes bromide's place and frees bromine: Cl2(g) + 2 NaBr(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + Br2(aq). The sodium never moves. The free halogen leaves as the diatomic element, Br2, not "Br."

Metals in acid. Acids can be written as HX, H2Y, or H3Z: they release one, two, or three H+ ions (H+ and X−; 2 H+ and Y2−; 3 H+ and Z3−). A metal that reacts with an acid replaces the H+, the cation partner, and the hydrogen leaves as H2 gas, so a metal dissolving in acid is a single displacement. With a one-H+ acid: Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). With a two-H+ acid, the anion keeps its 2− charge: Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g). Notice the title of this idea in lecture: metals might dissolve in acid. Whether a single displacement actually happens depends on the activity series (Section 7.8); copper, silver, and gold, for example, do not replace H+.

Movie Chemistry: Does Acid Really Eat Through Metal?

In spy movies and sci-fi movies, acid is a favorite villain's weapon: a few drops hiss and smoke, and a steel door, a floor, or a hull melts away in seconds. Real chemistry is much slower and choosier. An active metal such as zinc or magnesium does react with hydrochloric acid, by single displacement: the metal replaces H+ and the hydrogen bubbles off as H2 gas.

Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)

You see fizzing, and the metal slowly gets smaller, but a piece of zinc takes minutes or longer to dissolve, and only as long as there is acid left to use up. Metals below hydrogen in the activity series, such as copper, silver, and gold, do not react with hydrochloric acid at all (Section 7.8). That is why "metals might dissolve in acid." Real acids are still dangerous: they damage skin and eyes, which is why you wear goggles and gloves in lab.

Double Displacement

Two ionic compounds swap partners: AB + CD → AD + CB. A memory aid is outside–inside: in AB + CD, the two outside ions (A and D) pair up, and the two inside ions (B and C) pair up. Then write the cation first in each new formula (CB, not BC), and rebuild neutral formulas from the charges: AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq). A double displacement happens only if something "leaves" the solution as a product that is not dissolved ions: a precipitate (Section 7.5), water (neutralization), or a gas (Section 7.6). If all the products are aqueous ions, there is no reaction.

Combustion

A carbon-based fuel reacts rapidly with oxygen, releasing heat and light. A problem may say only "methane burns in air." That sentence is the whole problem: you supply the O2(g) (air is the oxygen source) and the products CO2 and H2O yourself, then balance. Complete combustion (enough O2) of a fuel containing C, H, and possibly O gives CO2 and H2O: CH4(g) + 2 O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2 H2O(g). Incomplete combustion (too little O2) also forms carbon monoxide and soot (carbon): 2 CH4(g) + 3 O2(g) → 2 CO(g) + 4 H2O(g). That is why gas heaters and generators must never run in closed spaces.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Classify and predict

Problem. Classify each reaction and predict the products, then balance: (a) K(s) + Br2(l); (b) the combustion of glucose, C6H12O6; (c) Pb(NO3)2(aq) + KI(aq); (d) heating MgCO3(s).

Solution.

  • (a) Combination: K+ and Br− make KBr. 2 K(s) + Br2(l) → 2 KBr(s).
  • (b) Combustion: C6H12O6(s) + 6 O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 6 H2O(l), the same overall equation as cellular respiration.
  • (c) Double displacement: Pb2+ pairs with I− (PbI2), K+ with NO3−. Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2 KNO3(aq) (bright yellow precipitate).
  • (d) Decomposition of a carbonate: MgCO3(s) → MgO(s) + CO2(g).

Check. Count atoms in each: all balanced. Ions recombine with the correct charges.

Professor Tip

Use the shape of the reactant side to classify. Two elements (or two or more species making one product): combination. One compound alone (often heated; a carbonate heated always counts): decomposition. An element plus a compound: single displacement. Two ionic compounds (often both aqueous): double displacement. A fuel plus O2(g), or a fuel that "burns in air": combustion. Then write the product formulas from ion charges, not by copying subscripts from the reactants.

Predict and Balance in Three Steps

Predicting a reaction from its reactants alone feels like the hardest problem in this chapter, but it follows a recipe. There are only five patterns, and every prediction uses the same three steps:

  1. Assign letters and match the pattern. Give each element or ion (each cation and anion) a letter: A, B, C, D. The letters A–D are labels for the pieces, not the element symbols B and C; ignore subscripts. Write the ion charges above the reactants now; it makes steps 2 and 3 much easier. Then match the letters to a pattern: A + B, AB, A + BC, AB + CD, or fuel + O2.
  2. Write correct product formulas. Follow the pattern's recipe to pair the pieces. For ions, swap-drop the charges to make each product neutral (Section 4.2). For two nonmetals, use bonding rules (Chapter 6): H makes one bond and O makes two, so H and O give H2O. Never copy a subscript from a reactant, and remember the diatomic elements (H2, O2, Cl2, ...).
  3. Balance with coefficients only (Section 7.2): polyatomic ions as units, zig-zag, even the odds, lonely elements last.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): The three steps for a metal and an acid

Problem. Predict the products and balance: Al(s) + HCl(aq) → ?

Solution.

  1. Letters. Al is a free element: A. HCl has two pieces, H+ (B) and Cl− (C). A + BC matches single displacement, A + BC → AC + B. Aluminum is a metal and makes Al3+, so it replaces the cation, H+.
  2. Formulas. AC: Al3+ and Cl− swap-drop to AlCl3. B: hydrogen leaves as the diatomic gas, H2. Skeleton: Al(s) + HCl(aq) → AlCl3(aq) + H2(g).
  3. Balance. Leave the lonely Al for last. Cl: 1 left, 3 right, so 3 HCl. Now H is 3 on the left and 2 on the right: even the odds with 6 (6 HCl and 3 H2). Then Cl: 6 left, so 2 AlCl3. The kicker: 2 Al.

2 Al(s) + 6 HCl(aq) → 2 AlCl3(aq) + 3 H2(g)

Check. Al 2 = 2; H 6 = 6; Cl 6 = 6. Coefficients 2, 6, 2, 3 have no common factor. AlCl3 is neutral: 3+ and 3(1−).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A halogen replaces an anion

Problem. Chlorine gas is bubbled into a solution of calcium iodide. Predict the products and balance.

Solution. Letters: Cl2 is A; CaI2 is Ca2+ (B) and I− (C). A + BC is single displacement. Chlorine forms Cl−, an anion, so it replaces the anion, I−. Formulas: Ca2+ and Cl− make CaCl2; iodine leaves as I2. Balance: Ca 1 = 1, Cl 2 = 2, I 2 = 2. It is already balanced.

Cl2(g) + CaI2(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + I2(aq)

Check. Calcium stays a cation and keeps its partner count; only the anion changed. (This one does happen: chlorine is more reactive than iodine.)

Biology Connection: Microbiology

The decomposition of hydrogen peroxide is the basis of the catalase test, one of the first tests used to sort unknown bacteria. Many bacteria, such as Staphylococcus, make the enzyme catalase, which speeds up 2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2. A small amount of the culture is mixed with a drop of 3% hydrogen peroxide: immediate bubbling (O2 gas) means catalase-positive; no bubbles means catalase-negative, as for Streptococcus. Your own cells use catalase too, to destroy the peroxide made during metabolism before it can damage proteins and DNA.

Valley Connection: Burning Versus Chipping Old Orchards

When an almond orchard reaches the end of its productive life, after about 20 to 25 years, the trees must be removed. For decades, the common practice was to pile and burn them. Open burning is incomplete combustion on a huge scale: besides CO2 and water, it releases carbon monoxide, soot, fine particles (PM2.5), and nitrogen oxides that help form ozone (Chapter 6), all of which the Valley's geography traps. California law passed in 2003 required the San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District to phase out most open burning of agricultural waste, and the district now funds alternatives. One alternative studied by UC Cooperative Extension researchers is whole orchard recycling: grinding the old trees into chips and working them into the soil, where microorganisms break them down slowly. Studies have found this can improve soil organic matter and water-holding capacity, an important benefit in a region where water is precious.

Sources: San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District, Agricultural Burning and alternatives programs (valleyair.org); California Senate Bill 705 (2003); UC Agriculture and Natural Resources, whole orchard recycling research (ucanr.edu).

Common Mistakes

  • Copying subscripts from reactants into products. In Pb(NO3)2 + KI, the product is PbI2 (from charges), not PbI.
  • Forgetting diatomic elements as products: H2(g), O2(g).
  • Letting a halogen replace the cation. Cl2 + 2 NaBr gives 2 NaCl + Br2, not ClBr + Na. A free element replaces the partner with the same kind of charge.
  • Skipping the charges. Label every ion's charge before you swap-drop; most wrong products come from a missing or wrong charge.
  • Calling every reaction with O2 combustion. 2 Mg + O2 → 2 MgO is a combination (and a redox reaction), but Mg is not a carbon fuel.

Summary

  • Combination: A + B → AB. Decomposition: AB → A + B. Single displacement: A + BC → AC + B. Double displacement: AB + CD → AD + CB. Combustion: fuel + O2 → CO2 + H2O.
  • Incomplete combustion also produces CO and soot.
  • Predict product formulas from ion charges, then balance.
  • Three steps: assign letters and match a pattern; swap-drop (ions) or bonding rules (nonmetals) for correct formulas; balance.
  • In single displacement a metal replaces the cation (including H+ in an acid); a halogen replaces the anion. In double displacement, outside ions pair and inside ions pair.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Classify: (a) 2 KClO3(s) → 2 KCl(s) + 3 O2(g); (b) Fe(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → FeCl2(aq) + H2(g); (c) 4 Fe(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Fe2O3(s).

Hint

Count reactants and products: one reactant breaking apart, an element replacing another, or elements joining into one product?

Answer

(a) decomposition. (b) single displacement. (c) combination (rusting).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of ethanol, C2H5OH(l).

Hint

Supply CO2 and H2O. Balance C, then H, then O, remembering the fuel's own O atom.

Answer

C2H5OH(l) + 3 O2(g) → 2 CO2(g) + 3 H2O(g). O: 1 + 6 = 7 = 4 + 3.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Predict the products and balance: CaCl2(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) → ?

Hint

Two ionic compounds: swap partners (outside–inside), swap-drop the charges, and check the green sheet for the insoluble product.

Answer

Double displacement: CaCl2(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) → CaCO3(s) + 2 NaCl(aq).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Predict the products and balance: Br2(aq) + KI(aq) → ?

Hint

A halogen forms an anion, so it replaces the anion. Write the products, then balance with iodine as diatomic.

Answer

Single displacement. Bromine forms Br−, so it replaces the anion, I−: Br2(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → 2 KBr(aq) + I2(aq). Br 2 = 2; K 2 = 2; I 2 = 2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Use the three steps for a three-H+ acid: Mg(s) + H3PO4(aq) → ?

Hint

Letters, formulas, balance: Mg replaces H+. Swap-drop Mg2+ with PO43−, then balance phosphate as a unit and leave the lonely Mg for last.

Answer

Letters: A + BC, single displacement; Mg2+ replaces H+. Swap-drop Mg2+ with PO43−: Mg3(PO4)2; hydrogen leaves as H2. Balance phosphate as a unit (2 H3PO4), zig to H (6 H, so 3 H2), and finish with the lonely Mg (3 Mg): 3 Mg(s) + 2 H3PO4(aq) → Mg3(PO4)2(s) + 3 H2(g). (Magnesium phosphate is insoluble.) Check: Mg 3 = 3; PO4 2 = 2; H 6 = 6.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

"Propane burns in air." Write the balanced equation.

Hint

Burns in air means fuel + O2 → CO2 + H2O. Balance C, then H, then O.

Answer

Supply O2 and the combustion products yourself: C3H8(g) + 5 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 4 H2O(g). After C and H, oxygen on the right is 6 + 4 = 10 (even), so 5 O2 finishes it.
