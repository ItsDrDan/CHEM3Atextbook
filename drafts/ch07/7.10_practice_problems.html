7.1 Chemical Changes and Chemical Equations

Problem 7.1

Which observation is the best evidence that a chemical reaction has happened?

A. Rubbing alcohol evaporates from a patient's arm, and the skin feels cool.
B. The inner pouch of an instant cold pack breaks, the ammonium nitrate inside dissolves in the water, and the pack gets cold.
C. An effervescent antacid tablet containing sodium bicarbonate and citric acid is dropped into water and fizzes as a new gas forms.
D. Water in a kettle bubbles as it boils.

Hint

Ask of each choice: did a new substance form? A gas, a temperature change, or bubbles can also come from a change of state or dissolving.

Answer

C. When the tablet dissolves, the bicarbonate and the citric acid react with each other and form carbon dioxide, a substance that was not in the tablet. A gas that forms from a new substance (not boiling, not a dissolved gas escaping) is evidence of a chemical change.

  • A is evaporation, a change of state. The alcohol molecules are the same in the air as on the skin; the cooling is the energy needed to vaporize them.
  • B shows an energy change, but dissolving is a physical change: the NH4+ and NO3− ions just spread out in the water and no new substance forms. An energy change is a clue, not proof.
  • D: the bubbles in boiling water are water vapor, the same substance as the liquid, so this is a physical change.

The table below lists six observations from a clinic, a lab, and a Valley farm.

Chemical or physical change? (blanks marked ?)
ObservationChemical change?Evidence or reason

Fill in the last two columns.

Hint

For each row, ask whether the substance after the change is a new substance or the same substance in a different state. A sign alone (a gas, heat) can mislead.

Problem 7.3

Ammonia for the nitrogen fertilizers used on Valley crops is made by the reaction below, run at high temperature and pressure with an iron catalyst (Fe written over the arrow).

N2(g) + 3 H2(g) → 2 NH3(g)    (Fe catalyst)

Which statement about this equation is correct?

A. "3 H2" means three hydrogen molecules, which contain 6 hydrogen atoms in all.
B. The 2 in "2 NH3" means each ammonia molecule contains two nitrogen atoms.
C. The iron written over the arrow is a reactant that is used up in the reaction.
D. The (g) labels mean that each substance is dissolved in water.

Hint

Separate the three kinds of numbers: a coefficient counts molecules, a subscript counts atoms in one molecule, and anything over the arrow is a catalyst. Check what (g) means.

Answer

A. A coefficient counts molecules, and the subscript counts atoms in each molecule: 3 molecules × 2 H atoms = 6 H atoms. The right side also has 6 H atoms (2 × 3), as conservation of mass requires.

  • B confuses a coefficient with a subscript. "2 NH3" is two molecules, each with one N and three H atoms.
  • C: a substance written over the arrow is a catalyst. It speeds up the reaction but is not used up, so it is not counted as a reactant or a product.
  • D: (g) means gas. Dissolved in water is (aq), aqueous.

Problem 7.4

Write a balanced equation, with phase labels, for each description. (a) Farmers spread quicklime to raise soil pH. Solid calcium oxide reacts with liquid water to form solid calcium hydroxide. (b) In a lab demonstration, a small piece of solid sodium metal is dropped into liquid water; it forms aqueous sodium hydroxide and hydrogen gas.

Hint

Turn names into formulas first (Chapter 4), remembering that hydrogen gas is diatomic. Then count atoms on each side and add coefficients, never changing subscripts.

Answer

(a) Names become formulas (Chapter 4): calcium oxide is Ca2+ with O2−, CaO; calcium hydroxide is Ca2+ with two OH−, Ca(OH)2.

CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(s)

Already balanced: Ca 1 = 1; O 2 = 2; H 2 = 2.

(b) Hydrogen gas is diatomic, H2(g). Skeleton: Na + H2O → NaOH + H2. H: 2 on the left and 3 on the right, so put 2 in front of H2O and of NaOH (4 H on each side), then 2 in front of Na:

2 Na(s) + 2 H2O(l) → 2 NaOH(aq) + H2(g)

Check: Na 2 = 2; H 4 = 2 + 2; O 2 = 2.

Problem 7.5

A student heats 3.50 g of iron filings with excess powdered sulfur. All of the iron reacts, and 5.51 g of iron(II) sulfide forms. (a) Write the balanced equation, with phase labels. (b) What mass of sulfur reacted?

Hint

(a) Iron(II) is Fe2+ and sulfide is S2−; combine them. (b) Use conservation of mass, counting only the sulfur that ended up in the product.

Answer

(a) Iron(II) is Fe2+ and sulfide is S2−, so the product is FeS. This is a combination reaction:

Fe(s) + S(s) → FeS(s)

(b) By conservation of mass, mass of Fe + mass of S that reacted = mass of FeS: 3.50 g + mass of S = 5.51 g, so the sulfur that reacted is 5.51 g − 3.50 g = 2.01 g. (Only the sulfur that reacted counts; the excess sulfur is left over and is not part of the product.)

Problem 7.6

A 2.0 g effervescent antacid tablet is dropped into a cup holding 150.0 g of water. The cup is open to the air. After the fizzing stops, the cup and its contents (not counting the cup itself) have a mass of 151.4 g. Which statement best explains the result?

A. About 0.6 g of mass was destroyed in the reaction.
B. About 0.6 g of carbon dioxide gas escaped into the air; the total mass of all the products still equals the total mass of the reactants.
C. The water evaporated, so the 0.6 g that was lost is water vapor.
D. The atoms in the tablet changed into lighter elements.

Hint

Add the masses before and after. Mass is conserved, so where could the missing mass have gone from an open cup that fizzes?

Answer

B. Before: 2.0 g + 150.0 g = 152.0 g. After: 151.4 g. The difference, 152.0 g − 151.4 g = 0.6 g, is the mass of the CO2 that bubbled out of the open cup. In a sealed container, the mass would not change.

  • A breaks the law of conservation of mass. Matter is not destroyed in a chemical reaction; it left the cup as a gas.
  • C: a little water does evaporate from any open cup, but the fizzing is the gas formed by the reaction, CO2. Evaporation during a few minutes is far too slow to explain the loss.
  • D: chemical reactions rearrange atoms; they never change one element into another (that happens only in nuclear reactions, Chapter 14).

7.2 Balancing Chemical Equations

Problem 7.7

In a blast furnace, iron ore is reduced to iron metal with carbon. When the equation below is balanced with the smallest whole numbers, what is the coefficient of C?

__ Fe2O3(s) + __ C(s) → __ Fe(s) + __ CO2(g)

A. 1
B. 2
C. 3
D. 6

Hint

Start with O: Fe2O3 has 3 and CO2 has 2, so find the smallest number both divide into. Then balance C and Fe, and use the smallest whole numbers.

Answer

C. O: Fe2O3 has 3 O and CO2 has 2, so the smallest equal number is 6: 2 Fe2O3 and 3 CO2. Then C: 3 CO2 needs 3 C. Fe: 2 × 2 = 4 Fe.

2 Fe2O3(s) + 3 C(s) → 4 Fe(s) + 3 CO2(g)

Check: Fe 4 = 4; O 6 = 6; C 3 = 3.

  • A: with 1 C, there would be 1 CO2 (2 O), but O comes from Fe2O3 in groups of 3; 2 and 3 cannot match unless both are adjusted.
  • B: 2 C gives 2 CO2 with 4 O, which no whole number of Fe2O3 (3, 6, 9 … O) can supply.
  • D: 6 C works only with 4 Fe2O3, 8 Fe, and 6 CO2, which is the right answer doubled. The coefficients must be the smallest whole numbers.

Problem 7.8

Balance each equation. (a) Phosphorus burns in a match head: P4(s) + O2(g) → P4O10(s). (b) Aluminum reacts with sulfuric acid: Al(s) + H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + H2(g). (c) Phosphate fertilizer is made by treating phosphate rock with sulfuric acid: Ca3(PO4)2(s) + H2SO4(aq) → CaSO4(s) + H3PO4(aq).

Hint

(a) Only O needs work. (b) and (c) Treat sulfate and phosphate as units; balance the metal and the polyatomic ions before H.

Answer

(a) P is already 4 = 4. O: 10 on the right, so 5 O2:

P4(s) + 5 O2(g) → P4O10(s)

(b) Treat sulfate as a unit. Al2(SO4)3 has 2 Al and 3 SO4, so use 2 Al and 3 H2SO4. Then 3 H2SO4 has 6 H, which makes 3 H2:

2 Al(s) + 3 H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3 H2(g)

Check: Al 2 = 2; SO4 3 = 3; H 6 = 6.

(c) Treat phosphate and sulfate as units. Ca: 3 on the left, so 3 CaSO4; that needs 3 SO4, so 3 H2SO4. PO4: 2 on the left, so 2 H3PO4. H: 6 = 2 × 3.

Ca3(PO4)2(s) + 3 H2SO4(aq) → 3 CaSO4(s) + 2 H3PO4(aq)

Check: Ca 3 = 3; PO4 2 = 2; SO4 3 = 3; H 6 = 6.

Problem 7.9

Which equation is balanced and uses correct formulas?

A. 2 KI(aq) + Cl2(g) → 2 KCl(aq) + I2(aq)
B. H2O2(aq) → H2O(l) + O2(g)
C. N2(g) + H2(g) → 2 NH3(g)
D. Na(s) + Cl2(g) → NaCl2(s)

Hint

Check two things for each choice: are the formulas real (diatomic elements, correct ion ratios), and does every atom count match on both sides?

Answer

A. K 2 = 2; I 2 = 2; Cl 2 = 2, and every formula is correct (K+ with I− or Cl−; iodine is the diatomic element I2).

  • B is not balanced: 2 O on the left and 1 + 2 = 3 O on the right. The balanced form is 2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2.
  • C balances N (2 = 2) but not H: 2 on the left and 6 on the right. It needs 3 H2.
  • D has the same atoms on both sides, but NaCl2 is not a real compound. Na+ and Cl− combine 1:1 as NaCl; the subscript was changed to force a balance. Correct: 2 Na + Cl2 → 2 NaCl.

Problem 7.10

Butane, C4H10, is the fuel in disposable lighters and in the small torches used in some labs. (a) Balance the equation for its complete combustion. (b) What is the sum of the coefficients in the balanced equation?

C4H10(g) + O2(g) → CO2(g) + H2O(g)

Balance C, then H, then O. If the oxygen total on the right is odd, even the odds: double the fuel and start again. Then add the coefficients.

Problem 7.11

When the equation below is balanced with the smallest whole numbers, what is the coefficient of NaNO3?

__ Ba(NO3)2(aq) + __ Na3PO4(aq) → __ Ba3(PO4)2(s) + __ NaNO3(aq)

A. 2
B. 3
C. 5
D. 6

Hint

Treat nitrate and phosphate as units. Start with the precipitate's 3 Ba and 2 PO4, then count how many Na and NO3 that brings in.

Answer

D. Treat the ions as units. Ba3(PO4)2 needs 3 Ba(NO3)2 and 2 Na3PO4. That brings in 3 × 2 = 6 NO3 and 2 × 3 = 6 Na, so 6 NaNO3.

3 Ba(NO3)2(aq) + 2 Na3PO4(aq) → Ba3(PO4)2(s) + 6 NaNO3(aq)

Problem 7.12

A car's airbag inflates when solid sodium azide, NaN3, decomposes into sodium metal and nitrogen gas. Three students balanced the equation:

Student 1: 2 NaN3(s) → 2 Na(s) + 3 N2(g)
Student 2: NaN3(s) → Na(s) + N3(g)
Student 3: 4 NaN3(s) → 4 Na(s) + 6 N2(g)

Which student is correct, and what is wrong with each of the other two answers?

7.3 Classifying Reactions by Pattern

Problem 7.13

In the catalase test, a microbiology student mixes a bit of a bacterial colony with a drop of 3% hydrogen peroxide. The mixture bubbles right away. Which gas makes the bubbles, and what type of reaction is it?

2 H2O2(aq) → 2 H2O(l) + O2(g)    (catalase)

A. CO2; combustion
B. O2; decomposition
C. H2; single displacement
D. O2; combination

Hint

Read the equation: how many reactants and products are there, and which product is a gas? Match that to a pattern.

Answer

B. One reactant breaks down into two simpler products, so it is a decomposition, and the gas is oxygen, O2. The enzyme catalase is a catalyst, written over the arrow; bubbles mean the bacteria are catalase-positive.

  • A: there is no carbon in the equation, so CO2 cannot form, and combustion needs a fuel reacting with O2. Here O2 is a product.
  • C: no hydrogen gas forms, and a single displacement needs an element reacting with a compound. The only reactant is one compound.
  • D has the right gas but the wrong pattern. A combination joins two or more reactants into one product; this is the reverse, one reactant into two products.

Problem 7.14

Classify each reaction as combination, decomposition, single displacement, double displacement, or combustion.

(a) 2 HgO(s) → 2 Hg(l) + O2(g)

(b) SO3(g) + H2O(l) → H2SO4(aq)

(c) Cl2(g) + 2 NaBr(aq) → 2 NaCl(aq) + Br2(aq)

(d) Na2S(aq) + Cu(NO3)2(aq) → CuS(s) + 2 NaNO3(aq)

(e) 2 C3H8O(l) + 9 O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 8 H2O(g)

Hint

Count reactants and products and look at what kind they are (element, compound, fuel + O2). Match each to A + B, AB, A + BC, AB + CD, or fuel + O2.

Answer
  • (a) Decomposition: one compound, mercury(II) oxide, breaks into two elements when heated.
  • (b) Combination: two reactants form one product. (This is a step in forming acid rain from sulfur pollution.)
  • (c) Single displacement: an element (Cl2) replaces another element (bromine) in a compound. A more reactive halogen can push out a less reactive one.
  • (d) Double displacement: two ionic compounds swap partners, and the product CuS is an insoluble sulfide (a precipitate).
  • (e) Combustion: a C, H, O fuel (isopropyl alcohol, rubbing alcohol) reacts with O2 to form CO2 and H2O.

Problem 7.15

Predict the products, write the balanced equation with phase labels, and name the pattern. (a) Aluminum metal reacts with liquid bromine. (b) Solid zinc carbonate is heated strongly. (c) Magnesium metal is added to sulfuric acid. (d) Calcium metal is heated in nitrogen gas.

Hint

Use the three steps: letters, formulas, balance. Write the ion charges first, swap-drop to build each product, and remember that Br2, H2, and N2 are diatomic.

Answer

(a) Combination. Al3+ and Br− make AlBr3. Br: 2 and 3 meet at 6, so 3 Br2 and 2 AlBr3, then 2 Al:

2 Al(s) + 3 Br2(l) → 2 AlBr3(s)

(b) Decomposition of a metal carbonate into a metal oxide and CO2 (Zn2+ with O2− is ZnO):

ZnCO3(s) → ZnO(s) + CO2(g)

(c) Single displacement. Magnesium replaces hydrogen; Mg2+ pairs with SO42− (MgSO4, soluble), and hydrogen leaves as H2 gas:

Mg(s) + H2SO4(aq) → MgSO4(aq) + H2(g)

(d) Combination. Ca2+ and the nitride ion, N3−, cancel at Ca3N2 (3(+2) + 2(−3) = 0). N2 supplies the 2 N, so 3 Ca:

3 Ca(s) + N2(g) → Ca3N2(s)

Problem 7.16

A family uses a charcoal grill inside a closed garage during a power outage, and two of them come to the emergency department with headaches and confusion. Which balanced equation shows the incomplete combustion of charcoal (carbon) that produces carbon monoxide?

A. C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g)
B. 2 C(s) + O2(g) → 2 CO(g)
C. 2 CO(g) + O2(g) → 2 CO2(g)
D. C(s) + O2(g) → CO(g)

Hint

Incomplete combustion means too little O2, so the product is CO. Which choice makes CO and is also balanced?

Answer

B. With too little O2, each carbon atom picks up only one oxygen atom and forms CO. The equation is balanced: C 2 = 2; O 2 = 2. In a closed space, the O2 runs low and CO builds up. CO binds to hemoglobin much more tightly than O2 does, which is why carbon monoxide poisoning causes these symptoms; respiratory therapists use a CO-oximeter, not a standard pulse oximeter, to measure it. (Context only; this is not treatment advice.)

  • A is complete combustion (plenty of O2), which makes CO2, not CO.
  • C is carbon monoxide burning further to CO2. It removes CO rather than producing it.
  • D shows the right product but is not balanced: 2 O on the left and 1 O on the right.

Problem 7.17

Almond shells and old orchard wood are mostly cellulose, whose repeating unit is C6H10O5. (a) Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of one C6H10O5 unit. (b) When a pile of prunings smolders with too little air, what other products form besides CO2 and H2O?

Hint

(a) Balance C, then H, then O, and remember the fuel already supplies some O. (b) What does too little air do to the carbon products?

Answer

(a) C: 6 CO2. H: 10 H makes 5 H2O. O on the right: 6(2) + 5 = 17. The fuel already supplies 5 O, so O2 must supply 17 − 5 = 12 O atoms, or 6 O2:

Check: C 6 = 6; H 10 = 10; O 5 + 12 = 17 = 12 + 5.

(b) Incomplete combustion also forms carbon monoxide (CO) and soot (carbon), along with the fine particles (PM2.5) that make smoke harmful to breathe. That is one reason open agricultural burning is restricted in the San Joaquin Valley.

Which reaction is a double displacement?

7.4 Electrolytes and Solubility Rules

Problem 7.19

Household ammonia cleaner is a solution of NH3 in water. Which of the following is a weak electrolyte?

A. HNO3
B. NH3
C. KCl
D. C12H22O11 (sucrose)

Hint

Use the step-by-step classification: is it ionic? Is it an acid or a base on the green-sheet strong list? If not on the list, it is weak.

Answer

B. Ammonia is a weak base: only a small fraction of the NH3 molecules react with water to form NH4+ and OH− ions, so its solution conducts weakly.

  • A: nitric acid is on the green sheet's list of strong acids, so it ionizes completely. It is a strong electrolyte.
  • C: potassium chloride is a soluble ionic compound (Group 1), which separates completely into K+ and Cl−. Strong electrolyte.
  • D: sucrose dissolves as whole molecules and forms no ions, so it is a nonelectrolyte.

Problem 7.20

Write the equation for each compound dissolving in water, showing the ions with the correct counts and (aq) labels. If no ions form, say so. (a) K3PO4; (b) (NH4)2SO4, a common fertilizer; (c) Fe(NO3)3; (d) C6H12O6, glucose.

Split each soluble ionic compound into its ions, keeping polyatomic ions whole, and use the subscripts for the counts. Is the last compound ionic at all?

(a) K3PO4(s) → 3 K+(aq) + PO43−(aq)

(b) (NH4)2SO4(s) → 2 NH4+(aq) + SO42−(aq)

Problem 7.22

All four compounds below matter in health care. According to the green-sheet solubility rules, which one is soluble in water?

A. Ca3(PO4)2, the mineral in bone
B. CaC2O4, the main compound in most kidney stones
C. BaSO4, the contrast agent swallowed before GI X-rays
D. KCl, added to IV fluids to replace potassium

Check each against the green sheet. Which cation makes every compound soluble, and which anions have exceptions that include these cations?

Problem 7.24

Answer

(A test in an Fe2+ solution would not help: Fe is the highest of the three, so none of them would react.)

Problem 7.48

Use the activity series, listed from most active to least active:

Li, K, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Ni, Sn, Pb, H2, Cu, Ag, Au

Which reaction occurs?

A. Cu(s) + ZnSO4(aq) →
B. Pb(s) + MgCl2(aq) →
C. Ni(s) + 2 AgNO3(aq) →
D. Au(s) + HCl(aq) →

Hint

A free metal displaces only the ions of a metal below it (or H2 from acid if it is above H2). Compare each pair.

Answer

C. Nickel is above silver, so it displaces Ag+:

  • B: lead is far below magnesium. No reaction.
  • D: gold is below H2 and does not react with acids such as HCl.

Problem 7.49

Use the activity series, listed from most active to least active:

Li, K, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Ni, Sn, Pb, H2, Cu, Ag, Au

Potassium metal is stored under oil in the stockroom, but copper pipes carry drinking water for decades. (a) Write the balanced equation, with phase labels, for potassium reacting with liquid water to form aqueous potassium hydroxide and hydrogen gas. (b) Use the series to explain why copper does not react with water.

Hint

(a) Potassium replaces one H in water, and hydrogen leaves as H2; balance H last. (b) Where is copper compared with H2?

Answer

(a) Potassium replaces one H in each water molecule, and the H atoms pair up as H2:

2 K(s) + 2 H2O(l) → 2 KOH(aq) + H2(g)

Check: K 2 = 2; H 4 = 2 + 2; O 2 = 2. Potassium is near the top of the series, among the metals active enough to react with water, so it must be kept away from air and moisture.

(b) Copper is below H2. It cannot even displace hydrogen from acids, so it certainly cannot displace hydrogen from water. Only the most active metals at the top of the list react with water.

Problem 7.51

Acetone, C3H6O, is the solvent in nail-polish remover, and it is also one of the ketones that can give a fruity smell to the breath of a person in diabetic ketoacidosis. (a) Write the balanced equation for its complete combustion. (b) Find its molar mass. (c) In a sealed container, 1.16 g of acetone burns completely with exactly 2.56 g of oxygen, forming 2.64 g of carbon dioxide. What mass of water forms? (H 1.008, C 12.011, O 15.999)

Hint

(a) Balance C, then H, then O, remembering the fuel supplies one O. (b) Add atomic masses × subscripts. (c) Use conservation of mass in the sealed container.

Answer

(a) C: 3 CO2. H: 6 H makes 3 H2O. O on the right: 3(2) + 3 = 9; the fuel supplies 1, so O2 supplies 8 O atoms, or 4 O2:

C3H6O(l) + 4 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 3 H2O(g)

Check: C 3 = 3; H 6 = 6; O 1 + 8 = 9 = 6 + 3.

(b) 3(12.011) + 6(1.008) + 15.999 = 36.033 + 6.048 + 15.999 = 58.080 g/mol (3 decimal places, set by the masses as printed).

(c) Conservation of mass: total reactant mass = 1.16 g + 2.56 g = 3.72 g, so the water is 3.72 g − 2.64 g = 1.08 g. (Chapter 8 shows how to predict all of these masses from the balanced equation.)

Problem 7.52

Use the activity series, listed from most active to least active:

Li, K, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Ni, Sn, Pb, H2, Cu, Ag, Au

A strip of tin metal is placed in aqueous copper(II) chloride. (a) Write the formula of copper(II) chloride (Chapter 4). (b) Will a reaction occur? (c) If so, write the balanced equation, with tin forming tin(II) chloride, and name the reaction pattern. (d) Write the two half-reactions and identify the oxidizing and reducing agents.

Hint

(a) Use swap-drop for copper(II) and chloride. (b) Compare tin and copper in the series. (d) Oxidation puts electrons on the right; reduction on the left.

Answer

(a) Copper(II) is Cu2+, so it needs two Cl−: CuCl2.

(b) Yes. Tin is above copper in the series.

(c) Single displacement (and a redox reaction):

Sn(s) + CuCl2(aq) → SnCl2(aq) + Cu(s)

(d) Oxidation: Sn(s) → Sn2+(aq) + 2 e−. Reduction: Cu2+(aq) + 2 e− → Cu(s). The electron counts already match (2 each). Reducing agent: Sn (oxidized, 0 → +2). Oxidizing agent: CuCl2, specifically the Cu2+ ion (reduced, +2 → 0). Cl− is a spectator.

Problem 7.53

Potassium hydrogen carbonate (potassium bicarbonate) is the active ingredient in some antacids. Which is the correct balanced equation for its reaction with hydrochloric acid?

A. KHCO3(aq) + HCl(aq) → KCl(aq) + H2CO3(aq)
B. KHCO3(aq) + HCl(aq) → KCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
C. KHCO3(aq) + HCl(aq) → KCl(aq) + H2(g) + CO3(g)
D. KHCO3(aq) + 2 HCl(aq) → KCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

Hint

Write the correct salt formula from the ion charges, then check the unstable-products table: what does H2CO3 become?

Answer

B. From the names: potassium is K+ and hydrogen carbonate is HCO3−, so KHCO3; hydrochloric acid is HCl. Swapping partners gives KCl and H2CO3, and carbonic acid becomes H2O(l) + CO2(g). Check: K 1 = 1; H 2 = 2; C 1 = 1; O 3 = 1 + 2; Cl 1 = 1.

  • A is balanced but stops too early. H2CO3 is unstable and should never be a final product.
  • C invents products. The carbonic acid breaks down into water and CO2, not H2 gas, and "CO3(g)" is not a real substance.
  • D uses a wrong formula: K+ and Cl− combine 1:1, so the salt is KCl, not KCl2. (D is not balanced in H either.)

Problem 7.54

Baking soda, sodium hydrogen carbonate, helps baked goods rise because heat breaks it down:

2 NaHCO3(s) → Na2CO3(s) + H2O(g) + CO2(g)

(a) What type of reaction is this? (b) Find the molar mass of NaHCO3. (c) A student heats 4.20 g of NaHCO3 in an open crucible until the reaction is complete, and 2.65 g of solid sodium carbonate remains. What total mass of gases escaped? (H 1.008, C 12.011, O 15.999, Na 22.99)

Hint

(a) Count the reactants and products. (b) Add atomic masses. (c) Use conservation of mass: the gases are whatever mass left the crucible.

Answer

(a) Decomposition: one reactant breaks into three simpler products when heated. (No oxidation numbers change, so it is not redox.)

(b) 22.99 + 1.008 + 12.011 + 3(15.999) = 22.99 + 1.008 + 12.011 + 47.997 = 84.006, which rounds to 84.01 g/mol (2 decimal places, set by Na).

(c) Conservation of mass: gases = 4.20 g − 2.65 g = 1.55 g of water vapor and carbon dioxide together. The solid weighs less because the gases left the crucible, not because mass was destroyed.

Problem 7.55

(Going Further, not on CHEM 3A exams unless assigned) A hospital lab checks the strength of a hydrogen peroxide disinfectant by reacting it with purple permanganate ion in acidic solution. Permanganate is reduced to colorless Mn2+, and the peroxide is oxidized to O2. Balance the reaction in acidic solution with the half-reaction method.

MnO4−(aq) + H2O2(aq) → Mn2+(aq) + O2(g)

Hint

For each half-reaction: balance atoms other than O and H, then O with H2O, H with H+, and charge with electrons. Then match the electrons and add.

Answer

Oxidation: H2O2 → O2. O is balanced (2 = 2); add 2 H+ on the right for H; the charge is 0 on the left and +2 on the right, so add 2 e− on the right:

H2O2 → O2 + 2 H+ + 2 e−

Reduction: MnO4− → Mn2+. Add 4 H2O on the right for O; add 8 H+ on the left for H; the charge is −1 + 8 = +7 on the left and +2 on the right, so add 5 e− on the left:

MnO4− + 8 H+ + 5 e− → Mn2+ + 4 H2O

The electrons meet at 10: multiply the oxidation by 5 and the reduction by 2, add, and cancel 10 e− and 10 of the 16 H+:

2 MnO4−(aq) + 5 H2O2(aq) + 6 H+(aq) → 2 Mn2+(aq) + 5 O2(g) + 8 H2O(l)

Check: Mn 2 = 2; O 8 + 10 = 18 = 10 + 8; H 10 + 6 = 16 = 16; charge −2 + 6 = +4 on the left and 2(+2) = +4 on the right.

Problem 7.56

(Going Further, not on CHEM 3A exams unless assigned) Many pacemakers run for years on a lithium–iodine battery, whose overall reaction is

2 Li(s) + I2(s) → 2 LiI(s)

Which electrode is the anode, and what happens there?

A. The lithium electrode; lithium is oxidized: Li → Li+ + e−.
B. The iodine electrode; iodine is oxidized: I2 → 2 I− + 2 e−.
C. The lithium electrode; lithium is reduced: Li + e− → Li−.
D. The iodine electrode; iodine is reduced: I2 + 2 e− → 2 I−.

Hint

Which process, oxidation or reduction, happens at the anode? Track each element's oxidation number from the reactants to LiI.

Answer

A. The anode is where oxidation happens. Lithium goes from 0 to +1 in LiI, so it loses electrons, which flow out through the wire to the pacemaker circuit.

  • B: iodine gains electrons (0 → −1). The half-reaction as written is not even balanced in charge: 0 on the left, −4 on the right.
  • C: lithium, a Group 1 metal, loses electrons to form Li+; it does not gain them. Reduction happens at the cathode, not the anode.
  • D correctly describes the cathode, where iodine is reduced. The question asks for the anode.

Problem 7.57

Balance with the smallest whole numbers. What is the coefficient of H2O?

__ Sr(OH)2(aq) + __ H3PO4(aq) → __ Sr3(PO4)2(s) + __ H2O(l)

A. 2
B. 3
C. 6
D. 12

Hint

Write water as HOH and zig-zag: start with phosphate, zig to its partner H, zag to OH, then zig to Sr.

Answer

C. Write water as HOH and zig-zag. Phosphate: 2 on the right, so 2 H3PO4. Zig to H: 6 H, so 6 HOH. Zag to OH: 6 OH, so 3 Sr(OH)2. Zig to Sr: 3 = 3.

3 Sr(OH)2(aq) + 2 H3PO4(aq) → Sr3(PO4)2(s) + 6 H2O(l)

Check: Sr 3 = 3; P 2 = 2; H 6 + 6 = 12 = 12; O 6 + 8 = 14 = 8 + 6.

  • A is the coefficient of H3PO4, not of water.
  • B is the coefficient of Sr(OH)2. With only 3 H2O, the 12 H atoms on the left would not all be accounted for.
  • D is the total number of H atoms on each side (12), not the number of water molecules; each H2O holds 2 H.

Problem 7.58

Use the three steps (letters, formulas, balance) to predict and balance each reaction. (a) Chlorine gas is bubbled into magnesium bromide solution. (b) Calcium metal is dropped into hydrobromic acid, HBr(aq). (c) Pentane, C5H12, burns in air.

Hint

Use the three steps: letters, formulas, balance. (a) A halogen replaces the anion. (b) A metal replaces the cation (H+). (c) Burns in air means fuel + O2.

Answer

(a) A + BC: single displacement. Chlorine forms Cl−, an anion, so it replaces bromide. Mg2+ and Cl− make MgCl2; bromine leaves as Br2:

Cl2(g) + MgBr2(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + Br2(aq)

Already balanced: Mg 1, Cl 2, Br 2 on each side.

(b) A + BC: single displacement. Calcium forms Ca2+, a cation, so it replaces H+. Ca2+ and Br− make CaBr2; hydrogen leaves as H2. Balance Br with 2 HBr, which also fixes H:

Ca(s) + 2 HBr(aq) → CaBr2(aq) + H2(g)

(c) "Burns in air" means combustion: supply O2, CO2, and H2O. C: 5 CO2. H: 12 H, so 6 H2O. O on the right: 10 + 6 = 16, even, so 8 O2:

C5H12(l) + 8 O2(g) → 5 CO2(g) + 6 H2O(g)

Check: C 5 = 5; H 12 = 12; O 16 = 16.

Problem 7.59

Which substance is a weak electrolyte?

A. KNO3
B. H3PO4
C. HClO4
D. C6H12O6

Hint

Use the step-by-step classification: H in front marks an acid. Is that acid on the green-sheet strong-acid list?

Answer

B. H in front marks an acid. Phosphoric acid is not on the green sheet's list of strong acids, so it is a weak acid and a weak electrolyte: only a small fraction of its molecules form ions.

  • A is ionic (K+ and NO3−) and soluble (AANA), so it is a strong electrolyte.
  • C, perchloric acid, is on the strong-acid list, so it is a strong electrolyte.

Problem 7.60

Solutions of chromium(III) nitrate and potassium hydroxide are mixed. Follow the full routine to write the molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations.

Hint

Follow the full routine: names to formulas, swap-drop, balance (zig-zag), label phases, split the aqueous strong electrolytes, and cancel the spectators.

Answer

Formulas: Cr3+ and NO3− give Cr(NO3)3; K+ and OH− give KOH. Double displacement: outside ions Cr3+ and OH− give Cr(OH)3; inside ions give KNO3. Balance by zig-zag: 3 NO3 on the left, so 3 KNO3; zig to K, so 3 KOH; OH is then 3 = 3. Phases: Cr(OH)3 is insoluble (hydroxide, and Cr3+ is not an exception); KNO3 is soluble.

Molecular: Cr(NO3)3(aq) + 3 KOH(aq) → Cr(OH)3(s) + 3 KNO3(aq)

Complete ionic: Cr3+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq) + 3 K+(aq) + 3 OH−(aq) → Cr(OH)3(s) + 3 K+(aq) + 3 NO3−(aq)

Net ionic (cancel 3 K+ and 3 NO3−): Cr3+(aq) + 3 OH−(aq) → Cr(OH)3(s)

Check: charge +3 + 3(−1) = 0 on the left, 0 on the right.

Problem 7.61

Perchloric acid, HClO4(aq), is added to sodium bisulfite solution, NaHSO3(aq). Bubbles form. Write the molecular and net ionic equations, and name the gas.

Hint

Swap partners, then check the unstable-products table for H2SO3. In the net ionic equation, keep the bisulfite ion whole.

Answer

Swap partners: NaClO4 (sodium perchlorate) and H2SO3. Balance first (it already is), then replace H2SO3; by subtraction, H2SO3 − H2O = SO2:

HClO4(aq) + NaHSO3(aq) → NaClO4(aq) + H2O(l) + SO2(g)

Complete ionic: H+(aq) + ClO4−(aq) + Na+(aq) + HSO3−(aq) → Na+(aq) + ClO4−(aq) + H2O(l) + SO2(g). Cancel Na+ and ClO4−:

H+(aq) + HSO3−(aq) → H2O(l) + SO2(g)

The gas is sulfur dioxide. Bisulfite stays whole in the net ionic equation because it is a polyatomic ion.

Problem 7.62

Aluminum's protective oxide layer forms by 4 Al(s) + 3 O2(g) → 2 Al2O3(s). (a) Is this reaction redox? (b) Write the oxidation and reduction half-reactions. (c) How many electrons are transferred in the balanced equation? (d) What is the oxidation number of H in lithium hydride, LiH?

Hint

(a) Do any oxidation numbers change from the elements? (b) Al loses electrons; O2 gains them. (c) Find the least common multiple. (d) Which rule comes first for Li and H?

Answer

(a) Yes. It is a combination in which free elements (oxidation number 0) end up in a compound, so oxidation numbers change: Al 0 → +3 (by reverse swap-drop: 3 O2− give 6−, shared by 2 Al) and O 0 → −2.

(b) Oxidation: Al → Al3+ + 3 e−. Reduction: O2 + 4 e− → 2 O2−.

(c) The least common multiple of 3 and 4 is 12: 4 Al lose 12 electrons and 3 O2 gain 12. 12 electrons are transferred.

(d) The Group 1 rule comes before the hydrogen rule, so Li is +1 and H is −1.

Problem 7.63

Which equation is correctly balanced?

A. Zn(s) + Ag+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Ag(s)
B. Zn(s) + 2 Ag+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + 2 Ag(s)
C. 2 Zn(s) + Ag+(aq) → 2 Zn2+(aq) + Ag(s)
D. Zn(s) + Ag2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Ag(s)

Hint

Check both atoms and charge for each choice. How many electrons does Zn lose, and how many does each Ag+ gain?

Answer

B. Zinc loses 2 electrons (Zn → Zn2+ + 2 e−) and each silver ion gains 1 (Ag+ + e− → Ag), so two silver ions are needed. Atoms balance (Zn 1, Ag 2), and charge balances: +2 on each side.

  • A balances the atoms but not the charge: +1 on the left, +2 on the right. Electrons cannot just appear or vanish.
  • C puts the coefficient on the wrong species. The atoms balance, but the charge gets worse: +1 on the left, +4 on the right.
  • D changes the silver ion into Ag2+ to force the charges to match. Like changing a subscript, changing an ion's charge changes the substance; silver ions are Ag+.
