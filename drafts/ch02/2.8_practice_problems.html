2.1 Atomic Theory

Problem 2.1

Which idea from Dalton's atomic theory was later shown to be incorrect?

A. Atoms combine in simple, whole-number ratios to form compounds.
B. All atoms of a given element have the same mass.
C. In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed.
D. Each element is made of tiny particles called atoms.

Hint

Section 2.5 shows that atoms of one element can have different numbers of neutrons. Which statement does that discovery contradict?

Answer

B. Atoms of the same element always have the same number of protons, but they can have different numbers of neutrons, so they can have different masses. These versions of an element are isotopes (Section 2.5).

  • A still holds: water is always 2 H atoms to 1 O atom. This idea explains the law of definite proportions.
  • C still holds for chemical reactions, and it explains the law of conservation of mass. (Nuclear changes, in Chapter 14, are a different kind of change.)
  • D is the heart of the theory and has held up for two centuries.

Hint

(a) Percent Mg = mass of Mg ÷ (mass of Mg + mass of O) × 100%. (b) Compare the two percents. (c) Use the same oxygen-to-magnesium mass ratio.

(a) Percent magnesium = mass of Mg ÷ total mass × 100%.

Problem 2.3

Carbon and oxygen form two different colorless gases. In a 7.00 g sample of Gas A, 3.00 g is carbon and 4.00 g is oxygen. In an 11.00 g sample of Gas B, 3.00 g is carbon and 8.00 g is oxygen. (a) Could A and B be the same compound? Explain. (b) How does Dalton's idea of whole-number ratios explain the relationship between them?

Hint

(a) For each gas, divide grams of oxygen by grams of carbon. (b) For the same 3.00 g of carbon, compare the two oxygen masses: what simple ratio do you get?

Answer

(a) No. Compare the grams of oxygen per gram of carbon. Gas A: 4.00 g ÷ 3.00 g = 1.33 g O per g C. Gas B: 8.00 g ÷ 3.00 g = 2.67 g O per g C. A single compound always has the same ratio by mass, so two different ratios mean two different compounds.

(b) For the same mass of carbon, Gas B has exactly twice the oxygen (8.00 g ÷ 4.00 g = 2.00). That fits atoms combining in whole-number ratios: if Gas A has one oxygen atom per carbon atom, Gas B has two. (These are carbon monoxide, CO, and carbon dioxide, CO2.)

Problem 2.4

In Thomson's cathode-ray tube, the glowing beam bent toward a positively charged plate. What did this show?

A. The beam was made of negatively charged particles.
B. The beam was made of positively charged particles.
C. Atoms have a small, dense nucleus.
D. The beam was made of neutral particles.

Hint

Opposite charges attract (Section 1.7). What charge must the beam carry to be pulled toward a positive plate?

Answer

A. Opposite charges attract (Section 1.7), so a beam pulled toward a positive plate must carry negative charge. Those particles are electrons.

  • B is backward: positive particles would be pushed away from a positive plate.
  • C is the conclusion of Rutherford's gold foil experiment, not Thomson's cathode-ray experiment.
  • D cannot be right: neutral particles are not pulled by either plate, so the beam would go straight.

Problem 2.6

Match each scientist with the evidence and the conclusion. Scientists: Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Chadwick.

Evidence: (P) A few alpha particles bounced back from a gold foil. (Q) A neutral particle about as heavy as a proton was detected in 1932. (R) Elements combine in fixed mass ratios. (S) A beam in a high-voltage tube bent toward a positive plate.

Conclusions: (1) Atoms contain negatively charged electrons. (2) Atoms combine in whole-number ratios. (3) The nucleus also contains neutrons. (4) Atoms have a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.

Hint

Match each piece of evidence to its experiment (mass ratios, high-voltage tube, gold foil, a neutral particle), then ask what new idea that evidence gave.

Answer

Dalton: R and 2. Fixed mass ratios in compounds led to atoms combining in whole-number ratios.

Thomson: S and 1. The cathode-ray beam was negative, so atoms contain electrons (plum pudding model).

Rutherford: P and 4. Bounced-back alpha particles revealed the nucleus.

Chadwick: Q and 3. The neutron explained why nuclei weigh more than their protons alone.

Each model was replaced because new evidence did not fit the old one. That is the scientific method at work, not a sign that the earlier scientists did bad science.

Problem 2.7

Which statement describes the nuclear model of the atom?

A. Electrons sit inside the nucleus with the protons.
B. The nucleus holds almost all of the atom's mass but takes up only a tiny fraction of its volume.
C. Positive charge is spread evenly through the whole atom.
D. The nucleus fills most of the atom, and electrons are packed tightly against it.

Hint

Recall the nuclear model in three sentences: where is the mass, and how much of the volume does the nucleus fill? One choice is the plum pudding model.

Answer

B. Protons and neutrons, the heavy particles, are in the nucleus, and the nucleus is roughly 1/100,000 of the atom's diameter. The rest of the atom is mostly empty space where the electrons move.

  • A is wrong: electrons are outside the nucleus, in the electron cloud.
  • C describes Thomson's plum pudding model, which the gold foil experiment ruled out.
  • D is wrong on size: if the nucleus filled most of the atom, most alpha particles would have been deflected, not 1 in 8000.

Problem 2.11

A neutral carbon atom has 6 protons, 6 neutrons, and 6 electrons. Using the particle masses in Section 2.2 (proton 1.0073 amu, neutron 1.0087 amu, electron 0.00055 amu), calculate (a) the total mass of the 6 electrons, (b) the total mass of all 18 particles, and (c) the percent of the total that comes from electrons.

Hint

Multiply each particle's mass by how many there are (counts are exact), add the three totals, then find electron mass ÷ total × 100%.

Answer

(a) 6 × 0.00055 amu = 0.0033 amu (the 6 is a count, so it is exact; 0.00055 has two significant figures).

(b) Protons: 6 × 1.0073 = 6.0438 amu. Neutrons: 6 × 1.0087 = 6.0522 amu. Total: 6.0438 + 6.0522 + 0.0033 = 12.0993 amu.

(c) \( \dfrac{0.0033\ \text{amu}}{12.0993\ \text{amu}} \times 100\% = 0.027\% \)

Only about 0.027% of the atom's mass comes from electrons (two significant figures, set by 0.0033). That is why almost all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus, and why electrons are left out of the mass number.

Problem 2.12

(a) Using the masses in amu (proton 1.0073 amu, electron 0.00055 amu), how many times heavier is a proton than an electron? (b) Repeat the calculation with the masses in grams (proton 1.6726 × 10−24 g, electron 9.109 × 10−28 g). (c) Why do the two answers have different numbers of significant figures?

Hint

(a) (b) Divide the proton mass by the electron mass, in each unit. (c) Count the significant figures in each electron mass you divided by.

Answer

(a) \( \dfrac{1.0073\ \text{amu}}{0.00055\ \text{amu}} = 1831 \rightarrow 1800 \) (two significant figures). Written as 1800 without a decimal point, the zeros are not significant, so it correctly shows two significant figures; 1.8 × 103 says the same thing.

(b) \( \dfrac{1.6726 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{g}}{9.109 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{g}} = 1836.2 \rightarrow 1836 \) (four significant figures).

(c) In a division, the answer keeps the smaller number of significant figures. In (a), 0.00055 has only two (the leading zeros do not count). In (b), 9.109 has four. Both answers say the same thing: a proton is about 1800 times heavier than an electron.

(b) Europium-153: 52.17%. Europium-151: 100% − 52.17% = 47.83%. (Each subtraction leaves four significant figures, so the fraction has four. The accepted values, 52.19% and 47.81%, differ slightly because the masses here are rounded.)

(c) 151.964 is below 152, so the shortcut points to europium-151, which is wrong. The real midpoint of the two isotope masses is (150.920 + 152.921) ÷ 2 = 151.921 amu, and the average, 151.964, is above that, so europium-153 is the slight majority. When two isotopes are nearly equally abundant, use the real masses, not the mass numbers.

Problem 2.54

The periodic table lists 85.47 amu for rubidium, which has two natural isotopes, rubidium-85 and rubidium-87. Which statement is correct?

A. Every rubidium atom has a mass of 85.47 amu.
B. No single rubidium atom has a mass of 85.47 amu; the value is a weighted average of the masses of rubidium-85 and rubidium-87 atoms.
C. 85.47 is the mass number of rubidium.
D. Natural rubidium is half rubidium-85 and half rubidium-87.

Hint

Each rubidium atom is either rubidium-85 or rubidium-87. Can one atom have a mass between them? Is a mass number ever a decimal?

Answer

B. Each atom is either rubidium-85 (about 84.91 amu) or rubidium-87 (about 86.91 amu). 85.47 describes the natural mixture, not any one atom.

  • A is the most common misunderstanding: the average describes the mixture.
  • C is wrong: a mass number counts protons plus neutrons, so it is always a whole number and belongs to one isotope.
  • D would give an average near 86. The real average, 85.47, is much closer to 85, so rubidium-85 is the large majority (about 72%).

Problem 2.55

Gold has only one natural isotope, gold-197, with a mass of 196.97 amu. (a) Use 1 amu = 1.6605 × 10−24 g to find the mass of one gold atom in grams. (b) How many gold atoms are in a 1.000 g gold bead? (c) How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in one gold-197 atom?

Hint

(a) Multiply amu by the grams-per-amu factor. (b) Use the mass of one atom as a conversion factor (1 atom per that many grams). (c) Z, then A − Z.

Answer

(a) \( 196.97\ \text{amu} \times \dfrac{1.6605 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{amu}} = 3.2707 \times 10^{-22}\ \text{g} \)

Both numbers have five significant figures, so the answer is 3.2707 × 10−22 g.

(b) Use the mass of one atom as a conversion factor:

\( 1.000\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{atom}}{3.2707 \times 10^{-22}\ \text{g}} = 3.057 \times 10^{21}\ \text{atoms} \)

The answer is 3.057 × 1021 atoms (four significant figures, set by 1.000 g). Calculator tip (TI-36X Pro): enter 3.2707 [×10n] [(−)] 22 for the bottom number. Atoms are so small that even a small bead holds trillions of billions of them. Chapter 5 turns this kind of count into the mole.

(c) Gold, Au: Z = 79, so 79 protons and 79 electrons; 197 − 79 = 118 neutrons. Because gold has only one natural isotope, its average atomic mass is simply the mass of that isotope.

Problem 2.56

Classify each substance as an element, a compound, or a mixture (homogeneous or heterogeneous), and say whether it is made of neutral atoms, molecules, or ions (or a combination).

(a) sodium metal
(b) chlorine gas, Cl2
(c) pure sodium chloride (table salt)
(d) normal saline (0.90% sodium chloride dissolved in water)

Hint

For each, ask: one kind of atom or more? Chemically joined in a fixed ratio? Made of ions, molecules, or neutral atoms? If a mixture, is it uniform?

Answer

(a) Element, made of sodium atoms. Sodium metal reacts violently with water.

(b) Element, made of Cl2 molecules (two chlorine atoms joined). Chlorine is one of the diatomic seven, and the gas is toxic.

(c) Compound, made of Na+ and Cl− ions in a fixed 1:1 ratio. The ions behave completely differently from the atoms in (a) and the molecules in (b): table salt is safe to eat.

(d) Homogeneous mixture: water molecules with Na+ and Cl− ions spread evenly through them. The proportions could be changed, which makes it a mixture rather than a compound.

Only (c) and (d) contain sodium ions, the form of sodium measured on a lab panel.

Problem 2.57

The nucleus of an atom is roughly 1/100,000 of the atom's diameter. Imagine a model in which the nucleus is a marble 1.0 cm across. (a) About how wide would the whole atom be, in meters and in kilometers? (b) What does this model say about the space that the electrons occupy?

Hint

(a) Multiply the marble's width by 100,000, then convert cm to m and m to km. (b) Compare the nucleus's size with the space around it.

Answer

(a) The atom is about 100,000 (105) times wider than the nucleus:

\( 1.0\ \text{cm} \times 10^{5} = 1.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{cm} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{m}}{100\ \text{cm}} = 1.0 \times 10^{3}\ \text{m} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{km}}{1000\ \text{m}} = 1.0\ \text{km} \)

The atom would be about 1.0 × 103 m, or 1.0 km, across, roughly ten football fields in a row. The ratio itself is approximate, so treat this as an estimate.

(b) The electrons move through a space enormously larger than the nucleus, and nearly all of it is empty. That is why 99% of the alpha particles in the gold foil experiment passed through untouched (see the gold foil simulation in the Section 2.1 problems).

Problem 2.58

Mateo's chloride result is 102 mmol/L. Natural chlorine is 75.77% chlorine-35 and 24.23% chlorine-37 (Section 2.6), and the chloride ions in his blood have the same mix. (a) Give the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in a chlorine-35 chloride ion and in a chlorine-37 chloride ion. (b) Of the 102 mmol/L, how much is chloride made from chlorine-37? (c) Does the lab need to report the two isotopes separately to describe how chloride behaves in his body? Explain.

Hint

(a) Chloride is 1− with Z = 17; neutrons = A − Z. (b) Take 24.23% of 102 mmol/L. (c) Does chemistry depend on neutrons or on electrons?

Answer

(a) Both have Z = 17 and a 1− charge: 17 protons and 18 electrons. Chlorine-35 chloride has 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons; chlorine-37 chloride has 37 − 17 = 20 neutrons.

(b) \( 102\ \text{mmol/L} \times \dfrac{24.23}{100} = 24.7\ \text{mmol/L} \)

About 24.7 mmol/L (three significant figures, set by 102) is chloride made from chlorine-37.

(c) No. Both isotopes form chloride ions with the same 18 electrons, so they behave the same way chemically in the blood. The lab reports total chloride; the isotopes differ only in mass.

Problem 2.59

Consider three particles: \( ^{40}_{18}\text{Ar} \), \( ^{40}_{19}\text{K}^{+} \), and \( ^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}^{2+} \). (a) Give the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in each. (b) What do all three have in common? (c) Are any two of them isotopes of each other? Explain.

Hint

Use protons = Z, neutrons = A − Z, and electrons = protons − charge for each. Remember that isotopes must have the same number of protons.

Answer

(a) Ar: 18 protons, 40 − 18 = 22 neutrons, 18 electrons. K+: 19 protons, 21 neutrons, 19 − 1 = 18 electrons. Ca2+: 20 protons, 20 neutrons, 20 − 2 = 18 electrons.

(b) All three have the same mass number (40) and the same number of electrons (18).

(c) No. Isotopes must have the same number of protons, and these have 18, 19, and 20: three different elements. Sharing a mass number or an electron count does not make particles the same element. Only the proton count does.

Problem 2.60

When a sulfur atom gains two electrons, the particle that forms is

A. a cation with a 2+ charge
B. an anion with a 2− charge
C. an anion with a 2+ charge
D. sulfur-34, a heavier isotope of sulfur

Hint

Use charge = protons − electrons for sulfur (Z = 16) after it gains two electrons. Does gaining electrons change the mass number?

Answer

B. Gaining two negative electrons gives 16 protons and 18 electrons: charge = 16 − 18 = 2−. A negative ion is an anion (onions make you cry: anions are the negative ones). The ion is S2−, sulfide.

  • A gets the sign backward. Cations (cats have paws: "paws-itive") form when electrons are lost.
  • C mixes the two: an anion is always negative.
  • D confuses electrons with neutrons. Electrons have almost no mass and do not change the mass number; adding neutrons makes an isotope.

Problem 2.61

Which set shows the correct fixed charges of aluminum, zinc, and silver?

A. Al3+, Zn2+, Ag+
B. Al3+, Zn+, Ag2+
C. Al+, Zn2+, Ag3+
D. Al2+, Zn2+, Ag+

Hint

Use "the steps" from 2.4: start at aluminum on the top step and walk down the diagonal, one charge lower each step.

Answer

A. Walk down the steps: aluminum (top step) 3+, zinc 2+, silver 1+.

  • B swaps zinc and silver.
  • C walks the steps in the wrong direction: start at aluminum with 3+.
  • D gives aluminum 2+; aluminum always forms Al3+.

Problem 2.62

Potassium (Z = 19) comes right after argon (Z = 18), but on the periodic table it starts a new row, directly under sodium. Why?

A. Potassium is heavier than argon, and heavier elements always start new rows.
B. Potassium behaves like sodium (a soft metal that reacts violently with water), and the table stacks elements with similar properties in the same column.
C. Mendeleev arranged the elements alphabetically by their Latin names (kalium, natrium).
D. Every row of the periodic table has exactly eight elements.

Hint

Mendeleev started a new row whenever properties repeated. How does potassium behave compared with sodium?

Answer

B. The table is ordered by atomic number, but a new row starts whenever properties repeat, so that each column holds a family of similar elements. Potassium is an alkali metal like lithium and sodium, so it lands in group 1.

  • A: every element is heavier than the one before it (with a few exceptions), but that alone does not decide where rows break.
  • C: the order is by atomic number, not by name; the Latin names only explain the symbols K and Na.
  • D: rows have 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, and 32 elements; only periods 2 and 3 have eight.

Problem 2.64

A student reads the iron tile (26, Fe, 55.85) and writes: "Fe3+ has 23 protons, 23 electrons, and 55.85 neutrons." (a) Find each error. (b) Give the correct counts for an Fe3+ ion made from iron-56.

Hint

(a) Which count never changes when an ion forms? What is the decimal on the tile? (b) Protons = Z, neutrons = A − Z, electrons = protons − charge.

Answer

(a) Three errors. The proton count never changes when an ion forms, so Fe3+ still has 26 protons; the charge comes from losing electrons. The electrons should be 26 − 3, not 23 protons and 23 electrons (that would be neutral). And 55.85 is an average atomic mass, not a count; particles come in whole numbers.

(b) Iron-56: 26 protons, 56 − 26 = 30 neutrons, 26 − 3 = 23 electrons.

Problem 2.65

Silicon, the element in computer chips and in the quartz of Sierra granite, has three natural isotopes. Silicon-28 (27.977 amu) is 92.23% of silicon atoms and silicon-29 (28.976 amu) is 4.68%. The rest is silicon-30 (29.974 amu). (a) What is the percent abundance of silicon-30?

A. 3.09%
B. 7.77%
C. 96.91%
D. 33.3%

Then (b) calculate silicon's average atomic mass.

Hint

(a) The three percents must add to 100%: subtract both given values. (b) Change each percent to a decimal, multiply by its mass, and add all three.

Answer

(a) A. The three percents must add to 100%: 100.00% − 92.23% − 4.68% = 3.09%.

  • B subtracted only silicon-28 (100.00 − 92.23) and forgot silicon-29.
  • C added the two given percents instead of subtracting them from 100%.
  • D assumes the three isotopes are equally common; the problem says they are not.

(b) Change each percent to a decimal and keep each product in parentheses on the calculator:

\( (0.9223)(27.977) + (0.0468)(28.976) + (0.0309)(29.974) = 25.803 + 1.3561 + 0.92620 = 28.09\ \text{amu} \)

The answer is 28.09 amu, rounded to the hundredths place (the last place known in the 1.36 and 25.80 terms). It is very close to 28 because silicon-28 is the large majority, and it matches the yellow-sheet value.

Problem 2.66

A made-up element has two isotopes: 120.0 amu (25.0% of atoms) and 124.0 amu (75.0% of atoms). (a) What is the plain average (mean) of the two masses? (b) What is the weighted average atomic mass? (c) Which one belongs on the periodic table, and why?

Hint

(a) Add the masses and divide by 2. (b) Multiply each fraction by its mass and add. (c) Which average counts how common each isotope is?

Answer

(a) (120.0 + 124.0) ÷ 2 = 122.0 amu.

(b) \( (0.250)(120.0\ \text{amu}) + (0.750)(124.0\ \text{amu}) = 30.0 + 93.0 = 123.0\ \text{amu} \)

(c) The weighted average, 123.0 amu. Three of every four atoms are the heavier isotope, so the average is pulled toward 124.0. The plain mean would be right only if the two isotopes were equally common.

Problem 2.67

Many home smoke detectors contain a tiny amount of americium-241. (a) How many protons and neutrons are in an americium-241 nucleus? (b) In the detector, the americium is present as americium(IV) oxide, which contains Am4+ ions. How many electrons does one Am4+ ion have? (c) Is Am4+ a cation or an anion?

Hint

(a) Find americium's Z; neutrons = A − Z. (b) Electrons = protons − charge works for any element. (c) Cats have paws.

Answer

(a) Americium has Z = 95: 95 protons and 241 − 95 = 146 neutrons.

(b) 95 − 4 = 91 electrons. The charge was given, so you do not need to know whether americium "usually" forms this ion; charge = protons − electrons works for any element.

(c) A cation: it is positive because it lost electrons (cats have paws).
