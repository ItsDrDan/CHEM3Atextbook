Patient Case: Meet Mateo

Mateo Herrera is 34 and works as an irrigation technician on an almond orchard in western Fresno County. He has had asthma since childhood, and he knows the pattern: on hot summer afternoons when the Valley's ozone is high, or during a week of wildfire smoke, his chest tightens and his rescue inhaler gets a workout. This year a bad smoke week sends him to the emergency department.

The first number the team looks at is not on a monitor; it is on a lab report. His basic metabolic panel, a routine blood test, lists sodium 138, potassium 3.9, and chloride 102, each in millimoles per liter (mmol/L). Those three values describe ions: atoms that have gained or lost electrons. In this chapter you will learn what atoms are made of, how they turn into ions, and why two atoms of the same element can have different masses. Mateo's story continues in the chapters where chemistry and his care meet.

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Describe how the model of the atom developed from Dalton's atomic theory to the nuclear atom (Section 2.1).
  • Give the charge, relative mass, and location of protons, neutrons, and electrons, and use the atomic number (Section 2.2).
  • Find elements by symbol, group, and period, and classify them as metals, nonmetals, or metalloids (Section 2.3).
  • Explain how ions form and count the protons and electrons in an ion (Section 2.4).
  • Write and read isotope notation and count the protons, neutrons, and electrons in an isotope (Section 2.5).
  • Calculate an element's average atomic mass from isotope masses and abundances (Section 2.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Every electrolyte value on a lab report, every radioactive tracer in nuclear medicine, every fluoride ion in toothpaste, and every iron atom in hemoglobin starts with the same three particles: protons, neutrons, and electrons. Once you can count them, you can explain why sodium and chloride ions behave so differently from sodium and chlorine atoms, why some isotopes are useful in medicine and others are dangerous, and where the numbers on the periodic table come from. Chapter 3 then looks at how electrons are arranged, and Chapter 4 uses ions to build and name compounds.
