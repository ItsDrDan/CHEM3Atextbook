  • Describe what Thomson's cathode-ray experiment and Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed.
  • Describe the nuclear model of the atom, state its three main points, and compare the sizes of the atom and the nucleus.
  • Explain why scientific models of the atom changed over time.

Nobody has ever looked at an atom the way you look at a grain of sand. Our picture of the atom was built from indirect evidence: masses measured on balances, beams bent by magnets, particles bouncing off foil. Each experiment tested the model of its day, and when the evidence did not fit, the model changed. This is the scientific method of Section 1.1 at work, and it is a good reminder that a theory is the best explanation so far.

Dalton's Atomic Theory

Ancient Greek philosophers guessed that matter was made of tiny, uncuttable pieces (atomos means "indivisible"), but they had no experiments. In the early 1800s the English schoolteacher John Dalton turned the idea into a scientific theory backed by measurements. Its main points:

  1. Each element is made of tiny particles called atoms.
  2. All atoms of a given element are alike, and they differ from the atoms of every other element.
  3. Atoms combine in simple, whole-number ratios to form compounds.
  4. In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged; they are not created, destroyed, or changed into atoms of another element.

Point 3 explains the law of definite proportions (also called the law of constant composition): a compound always contains the same elements in the same proportions by mass, whatever its source. Water from a Sierra stream and water made in a lab are both 2 hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom, so both are about 11% hydrogen and 89% oxygen by mass. Point 4 explains the law of conservation of mass you met in Section 1.1.

Points 2 and 3 are easier to remember with a picture. For point 2, compare sodium and silver. Both are shiny, silvery metals, and side by side they look a lot alike. Drop a piece of sodium into water and it fizzes, skates across the surface, and can burst into flame; drop silver into water and nothing happens at all. They look the same, but they are made of different atoms, and atoms of different elements have different properties. For point 3, think of water. Every water molecule has 2 hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom, H2O. A particle with any other ratio of hydrogen to oxygen would not be water; it would be a different substance. The carbon dioxide you breathe out is the same story: always 1 carbon to 2 oxygens, CO2.

Dalton was not right about everything. Atoms can be broken into smaller particles, and, as Section 2.5 shows, atoms of the same element do not all have the same mass. But his central idea, that matter is made of atoms that combine in fixed ratios, has held up for two centuries. Dalton's theory also leaves a question open: if every element is made of atoms, where do the differences between atoms of different elements come from? Answering that took a new theory, built on the discovery that atoms have parts.

Inside the Atom: Thomson and Rutherford

The electron

In 1897 J. J. Thomson studied cathode rays, the glowing beams that form in a sealed tube when a high voltage is applied. The same kind of tube once made the picture in old box-shaped televisions, and the glow of a neon sign outside a store comes from a related gas-filled tube. Thomson sent the beam between two charged plates, one positive and one negative. The beam bent toward a positively charged plate, so it had to be made of negatively charged particles, and those particles were the same no matter which metal the tube was made of. Thomson had found the electron, a particle far lighter than any atom. Because atoms are electrically neutral, he reasoned that each atom must also contain positive charge to balance the electrons. He pictured the atom as a sphere of spread-out positive charge with electrons scattered through it, the "plum pudding" model.

A few years later, in 1909, Robert Millikan measured the electron’s charge. He sprayed tiny oil drops between two charged metal plates, gave them an electric charge, and adjusted the voltage until a drop hung still. Every drop carried a whole-number multiple of the same small charge, 1.6 × 10−19 C: the charge of one electron.

The nucleus

Around 1909 Ernest Rutherford's team tested that model by firing alpha particles (small, fast, positively charged particles) at a gold foil only about a thousand atoms thick (Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\)). If positive charge were spread thinly through each atom, every alpha particle should have passed straight through. Most did. But about 1 in 8000 bounced off at a large angle, some almost straight back.

Rutherford concluded that all of an atom's positive charge and almost all of its mass are packed into a tiny center, the nucleus, and that the rest of the atom is mostly empty space where the electrons move. An alpha particle bounces back only on the rare occasion it heads almost straight at a nucleus; like charges repel (Section 1.7), and the nucleus is far heavier than the alpha particle. This is the nuclear model of the atom (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)).

Nuclear atomic theory in three sentences

Put the evidence together and you have nuclear atomic theory, the picture of the atom used in the rest of this course:

  1. Almost all of the atom's mass (about 99.9%) and all of its positive charge are in the nucleus, where the protons and neutrons are.
  2. Most of the atom's volume is empty space, through which the negatively charged electrons are spread.
  3. A neutral atom has as many electrons outside the nucleus as protons inside it, so the charges cancel.

The sizes show how empty an atom is. Atoms are measured in picometers (1 pm = 10−12 m). A hydrogen atom, one of the smallest, is about 100 pm across, and even the largest atoms are only a few hundred picometers across. The nucleus is about 0.001 pm across, roughly 100,000 times smaller than the atom around it. If the nucleus were a pea at the center of a football stadium, the electrons would be moving around somewhere out in the stands.

Professor Tip

On exams, connect each scientist to the evidence and the conclusion, not just a name: Dalton (mass ratios in compounds → atoms combine in whole-number ratios), Thomson (cathode rays bend toward + → electrons exist; plum pudding), Rutherford (a few alpha particles bounce back → tiny, dense, positive nucleus). Rutherford later named the proton, and in 1932 James Chadwick found the neutral neutron, which explained why nuclei weigh more than their protons alone.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Which result supports which model?

Problem. In the gold foil experiment, what would the plum pudding model predict, and which observation ruled it out?

Solution. In the plum pudding model, positive charge and mass are spread evenly through each atom, so nothing in the foil is concentrated enough to turn an alpha particle sharply. The model predicts that all alpha particles pass through with at most small deflections. The observation that ruled it out was the small fraction of alpha particles deflected at large angles or bounced back, which requires a small, massive, positive nucleus.

Check. A falsifiable prediction (all particles pass through) was tested and failed, so the model was revised. That is the scientific method from Section 1.1.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): The law of definite proportions

Problem. One sample of table salt from a Valley grocery store contains 3.93 g of sodium and 6.07 g of chlorine. A second sample, made in a lab, contains 7.86 g of sodium. If both are pure sodium chloride, how much chlorine is in the second sample?

Solution. A compound always has the same mass ratio of its elements. The second sample has twice the sodium (7.86 g ÷ 3.93 g = 2.00), so it must have twice the chlorine: 2.00 × 6.07 g = 12.1 g of chlorine.

Check. Percent sodium in sample 1: 3.93 ÷ (3.93 + 6.07) = 39.3%. Sample 2: 7.86 ÷ (7.86 + 12.14) = 39.3%. Same composition, as the law requires.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Rutherford's alpha particles are a form of ionizing radiation: radiation with enough energy to knock electrons off atoms. The same "mostly empty space" idea explains why X-rays and gamma rays can pass through the body at all: most of each atom is empty, so many photons travel through tissue without hitting anything. Dense tissue such as bone, with more and heavier atoms in the way, stops more of them. Alpha particles, by contrast, are large and slow by comparison and are stopped by a sheet of paper or the outer layer of dead skin. You will compare alpha, beta, and gamma radiation in Chapter 14.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking the nucleus is large. It holds almost all the mass but takes up a tiny fraction of the volume.
  • Mixing up the experiments. Cathode rays revealed the electron; the gold foil revealed the nucleus.
  • Thinking atoms that look alike are alike. Sodium and silver are both shiny silver metals, but they are different elements made of different atoms, and they behave very differently in water.
  • Thinking a replaced model was "wrong science." Each model explained the evidence of its time; better evidence led to a better model.

Summary

  • Dalton: elements are made of atoms; atoms combine in whole-number ratios and are rearranged, not destroyed, in reactions. Compounds have fixed composition (law of definite proportions).
  • Thomson found the negatively charged electron and proposed the plum pudding model.
  • Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space where electrons move.
  • Nuclear atomic theory: the nucleus holds about 99.9% of the mass and all the positive charge; most of the volume is empty space with electrons; a neutral atom has equal numbers of electrons and protons. A small atom is about 100 pm across; its nucleus is about 0.001 pm.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which part of Dalton's theory explains the law of conservation of mass?

Hint

Look at Dalton's point about what happens to atoms in a chemical reaction. If no atoms appear or vanish, what must be true of the total mass?

Answer

Atoms are rearranged in a chemical reaction but are not created or destroyed, so the total mass stays the same.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Why did most alpha particles pass straight through the gold foil?

Hint

Think about Rutherford's conclusion: where is nearly all of the atom's mass, and how much of the atom's volume does that region take up?

Answer

Atoms are mostly empty space; the nucleus is so small that most alpha particles never come close to one.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Carbon dioxide is always 27.3% carbon by mass. A 60.0 g sample of pure carbon dioxide contains how many grams of carbon?

Hint

Change the percent carbon to a decimal (divide by 100), then multiply by the sample mass. By the law of definite proportions, every sample has the same percent carbon.

Answer

27.3% of 60.0 g: 0.273 × 60.0 g = 16.4 g of carbon (law of definite proportions; 3 significant figures).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A student says, "Potassium and silver are both shiny gray metals, so they must be made of the same kind of atom." Use Dalton's second point to explain what is wrong, and name one observation that shows the difference.

Hint

Dalton's second point says atoms of different elements differ. Compare how the two metals behave, not how they look: what happens when each meets water?

Answer

Atoms of different elements differ from each other and give each element its own properties. Looking alike is not enough: potassium (an alkali metal, like sodium) reacts violently with water, while silver does not react with water at all. Different behavior means different atoms.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

A gold atom is about 290 pm across (2.9 × 102 pm), and its nucleus is about 0.014 pm across. About how many times wider is the atom than its nucleus?

Hint

Divide the atom's width by the nucleus's width; both are in pm, so the units cancel. Round to the significant figures of the given values.

Answer

2.9 × 102 pm ÷ 0.014 pm = 2.1 × 104, so the atom is about 21,000 times wider than its nucleus (2 significant figures). Even for a big atom with a big nucleus, almost all of the atom is empty space.
