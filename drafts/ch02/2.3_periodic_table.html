Learning Objectives

  • Write element symbols correctly, including symbols that come from Latin names, and know the names and symbols of the first 20 elements.
  • Explain why the periodic table has its shape: elements are ordered by atomic number and stacked by repeating properties (Mendeleev).
  • Locate an element by group (column) and period (row).
  • Classify an element as a metal, nonmetal, or metalloid, and as a main-group or transition element.
  • Name the alkali metals, alkaline earth metals, halogens, and noble gases, and recognize other group names.

The periodic table is the most useful single page in chemistry, and on your exams it is given to you. Learning to read it is like learning the alphabet of a new language: once you know where things are, you can predict how an element will behave before you ever see it in the lab.

Element Symbols

Each element has a one- or two-letter symbol. The first letter is always a capital; a second letter is always lowercase. That rule matters: Co is cobalt, an element, but CO is carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen.

Most symbols come from the English name (H, hydrogen; C, carbon; O, oxygen; Ca, calcium). A few come from older Latin names, and these are worth memorizing because they show up constantly, including in medicine.

Why the Table Has Its Odd Shape

If you only wanted to list the elements in order, you could write them in a neat rectangle: H, He, Li, Be, B, and so on, eight or ten to a row. The real periodic table looks nothing like that. It has a tall tower on the left, a taller one on the right, a sunken middle, and two rows parked underneath. The shape comes from chemistry, not from tidiness.

In 1869 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev wrote the known elements in order and noticed that their properties repeat. Lithium, sodium, and potassium are all soft metals that react violently with water. Beryllium and magnesium behave like each other, and chlorine, bromine, and iodine are all reactive nonmetals. So instead of continuing each row to the edge of a rectangle, he started a new row whenever the properties came back around, which stacked similar elements in the same column. Where the pattern called for an element no one had found yet, he left a gap and predicted its properties. When gallium (1875) and germanium (1886) were discovered, they matched his predictions closely, which convinced chemists that the pattern was real. The noble gases had not been discovered yet; when they were found in the 1890s, they fit neatly as a whole new column.

Mendeleev ordered the elements by atomic mass, because protons had not been discovered yet. Once atomic numbers were measured in the early 1900s, the table was reordered by atomic number, which fixed a few pairs that had been out of order by mass. The modern table therefore does two things at once: it lists elements in order of increasing atomic number and stacks them so that elements in the same column have similar properties. That is why the table is called periodic: properties repeat periodically, row after row. Chapter 3 shows the reason underneath, the arrangement of electrons.

Groups and Periods

The modern periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

  • A vertical column is a group (or family). Groups are numbered 1 to 18 from left to right. Some tables also use an older system, with "A" groups (1A–8A) for the main groups and "B" groups for the transition metals. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties, for reasons you will see in Chapter 3.
  • A horizontal row is a period. Periods are numbered 1 to 7 from top to bottom.
  • Main-group elements are in groups 1, 2, and 13–18, the two "towers" on the left and right. Their behavior is quite predictable from their position: this is where the predictable ion charges of Section 2.4 come from, and where most of the chemistry in this course happens. Transition metals fill groups 3–12 in the sunken middle. Their chemistry is less predictable (many can form more than one ion, which is why their names need Roman numerals in Chapter 4), and they are the metals in wiring, batteries, and electronics. The two rows set below the table, the lanthanides and actinides (the inner transition metals), belong in periods 6 and 7 but are pulled out so the table fits on a page.

Metals, Nonmetals, and Metalloids

A staircase line that starts at boron and steps down to the right divides the table.

  • Metals (left of the staircase; about three-quarters of all elements) are shiny, conduct heat and electricity well, and are malleable (can be hammered into sheets) and ductile (can be drawn into wires). All are solids at room temperature except mercury, a liquid. In reactions, metals tend to lose electrons (Section 2.4).
  • Nonmetals (upper right, plus hydrogen) are dull and brittle as solids, conduct poorly, and include several gases (such as N2, O2, F2, Cl2, and the noble gases) and one liquid, bromine. In reactions with metals they tend to gain electrons.
  • Metalloids (along the staircase: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, and usually At) have properties in between. Silicon and germanium are semiconductors, the basis of computer chips.

Professor Tip: Start With the First 20

Think of the periodic table as the chemist's alphabet: learn the "letters" (symbols), then the "families" (groups). You do not need to memorize the whole table at once. A good starting point is the first 20 elements, hydrogen through calcium, listed in the table below: put each name and symbol on a flashcard and drill them until they are automatic. They are worth the effort because they are the elements you will see most, and many are essential minerals in the body. A health-care worker will read K for potassium (as in a potassium deficiency) and Ca for calcium (bone strength) every day. Then add the Latin-based symbols above and any others your instructor assigns, and practice saying instantly whether an element is a metal, nonmetal, or metalloid.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Locate and classify

Problem. For each element, give the group, period, and classification (metal, nonmetal, or metalloid), and say whether it is a main-group or transition element: (a) S, (b) Ca, (c) Si, (d) Cu.

Solution. (a) Sulfur: group 16, period 3, nonmetal, main group (a chalcogen). (b) Calcium: group 2, period 4, metal, main group (an alkaline earth metal). (c) Silicon: group 14, period 3, metalloid, main group. (d) Copper: group 11, period 4, metal, transition element.

Check. Elements in groups 1, 2, and 13–18 are main-group; only copper, in group 11, falls in the 3–12 block.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Symbols that trip people up

Problem. Write the symbols for potassium, phosphorus, silver, and lead, and name the elements with symbols S, Sn, and Sb.

Solution. Potassium K (kalium), phosphorus P, silver Ag (argentum), lead Pb (plumbum). S is sulfur, Sn is tin (stannum), and Sb is antimony (stibium).

Check. Each two-letter symbol has a capital first letter and a lowercase second letter.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

The Latin symbol Sn for tin shows up in your toothpaste. Stannous fluoride contains tin (stannum, Sn) and fluorine, and it is used both to strengthen enamel against dental caries (tooth decay) and to reduce gingivitis (gum inflammation) and tooth sensitivity. Sodium fluoride contains sodium (natrium, Na). Both deliver fluorine to the tooth surface in the form of the fluoride ion, which you will meet in Section 2.4. Reading the active ingredient on the label is easier once you know the Latin roots: stannous means tin, ferrous means iron, cupric means copper.

Major Connection: Engineering

The metalloids are why your phone works. Pure silicon conducts electricity poorly, but adding tiny amounts of a group 13 or group 15 element (a process called doping) turns it into a controllable semiconductor. Engineers choose the dopant by its position on the periodic table: a group 15 atom such as phosphorus brings one more valence electron than silicon, and a group 13 atom such as boron brings one fewer.

Common Mistakes

  • Capitalizing the second letter. CO is a compound; Co is cobalt.
  • Calling hydrogen an alkali metal. It sits above group 1 but is a nonmetal.
  • Mixing up groups and periods. Groups go down (columns); periods go across (rows).
  • Thinking the table is just a numbered list. It is ordered by atomic number, but its rows break so that elements with similar properties line up in columns.
  • Guessing Latin symbols. K is potassium (not P, which is phosphorus); S is sulfur, Sn tin, Sb antimony.

Summary

  • Element symbols have a capital first letter; several come from Latin (Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Sn, Au, Hg, Pb, Sb).
  • The table is ordered by atomic number and stacked by repeating properties (Mendeleev), so groups are columns (1–18) of elements with similar properties; periods are rows (1–7).
  • Main-group elements (groups 1, 2, 13–18) are predictable; transition metals (3–12) are less so.
  • Metals are on the left and center, nonmetals on the upper right, metalloids along the staircase.
  • Know the alkali metals (1), alkaline earth metals (2), halogens (17), and noble gases (18).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Name the element in group 2, period 3, and classify it.

Hint

Count to the second column and down to the third row. Which named family is group 2?

Answer

Magnesium, Mg: an alkaline earth metal (main group).

Group 2 is the alkaline earth metals, and magnesium sits in period 3 of that column.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which of these are halogens: Cl, Ca, I, Ar, F?

Hint

Halogens are the group 17 family. Find each element's column on the periodic table.

Answer

Cl, I, and F (group 17). Ca is an alkaline earth metal; Ar is a noble gas.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A patient's chart says "hypokalemia." Which element is low?

Hint

Break the word apart: hypo- means low and -emia means blood. The middle root is a Latin element name; check the Latin-name table in 2.3.

Answer

Potassium (kalium, K). Hypo- means low and -emia refers to the blood.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Fluorine (Z = 9) and chlorine (Z = 17) are not next to each other in atomic number, yet they sit in the same column. Why?

Hint

Mendeleev stacked elements by repeating properties. How do fluorine and chlorine behave alike, and where must a new row start to put them in one column?

Answer

The table is stacked by repeating properties. Fluorine and chlorine are both very reactive nonmetals that form 1− ions and exist as diatomic molecules, so a new row starts after neon and chlorine lands under fluorine, in group 17 (the halogens).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Without looking: write the symbols for magnesium, sulfur, potassium, and silicon, and name the elements Ne, B, Al, and Cl.

Hint

The first letter of a symbol is a capital and any second letter is lowercase. One element here has a symbol from its Latin name.

Answer

Mg, S, K, Si; neon, boron, aluminum, chlorine.

These are among the first 20 elements; potassium's K comes from its Latin name, kalium, and the others come from their English names.
