Learning Objectives

  • Explain how an atom becomes a cation or an anion.
  • Count the protons and electrons in a monatomic ion, and find the charge from those counts.
  • Predict the usual charge of the ion formed by a main-group element from its group.
  • Name simple monatomic ions.

When Mateo's lab report lists sodium, potassium, and chloride, it is not describing atoms of sodium metal or chlorine gas. Sodium metal reacts violently with water, and chlorine gas is toxic, yet the sodium and chloride in his blood are harmless and essential. The difference is a few electrons. Atoms that gain or lose electrons become ions, and ions behave completely differently from the atoms they came from.

Cations and Anions

An ion is an atom (or group of atoms) with an overall electric charge because its numbers of protons and electrons are not equal. The number of protons never changes when an ion forms; only electrons are gained or lost (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

  • A cation is a positive ion. It forms when an atom loses electrons, so protons outnumber electrons. Example: a sodium atom (11 p+, 11 e−) loses one electron to become Na+ (11 p+, 10 e−). Metals usually form cations.
  • An anion is a negative ion. It forms when an atom gains electrons, so electrons outnumber protons. Example: a chlorine atom (17 p+, 17 e−) gains one electron to become Cl− (17 p+, 18 e−). Nonmetals usually form anions.

The charge is written as a superscript after the symbol, number first and sign second: Ca2+, O2−. A charge of one is written with the sign alone: Na+, Cl−.

charge = (number of protons) − (number of electrons)

Professor Tip

Electrons are negative, so losing them makes an ion more positive and gaining them makes it more negative. If that feels backward, think of it as losing a debt: lose a negative and your balance goes up. Then use the formula: charge = protons − electrons.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Count the particles in an ion

Problem. How many protons and electrons are in (a) K+, (b) S2−, (c) Al3+, (d) N3−?

Solution. Protons come from the atomic number. Electrons = protons − charge.

  1. K: Z = 19, so 19 protons; charge 1+, so 19 − 1 = 18 electrons.
  2. S: Z = 16, so 16 protons; charge 2−, so 16 + 2 = 18 electrons.
  3. Al: Z = 13, so 13 protons; charge 3+, so 13 − 3 = 10 electrons.
  4. N: Z = 7, so 7 protons; charge 3−, so 7 + 3 = 10 electrons.

Check. Protons − electrons should equal the charge: 19 − 18 = +1, 16 − 18 = −2, 13 − 10 = +3, 7 − 10 = −3. All match. (Notice that K+ and S2− both end up with 18 electrons, the same as the noble gas argon; Chapter 3 explains why.)

Naming monatomic ions

A monatomic cation keeps the element's name: Na+ is the sodium ion, Ca2+ the calcium ion. A monatomic anion takes the root of the element's name plus -ide: Cl− is chloride, O2− oxide, F− fluoride, S2− sulfide, N3− nitride.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Predict and name the ion

Problem. Predict the ion formed by (a) magnesium, (b) bromine, (c) oxygen, and name each.

Solution. (a) Mg is in group 2: Mg2+, magnesium ion. (b) Br is in group 17: Br−, bromide. (c) O is in group 16: O2−, oxide.

Check. The metal formed a cation and kept its name; the nonmetals formed anions ending in -ide.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The electrolytes on a basic metabolic panel are ions dissolved in the blood: sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), chloride (Cl−), and bicarbonate (HCO3−), with calcium (Ca2+) often measured too. Typical adult reference ranges are about 135–145 mmol/L for sodium, 3.5–5.0 mmol/L for potassium, and 98–106 mmol/L for chloride; each laboratory sets its own exact range. A value below the range is marked hypo- and a value above is hyper-: hyponatremia is low sodium, hyperkalemia is high potassium. Mateo's values (sodium 138, potassium 3.9, chloride 102 mmol/L) are all within the typical ranges. Nurses watch potassium closely because heart muscle depends on the right balance of K+ inside and outside its cells.

Biology Connection: Anatomy and Physiology

Calcium ions run your muscles. When a nerve signal reaches a muscle cell, Ca2+ ions are released inside the cell, and they trigger the protein filaments to slide past each other and contract the muscle. When the Ca2+ is pumped back into storage, the muscle relaxes. The same element, as calcium phosphate in bone and enamel, gives your skeleton and teeth their strength. Neither job could be done by neutral calcium atoms, which make a soft, reactive metal.

Valley Connection: Selenium at Kesterson

Selenium (Se, group 16) is an essential trace element: people and animals need a little of it. Too much is toxic. In the western San Joaquin Valley, the soils formed from rocks of the Coast Ranges that are naturally rich in selenium. When fields there are irrigated, water dissolves selenium from the soil, mostly as negatively charged, selenium-containing ions, and carries it away in subsurface drainage water.

In the 1970s and early 1980s, that drainage water was sent through the San Luis Drain to the Kesterson Reservoir, a set of shallow ponds in Merced County that was part of a national wildlife refuge. In 1983, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biologists found dead and badly deformed embryos and chicks of coots, grebes, stilts, and ducks there. Studies by the U.S. Geological Survey and the Fish and Wildlife Service traced the cause to selenium that had built up from the water into plants, insects, fish, and finally birds, a path scientists later nicknamed "from rock to duck." Kesterson was closed and its ponds were filled in, and managing selenium in farm drainage is still a major water-quality issue in the Valley today.

Sources: Ohlendorf, H. M., et al. (1986), "Embryonic mortality and abnormalities of aquatic birds: apparent impacts of selenium from irrigation drainwater," Science of the Total Environment, and related U.S. Geological Survey publications, for example "Selenium teratogenesis in natural populations of aquatic birds in central California" (USGS) and "Biogeochemical cycling of selenium in the San Joaquin Valley" (USGS).

Common Mistakes

  • Changing the number of protons. Ions form by gaining or losing electrons only.
  • Getting the sign backward. Losing electrons gives a positive ion; gaining electrons gives a negative ion.
  • Writing the charge as +2 instead of 2+. Ion charges are written number first, then sign: Ca2+.
  • Calling Cl− "chlorine." The anion is chloride; chlorine is the element.

Summary

  • Ions form when atoms gain or lose electrons; the number of protons does not change.
  • Cations (positive) form by losing electrons, usually from metals; anions (negative) form by gaining electrons, usually from nonmetals.
  • Charge = protons − electrons. Main-group charges follow the group: 1+, 2+, 3+ (Al), 3−, 2−, 1−.
  • Monatomic cations keep the element name; monatomic anions end in -ide.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many protons and electrons are in Ca2+ and in Br−?

Answer

Ca2+: 20 protons, 18 electrons. Br−: 35 protons, 36 electrons.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

An ion has 12 protons and 10 electrons. Identify it and give its charge.

Answer

Z = 12 is magnesium; charge = 12 − 10 = 2+, so it is Mg2+.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Name the ions F−, K+, and S2−.

Answer

Fluoride, potassium ion, sulfide.
