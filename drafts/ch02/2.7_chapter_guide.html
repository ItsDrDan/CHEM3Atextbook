Learning Objectives

  • Describe Dalton's atomic theory, the law of definite proportions, and the evidence for the electron and the nucleus.
  • Give the charge, relative mass, and location of protons, neutrons, and electrons; use the atomic number.
  • Write element symbols (including Latin-based ones and the first 20 elements); explain the shape of the periodic table (Mendeleev); locate elements by group and period; classify them as metals, nonmetals, or metalloids.
  • Explain how cations and anions form; count protons and electrons in ions; predict main-group ion charges and the fixed charges of Al, Zn, and Ag; name monatomic ions.
  • Read and write isotope notation and count protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Calculate an average atomic mass from isotope masses and abundances, including problems with a missing abundance.

Key Ideas

1. The atom was discovered by evidence, one model at a time

Dalton: elements are made of atoms that combine in whole-number ratios and are rearranged, not destroyed, in reactions (law of definite proportions; conservation of mass). Thomson: cathode rays are negative particles, the electron. Rutherford: a few alpha particles bounce off gold foil, so the atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space. Nuclear atomic theory in three sentences: the nucleus holds about 99.9% of the mass and all the positive charge; most of the volume is empty space with electrons; a neutral atom has equal numbers of electrons and protons. Different elements are made of different atoms: sodium and silver look alike but behave nothing alike in water. (Section 2.1)

2. Three particles, two places

Protons (1+, about 1 amu) and neutrons (0, about 1 amu) are in the nucleus; electrons (1−, about 0 amu) are outside it. The atomic number Z is the number of protons and defines the element. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. On a tile, the whole number is Z (a count); the decimal is the average atomic mass (not a count). In fill-in tables, take only the symbol and Z from the table. We never change the proton count; we only ever change electrons. (Section 2.2)

3. Read the periodic table like a map

Mendeleev stacked the elements so that properties repeat down each column; the modern table is ordered by atomic number. Groups are columns (1–18), periods are rows (1–7). Metals are on the left and center, nonmetals on the upper right (plus H), metalloids along the staircase from B. Main groups: 1, 2, 13–18; transition metals: 3–12. Named groups: alkali metals (1), alkaline earth metals (2), halogens (17), noble gases (18). Latin symbols: Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Sn, Au, Hg, Pb, Sb. Learn the first 20 elements (H to Ca) on flashcards. (Section 2.3)

4. Ions: gain or lose electrons, never protons

Cats have paws: cations are "paws-itive" (electrons lost, usually metals). Onions make you cry: anions are negative (electrons gained, usually nonmetals). Charge = protons − electrons. Main-group charges: group 1 → 1+, group 2 → 2+, Al → 3+, group 15 → 3−, group 16 → 2−, group 17 → 1−, group 18 → 0. The steps: Al3+, Zn2+, Ag+. Monatomic anions end in -ide (chloride, oxide). (Section 2.4)

5. Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons

Mass number A = protons + neutrons, so neutrons = A − Z. Isotopes of an element have the same Z and different A, written \( ^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \) or element-A (carbon-14). Isotopes are like the same pie in different sizes: same element, different mass. They behave alike chemically; some are radioactive (americium-241 in smoke detectors). (Section 2.5)

6. Atomic mass is a weighted average

Average atomic mass = Σ (percent ÷ 100 × isotope mass). The result lies between the isotope masses, closest to the most abundant isotope; it is the decimal number on the periodic table. Percent abundances add to 100%, so a missing one is 100% minus the rest. Use parentheses around each product on the calculator. (Section 2.6)

Key terms for Chapter 2
TermMeaningSection
Dalton's atomic theory (also atomic theory)The theory that elements are made of atoms, atoms of one element are alike, atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios, and atoms are rearranged (not created or destroyed) in reactions.2.1
law of definite proportions (also law of constant composition)A compound always contains the same elements in the same proportions by mass, whatever its source.2.1
electronA negatively charged subatomic particle (charge 1−, mass about 0 amu) found outside the nucleus; electrons are what atoms gain, lose or share in reactions.2.1
nucleus (also atomic nucleus)The tiny, dense center of an atom that holds all of its positive charge (protons) and almost all of its mass (protons and neutrons).2.1
ionizing radiationRadiation with enough energy to knock electrons off atoms and molecules, making ions; it includes X-rays, gamma rays and alpha and beta particles.2.1
protonA subatomic particle in the nucleus with a 1+ charge and a mass of about 1 amu. The number of protons identifies the element.2.2
neutronA subatomic particle in the nucleus with no charge and a mass of about 1 amu.2.2
atomic mass unit (also amu)A unit for the masses of atoms, defined as one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom (1 amu = 1.6605 × 10−24 g).2.2
atomic number (symbol Z)The number of protons in an atom's nucleus (symbol Z). It defines the element; in a neutral atom it also equals the number of electrons.2.2
group (also family)A vertical column of the periodic table, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in a group have similar chemical properties.2.3
periodA horizontal row of the periodic table, numbered 1 to 7.2.3
main-group elements (also representative elements)The elements in groups 1, 2 and 13–18, whose behavior is predictable from their position.2.3
transition metalsThe elements in groups 3–12, in the middle of the periodic table.2.3
metalAn element to the left of the staircase line that is shiny, conducts heat and electricity, and is malleable and ductile; metals tend to lose electrons and form cations.2.3
nonmetalAn element in the upper right of the periodic table (plus hydrogen) that is dull and brittle as a solid and conducts poorly; nonmetals tend to gain electrons.2.3
metalloid (also semimetal)An element along the staircase line (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te and usually At) with properties between those of metals and nonmetals.2.3
alkali metalsThe group 1 metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs): soft, very reactive metals that react with water. Hydrogen sits in group 1 but is not an alkali metal.2.3
alkaline earth metalsThe group 2 metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba): reactive metals that form 2+ ions.2.3
halogensThe group 17 elements (F, Cl, Br, I): very reactive nonmetals that exist as diatomic molecules.2.3
noble gasesThe group 18 elements (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe): very unreactive gases that exist as single atoms because their valence shells are full.2.3
ionAn atom or group of atoms with an overall electric charge because its numbers of protons and electrons are not equal. Only electrons are gained or lost.2.4
cationA positive ion, formed when an atom loses electrons. Metals usually form cations.2.4
anionA negative ion, formed when an atom gains electrons. Nonmetals usually form anions.2.4
mass number (symbol A)The total number of protons and neutrons in an atom's nucleus (symbol A).2.5
isotopes (also isotope)Atoms of the same element (same atomic number) with different numbers of neutrons, and so different mass numbers, such as chlorine-35 and chlorine-37.2.5
isotope symbol (also nuclide symbol, nuclear symbol)A symbol that shows the mass number as a superscript and the atomic number as a subscript to the left of the element symbol, as in 3717Cl; also written chlorine-37 or Cl-37.2.5
average atomic mass (also atomic mass)The weighted average of the masses of an element's isotopes, using their natural percent abundances; it is the mass shown on the periodic table.2.6
percent abundance (also natural abundance, fractional abundance)The percentage of an element's atoms in nature that are a given isotope; dividing by 100 gives the fractional abundance.2.6

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Fill in the table

Problem. Complete each row.

Particles in atoms and ions (blanks marked ?)
SpeciesProtonsNeutronsElectronsCharge
\( ^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}^{2+} \)???2+
?354636?
K-39???0

Solution. Ca-40 ion: 20 protons, 40 − 20 = 20 neutrons, 20 − 2 = 18 electrons. Second row: 35 protons is bromine; A = 35 + 46 = 81; charge = 35 − 36 = 1−, so it is \( ^{81}_{35}\text{Br}^{-} \). K-39: 19 protons, 39 − 19 = 20 neutrons, 19 electrons.

Check. Protons + neutrons = A and protons − electrons = charge in every row.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Mateo's electrolytes

Problem. Mateo's lab panel reports sodium, potassium, and chloride. Write each as an ion and give its protons and electrons.

Solution. Sodium (group 1) → Na+: 11 protons, 10 electrons. Potassium (group 1) → K+: 19 protons, 18 electrons. Chlorine (group 17) → Cl− (chloride): 17 protons, 18 electrons.

Check. Each charge equals protons − electrons: +1, +1, −1.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A weighted average

Problem. Boron is 19.9% boron-10 (10.013 amu) and 80.1% boron-11 (11.009 amu). Calculate its average atomic mass.

Solution. (0.199)(10.013) + (0.801)(11.009) = 1.993 + 8.818 = 10.811, reported as 10.8 amu (the abundances have 3 significant figures).

Check. The answer is between 10 and 11 and closer to 11, because boron-11 is about four times as common. The periodic table lists 10.81.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Locate and classify

Problem. Give the group, period, family name (if any), and classification of (a) Br and (b) Sr.

Solution. (a) Bromine: group 17, period 4, halogen, nonmetal (a liquid at room temperature). (b) Strontium: group 2, period 5, alkaline earth metal, metal.

Check. Group 17 is on the far right (nonmetals); group 2 is on the far left (metals).

Common Mistakes

  • Protons define the element; ions change electrons only.
  • Losing electrons makes a positive ion.
  • Neutrons = A − Z; the charge never enters the neutron count.
  • Use the whole-number atomic number for protons, not the decimal atomic mass.
  • Change percents to decimals before multiplying in a weighted average, and put each product in parentheses.
  • A missing abundance is 100% minus the others; do not overthink it.
  • Zinc is always 2+ and silver always 1+ (the steps); the plus sign means electrons were lost.
  • K is potassium; P is phosphorus. Cl− is chloride, not chlorine.
