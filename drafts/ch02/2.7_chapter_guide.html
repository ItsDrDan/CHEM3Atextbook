Learning Objectives

  • Describe Dalton's atomic theory, the law of definite proportions, and the evidence for the electron and the nucleus.
  • Give the charge, relative mass, and location of protons, neutrons, and electrons; use the atomic number.
  • Write element symbols (including Latin-based ones); locate elements by group and period; classify them as metals, nonmetals, or metalloids.
  • Explain how cations and anions form; count protons and electrons in ions; predict main-group ion charges; name monatomic ions.
  • Read and write isotope notation and count protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Calculate an average atomic mass from isotope masses and abundances.

Key Ideas

1. The atom was discovered by evidence, one model at a time

Dalton: elements are made of atoms that combine in whole-number ratios and are rearranged, not destroyed, in reactions (law of definite proportions; conservation of mass). Thomson: cathode rays are negative particles, the electron. Rutherford: a few alpha particles bounce off gold foil, so the atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space. (Section 2.1)

2. Three particles, two places

Protons (1+, about 1 amu) and neutrons (0, about 1 amu) are in the nucleus; electrons (1−, about 0 amu) are outside it. The atomic number Z is the number of protons and defines the element. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. (Section 2.2)

3. Read the periodic table like a map

Groups are columns (1–18), periods are rows (1–7). Metals are on the left and center, nonmetals on the upper right (plus H), metalloids along the staircase from B. Main groups: 1, 2, 13–18; transition metals: 3–12. Named groups: alkali metals (1), alkaline earth metals (2), halogens (17), noble gases (18). Latin symbols: Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Sn, Au, Hg, Pb, Sb. (Section 2.3)

4. Ions: gain or lose electrons, never protons

Cations are positive (electrons lost, usually metals); anions are negative (electrons gained, usually nonmetals). Charge = protons − electrons. Main-group charges: group 1 → 1+, group 2 → 2+, Al → 3+, group 15 → 3−, group 16 → 2−, group 17 → 1−. Monatomic anions end in -ide (chloride, oxide). (Section 2.4)

5. Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons

Mass number A = protons + neutrons, so neutrons = A − Z. Isotopes of an element have the same Z and different A, written \( ^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \) or element-A (carbon-14). Isotopes behave alike chemically; some are radioactive. (Section 2.5)

6. Atomic mass is a weighted average

Average atomic mass = Σ (percent ÷ 100 × isotope mass). The result lies between the isotope masses, closest to the most abundant isotope; it is the decimal number on the periodic table. (Section 2.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Fill in the table

Problem. Complete each row.

Particles in atoms and ions (blanks marked ?)
SpeciesProtonsNeutronsElectronsCharge
\( ^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}^{2+} \)???2+
?354636?
K-39???0

Solution. Ca-40 ion: 20 protons, 40 − 20 = 20 neutrons, 20 − 2 = 18 electrons. Second row: 35 protons is bromine; A = 35 + 46 = 81; charge = 35 − 36 = 1−, so it is \( ^{81}_{35}\text{Br}^{-} \). K-39: 19 protons, 39 − 19 = 20 neutrons, 19 electrons.

Check. Protons + neutrons = A and protons − electrons = charge in every row.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Mateo's electrolytes

Problem. Mateo's lab panel reports sodium, potassium, and chloride. Write each as an ion and give its protons and electrons.

Solution. Sodium (group 1) → Na+: 11 protons, 10 electrons. Potassium (group 1) → K+: 19 protons, 18 electrons. Chlorine (group 17) → Cl− (chloride): 17 protons, 18 electrons.

Check. Each charge equals protons − electrons: +1, +1, −1.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): A weighted average

Problem. Boron is 19.9% boron-10 (10.013 amu) and 80.1% boron-11 (11.009 amu). Calculate its average atomic mass.

Solution. (0.199)(10.013) + (0.801)(11.009) = 1.993 + 8.818 = 10.811, reported as 10.8 amu (the abundances have 3 significant figures).

Check. The answer is between 10 and 11 and closer to 11, because boron-11 is about four times as common. The periodic table lists 10.81.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Locate and classify

Problem. Give the group, period, family name (if any), and classification of (a) Br and (b) Sr.

Solution. (a) Bromine: group 17, period 4, halogen, nonmetal (a liquid at room temperature). (b) Strontium: group 2, period 5, alkaline earth metal, metal.

Check. Group 17 is on the far right (nonmetals); group 2 is on the far left (metals).

Common Mistakes

  • Protons define the element; ions change electrons only.
  • Losing electrons makes a positive ion.
  • Neutrons = A − Z; the charge never enters the neutron count.
  • Use the whole-number atomic number for protons, not the decimal atomic mass.
  • Change percents to decimals before multiplying in a weighted average.
  • K is potassium; P is phosphorus. Cl− is chloride, not chlorine.
