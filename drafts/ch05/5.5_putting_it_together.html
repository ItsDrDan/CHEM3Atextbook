Learning Objectives

  • Use a four-step routine (Given and Desire, map, equalities, calculate) for any mole problem.
  • Solve multi-step problems that combine molar mass, Avogadro's number, subscript ratios, and density.
  • Work backward from the mass of an element to the mass of the compound that contains it.
  • Pull the useful numbers out of a wordy problem, and estimate to check an answer.

Sections 5.1 to 5.4 each added one tool: Avogadro's number, molar mass, the road map, and subscript ratios. Real problems, on exams, in lab, and in the clinic, rarely say which tool to use. This section puts the tools together into one routine and works through the kinds of multi-step problems that come up most often. None of the chemistry is new; what is new is the planning.

The Four-Step Routine

  1. Given and Desire. Write what the problem gives you (number, unit, and substance) and what it wants (unit and substance).
  2. Map it out. Find the starting box and the ending box on the mole road map (Section 5.3) and count the arrows. Each arrow is one conversion factor. If the substance changes (compound to element), one arrow is a subscript ratio. If the problem leaves the road map (milliliters, for example), look for a factor it gives you, such as a density.
  3. Write each equality, then flip it to cancel. Write "1 mol NaOH = 39.997 g NaOH" first, then put it in the setup so the unit you just had is on the bottom. Let your units guide your process.
  4. Calculate and check. Multiply everything on top, divide by everything on the bottom, and round once, to the significant figures of the measured numbers you were given. Then ask whether the size of the answer makes sense.

Molar masses can always be calculated from the formula if they are not given, and unit conversions from Chapter 1 (mg to g, kg to g) can be added at either end of the map.

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Atoms of one element from grams of a compound

Problem. How many sodium atoms are in 5.00 g of sodium nitride, Na3N?

Given: 5.00 g Na3N. Desire: ??? Na atoms.

Map: g Na3N → mol Na3N → mol Na → Na atoms (three arrows: molar mass, subscript ratio, Avogadro's number).

Equalities: 1 mol Na3N = 3(22.99) + 14.007 = 82.977 g, which rounds to 82.98 g (Na has two decimal places); 1 mol Na3N = 3 mol Na; 1 mol Na = 6.0221 × 1023 Na atoms.

Solution.

\( {5.00\;\text{g Na}}_3\text{N} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol Na}_3\text{N}}{82.98\;\text{g Na}_3\text{N}} \times \dfrac{3\;\text{mol Na}}{1\;\text{mol Na}_3\text{N}} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\;\text{Na atoms}}{1\;\text{mol Na}} = {1.09 \times 10^{23}\;\text{Na atoms}} \)

Check. 5.00 g is about 0.06 mol of Na3N; three Na per formula unit gives about 0.18 mol of Na, a little under a fifth of a mole, so a little over 1023 atoms is right.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Moles to milliliters through density

Problem. Hand sanitizer is made with ethanol, C2H5OH (density 0.789 g/mL). What volume of ethanol contains 0.500 mol?

Given: 0.500 mol C2H5OH. Desire: ??? mL C2H5OH.

Map: Moles and milliliters are not directly connected. What links them? Molar mass turns moles into grams, and the density (Section 1.9) turns grams into milliliters. So: mol → g → mL.

Equalities: 1 mol C2H5OH = 2(12.011) + 6(1.008) + 15.999 = 46.069 g (count all six H, including the one in OH); 0.789 g = 1 mL.

Solution.

\( {0.500\;\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{46.069\;\text{g}}{1\;\text{mol}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mL}}{0.789\;\text{g}} = {29.2\;\text{mL C}}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \)

Check. Half a mole of ethanol weighs about 23 g, and ethanol is less dense than water, so the volume must be noticeably larger than 23 mL (about 1.3 mL for every gram). Three significant figures, like 0.500 and 0.789.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Working backward, element to compound

Problem. A patient needs a calcium supplement that supplies 500. mg of calcium. What mass of calcium carbonate, CaCO3, contains that much calcium?

Given: 500. mg Ca (the decimal point makes all three digits significant). Desire: ??? g CaCO3.

Map: the road map in reverse: g Ca → mol Ca → mol CaCO3 → g CaCO3, after converting mg to g.

Equalities: 1 g = 1000 mg; 1 mol Ca = 40.08 g; 1 mol CaCO3 = 1 mol Ca; 1 mol CaCO3 = 40.08 + 12.011 + 3(15.999) = 100.09 g.

Solution.

\( {500.\;\text{mg Ca}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{g}}{1000\;\text{mg}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol Ca}}{40.08\;\text{g Ca}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol CaCO}_3}{1\;\text{mol Ca}} \times \dfrac{100.09\;\text{g CaCO}_3}{1\;\text{mol CaCO}_3} = {1.25\;\text{g CaCO}}_3 \)

Check. Calcium is about 40% of calcium carbonate, so the compound must weigh about 2.5 times as much as the calcium it holds: 2.5 × 0.500 g = 1.25 g. This is the reverse of the antacid example in the Chapter Guide (Section 5.6). (Context only; follow the order and the product label.)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Same mass, which has more?

Problem. Which contains more oxygen atoms: 10.0 g of water, H2O, or 10.0 g of carbon dioxide, CO2?

Given: 10.0 g of each. Desire: compare mol O (more moles of O means more O atoms, so Avogadro's number is not needed).

Solution.

  • Water: 10.0 g ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 0.555 mol H2O; × 1 mol O per mol = 0.555 mol O.
  • Carbon dioxide: 10.0 g ÷ 44.009 g/mol = 0.2272 mol CO2; × 2 mol O per mol = 0.454 mol O.

Water has more oxygen atoms, even though each CO2 molecule has two O atoms and each water molecule has only one.

Check. This is why the shortcut in Section 5.3 (smaller molar mass, more particles) applies only to the particles in the formula itself. Once a subscript ratio is involved, do the conversion.

Reading a Wordy Problem

Problems written about real situations often include background information you do not need. A lot of the time it is just there to set the scene. Underline the given (a number with a unit and a substance) and the desire, then map only those.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Find the numbers that matter

Problem. Potassium chloride, KCl, is given to patients whose blood potassium is low. A pharmacy technician weighs out 2.50 g of KCl at 22 °C and dissolves it in 100 mL of sterile water in a 250 mL flask. How many potassium ions are in the solution?

Given: 2.50 g KCl. Desire: ??? K+ ions. The temperature, the water volume, and the flask size do not change how many ions there are; they are distractors.

Map and equalities: g KCl → mol KCl → mol K+ → K+ ions. 1 mol KCl = 39.10 + 35.45 = 74.55 g; 1 mol KCl = 1 mol K+; 1 mol K+ = 6.0221 × 1023 ions.

Solution.

\( {2.50\;\text{g KCl}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol KCl}}{74.55\;\text{g KCl}} \times \dfrac{1\;\text{mol K}^+}{1\;\text{mol KCl}} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\;\text{K}^+}{1\;\text{mol K}^+} = {2.02 \times 10^{22}\;\text{K}}^+\;\text{ions} \)

Check. 2.50 g is about 1/30 of a mole, and 1/30 of 6 × 1023 is about 2 × 1022. (Context only; this is not dosing guidance.)

Professor Tip: Estimate to Check

Before you trust a calculator answer, especially on a multiple-choice question, estimate with rounded masses: C ≈ 12, O ≈ 16, H ≈ 1, N ≈ 14. For 88 g of CO2, the molar mass is about 12 + 2(16) = 44, so the answer should be about 88 ÷ 44 = 2 mol. If your calculator says 0.5 mol or 3900 mol, a factor is upside down. Estimating is only a check: your written answer still uses the yellow-sheet masses as printed.

Professor Tip: Which Conversions Matter Most Later?

Of the three conversion factors in this chapter, molar mass and the mole-to-mole ratio are the ones you will use constantly for the rest of the course; in Chapter 8 the mole ratio comes from the coefficients of a balanced equation instead of from subscripts. Avogadro's number is essential now but is used far less often later. Spend your practice time accordingly.

Common Mistakes

  • Calculating before mapping. Count the arrows first; a three-arrow problem needs three factors.
  • Stopping one step early. Check the Desire: moles of Na3N are not atoms of Na.
  • Using a density as a molar mass. Density is g per mL; molar mass is g per mol.
  • Carrying distractor numbers into the setup. If a number has no place on your map, leave it out.
  • Rounding in the middle. Keep every digit until the end.

Summary

  • Given and Desire, map it, write equalities and flip them to cancel, then multiply the top and divide the bottom.
  • The subscript ratio is the arrow that changes the substance; density is the bridge from grams to volume.
  • The same road map runs in reverse, from an element back to its compound.
  • Strip distractors from wordy problems, and estimate with rounded masses to check.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What volume of water (density 1.00 g/mL) is 2.00 mol of water?

Hint

Moles to grams with the molar mass of water, then grams to mL with the density (mL on top).

Answer

2.00 mol × 18.015 g/mol = 36.03 g; × 1 mL/1.00 g = 36.0 mL.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

What mass of potassium chloride, KCl, contains 1.00 g of potassium?

Hint

Work backward: grams of K to moles of K, then moles of KCl (1 : 1), then grams of KCl.

Answer

1.00 g K ÷ 39.10 g/mol = 0.02558 mol K = 0.02558 mol KCl; × 74.55 g/mol = 1.91 g KCl.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

A student says, "CO2 has two oxygens, so any mass of CO2 has more oxygen atoms than the same mass of H2O." Is the student right?

Hint

Find the moles of O in the same mass of each. Per gram, which compound has more molecules? Does that outweigh the 2 : 1 subscript?

Answer

No. Per gram, water has more moles of molecules (18.015 vs 44.009 g/mol), and that outweighs the 2:1 subscript. 10.0 g of H2O holds 0.555 mol O, while 10.0 g of CO2 holds 0.454 mol O (Example 4).
