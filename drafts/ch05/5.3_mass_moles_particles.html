Learning Objectives

  • Convert between mass and moles using molar mass.
  • Convert between mass and number of particles in two steps, through moles.
  • Use millimoles and milligrams in clinical-style conversions.
  • Choose the conversion factor from the words in a problem, and compare equal-mass samples without a calculator.

You now have two conversion factors. Molar mass connects grams and moles; Avogadro's number connects moles and particles. Together they make the mole road map, the route for almost every calculation in the rest of this course.

Professor Tip: The Goal Is the Mole

"The goal is the mole." If you can get to moles, you can get anywhere: grams, particles, atoms of one element, and later, grams of a product in a reaction (Chapter 8), liters of a solution (Chapter 11), and liters of a gas (Chapter 10). The reason is practical. Nobody can count particles, and there is no instrument that reads moles directly. What you can measure in the lab is mass, on a balance. So almost every problem starts by turning what you measured into moles, and then turning moles into what you want.

Which Factor Do I Need?

Map the problem before you calculate, and let the units guide you. Each arrow on the road map has its own conversion factor, and the words in the problem tell you which one:

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Cue words and the conversion factor they call for
If the problem says…Use…Example factor
grams, mg, kg (a mass), together with molesMolar mass (Section 5.2)18.015 g H2O / 1 mol H2O
atoms, molecules, ions, formula units (something tiny, counted one by one)Avogadro's number (Section 5.1)6.0221 × 1023 molecules / 1 mol
a different substance: an element or ion inside the compoundSubscript mole ratio (Section 5.4)2 mol O / 1 mol CO2

Read carefully: atoms and molecules are not the same thing. "How many molecules of CO2" needs one factor less than "how many atoms of O in CO2."

Grams and Moles

Molar mass can be written as a conversion factor either way up, for example for NaCl:

\( \dfrac{58.44\ \text{g NaCl}}{1\ \text{mol NaCl}} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \dfrac{1\ \text{mol NaCl}}{58.44\ \text{g NaCl}} \)

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Grams to moles (Mateo's IV bag)

Problem. An IV bag contains 2.00 g of magnesium sulfate, MgSO4 (molar mass 120.37 g/mol). How many moles, and how many millimoles, of MgSO4 is that?

Given: 2.00 g MgSO4. Desire: ??? mol MgSO4. Equality: 1 mol MgSO4 = 120.37 g MgSO4.

Solution. Grams are in the given, so grams go on the bottom:

\( 2.00\ \text{g MgSO}_4 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol MgSO}_4}{120.37\ \text{g MgSO}_4} = 0.0166\ \text{mol MgSO}_4 = 16.6\ \text{mmol} \)

Check. 2 g is a small fraction of 120 g (one mole), so a small fraction of a mole makes sense. 1 mmol = 10−3 mol, so 0.0166 mol = 16.6 mmol. (Context only; this is not dosing guidance.)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Moles to grams

Problem. A lab procedure calls for 0.350 mol of NaCl. What mass should you weigh?

Given: 0.350 mol NaCl. Desire: ??? g NaCl. Equality: 1 mol NaCl = 58.44 g NaCl.

Solution.

\( 0.350\ \text{mol NaCl} \times \dfrac{58.44\ \text{g NaCl}}{1\ \text{mol NaCl}} = 20.5\ \text{g NaCl} \)

Check. About a third of a mole should weigh about a third of 58 g, roughly 20 g.

Grams to Particles (Two Steps)

There is no single factor that turns grams into particles. Go through moles: grams → moles (divide by molar mass), then moles → particles (multiply by Avogadro's number). You can do it in one line by chaining the factors.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Grams to molecules

Problem. How many water molecules are in 25.0 g of water (about one big sip)?

Given: 25.0 g H2O. Desire: ??? H2O molecules. Map: grams → moles → molecules (two arrows, so two factors). Equalities: 1 mol H2O = 18.015 g; 1 mol H2O = 6.0221 × 1023 molecules.

Solution.

\( 25.0\ \text{g H}_2\text{O} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}}{18.015\ \text{g H}_2\text{O}} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}} = 8.36 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules} \)

Check. 25.0 g is a bit more than one mole (18.015 g), so a bit more than 6.02 × 1023 molecules is right. The answer has 3 significant figures because the measurement, 25.0 g, has 3. The molar mass (5 significant figures) and Avogadro's number (5) are not what limits you; the number you started with is.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): The mass of one molecule

Problem. What is the mass, in grams, of a single water molecule?

Solution. One mole (6.0221 × 1023 molecules) has a mass of 18.015 g, so

\( \dfrac{18.015\ \text{g}}{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}} = 2.9915 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{g per molecule} \)

Check. A single molecule should be unimaginably light, and 3 × 10−23 g is. (In amu, it is 18.015 amu, the formula mass.)

Professor Tip: Multiply the Top, Divide the Bottom

Once the units cancel, you do not need to work out each step separately. Multiply every number on the top of the setup, then divide by every number on the bottom, and round once at the end. For Example 3 on the TI-36X Pro, type

25.0 × 6.0221E23 ÷ 18.015

with no parentheses needed, and the screen shows 8.357… × 1023. If you prefer to divide step by step, that works too; just do not round in the middle. Flipping a conversion factor does not change the equality it comes from: 1 mol H2O = 18.015 g can be written 18.015 g / 1 mol or 1 mol / 18.015 g. That is why you can turn a molar mass upside down whenever grams need to cancel.

Comparing Samples Without a Calculator

Some questions ask which sample has the most particles. If the samples all have the same mass, you can reason it out. Dividing the same mass by a smaller molar mass gives more moles, and more moles means more particles.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Which sample has the most atoms?

Problem. Five vials each hold 15.0 g of a pure metal: aluminum, iron, copper, silver, and lead. Which vial holds the most atoms? Which holds the fewest?

Solution. Every vial has the same mass, so compare molar masses: Al 26.98, Fe 55.85, Cu 63.55, Ag 107.87, Pb 207.20 g/mol. Aluminum has the smallest molar mass (its atoms are the lightest), so it takes the most aluminum atoms to make 15.0 g. The vial with the most atoms is aluminum; the vial with the fewest is lead.

Check. You can prove it for the two extremes: 15.0 g ÷ 26.98 g/mol = 0.556 mol Al, but 15.0 g ÷ 207.20 g/mol = 0.0724 mol Pb. More moles means more atoms (3.35 × 1023 Al atoms versus 4.36 × 1022 Pb atoms), so the aluminum vial holds almost eight times as many atoms.

This shortcut works only when the masses are equal. If the masses are different, convert each to moles.

Professor Tip: Map It First

Before you calculate, find your starting box and your ending box on the road map and count the arrows. Grams to moles is one step; grams to particles is two. Then write each conversion factor with units, and cross out units as they cancel. If the units you are left with are not the units you want, a factor is upside down. This habit is the same dimensional analysis you learned in Chapter 1, and it is what you will use for stoichiometry in Chapter 8.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many lab values are reported in millimoles per liter (mmol/L), a count of particles, while others in the United States are reported in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL), a mass. Molar mass connects them. Mateo's sodium of 138 mmol/L means 0.138 mol of Na+ per liter, or 0.138 mol × 22.99 g/mol = 3.17 g of sodium ions per liter of plasma. Blood glucose shows the conversion clearly: glucose (C6H12O6) has a molar mass of 180.156 g/mol, so a fasting glucose of 95 mg/dL is

\( \dfrac{95\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{dL}} \times \dfrac{10\ \text{dL}}{1\ \text{L}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol}}{180.156\ \text{mg}} = 5.3\ \text{mmol/L} \)

That is why nurses who work with glucose meters set to different units learn the shortcut "divide mg/dL by about 18" to get mmol/L. (Context only; always use the units on your facility's meter and report.)

Common Mistakes

  • Turning a mass into moles by relabeling it. "12 g of hydrogen" is a mass, not 12 mol. You need the molar mass to get to moles.
  • Trying to go straight from grams to particles. Always pass through moles.
  • Multiplying by molar mass to get moles. Grams ÷ (g/mol) = mol. Check that grams cancel.
  • Mixing up mmol and mol. 1 mmol = 0.001 mol; 16.6 mmol = 0.0166 mol.
  • Rounding the molar mass before you use it. Use the yellow-sheet values and round once at the end.

Summary

  • grams ÷ molar mass = moles; moles × molar mass = grams.
  • moles × 6.0221 × 1023 = particles; particles ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 = moles.
  • Grams ↔ particles always takes two steps, through moles.
  • Cue words pick the factor: mass → molar mass; atoms or molecules → Avogadro's number; another substance → subscript ratio.
  • For equal masses, the smaller molar mass gives more moles and more particles.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many moles are in 10.0 g of CO2 (44.009 g/mol)? How many molecules?

Hint

The goal is the mole: grams to moles with the molar mass (grams on the bottom), then moles to molecules with Avogadro's number.

Answer

10.0 ÷ 44.009 = 0.227 mol; 0.2272 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.37 × 1023 molecules.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

An acetaminophen tablet contains 325 mg of acetaminophen, C8H9NO2 (151.165 g/mol). How many millimoles is that?

Hint

Change mg to g, divide by the molar mass to get moles, then convert to millimoles (1 mol = 1000 mmol).

Answer

0.325 g ÷ 151.165 g/mol = 0.00215 mol = 2.15 mmol.

Grams divided by g/mol gives moles, and 1000 mmol per mol turns that into millimoles; dividing mg by the molar mass read as mg/mmol gives the same result in one step.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What is the mass of 2.50 × 1022 glucose molecules (180.156 g/mol)?

Hint

There is no direct path from molecules to grams. Go through moles: molecules to moles (Avogadro's number), then moles to grams (molar mass).

Answer

2.50 × 1022 ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 = 0.04151 mol; × 180.156 g/mol = 7.48 g.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Without a calculator: which contains more atoms, 5.00 g of helium or 5.00 g of neon?

Hint

Equal masses: which element has the smaller molar mass? A smaller molar mass means more moles in the same mass, and more moles means more atoms.

Answer

Helium. The masses are equal and helium has the smaller molar mass (4.003 vs 20.180 g/mol), so 5.00 g of He is more moles (1.25 mol vs 0.248 mol) and therefore more atoms.
