Learning Objectives

  • Write mole ratios from the subscripts in a chemical formula.
  • Find the moles or number of atoms or ions of one element in a given amount of a compound.
  • Find the mass of one element in a given mass of a compound.
  • Avoid the diatomic trap, and count polyatomic ions and total atoms in a formula.

A label that says "iron supplement, 325 mg ferrous sulfate" does not mean you swallow 325 mg of iron. Most of that mass is sulfate and water. To know how much iron is actually there, you need one more kind of conversion factor, and it is hiding in plain sight in every chemical formula: the subscripts.

Subscripts Are Mole Ratios

Start small. One CO2 molecule contains 1 C atom and 2 O atoms. Now scale up and watch the ratio:

The subscripts give the ratio no matter how many molecules you have, so one mole of CO2 molecules contains 1 mol of C atoms and 2 mol of O atoms. Think of each subscript as a bridge between the compound and the atoms inside it.

So each formula gives you exact conversion factors such as

\( \dfrac{2\ \text{mol O}}{1\ \text{mol CO}_2} \qquad \dfrac{3\ \text{mol Cl}^-}{1\ \text{mol AlCl}_3} \qquad \dfrac{12\ \text{mol H}}{1\ \text{mol C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6} \)

Because they are exact counts, these factors never limit significant figures.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Moles and atoms of an element

Problem. How many moles of oxygen atoms, and how many oxygen atoms, are in 3.9 mol of CO2?

Solution.

\( 3.9\ \text{mol CO}_2 \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol O}}{1\ \text{mol CO}_2} = 7.8\ \text{mol O} \)

\( 7.8\ \text{mol O} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{O atoms}}{1\ \text{mol O}} = 4.7 \times 10^{24}\ \text{O atoms} \)

Check. There are twice as many O atoms as CO2 molecules, so twice as many moles. 3.9 has 2 significant figures, so the answers have 2.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Ions in an ionic compound

Problem. Mateo's IV bag held 0.0166 mol of MgSO4 (Section 5.3). How many moles of Mg2+ ions, and how many moles of ions in total, does it release when it dissolves?

Solution. Each formula unit is one Mg2+ and one SO42−, so there is 0.0166 mol Mg2+ (16.6 mmol) and 0.0166 mol SO42−, for 0.0332 mol of ions in total.

Check. The polyatomic ion counts as one particle (Section 4.2). Counting particles in solution matters for osmolality in Chapter 11.

Polyatomic Ions as Units

Ratios work for polyatomic ions too, because the ion stays together as one unit (Section 4.2). Calcium phosphate, Ca3(PO4)2, a close relative of the mineral in bones and teeth, gives all of these exact factors per mole of compound:

  • 3 mol Ca2+ ions
  • 2 mol PO43− ions (so 5 mol of ions in all)
  • 2 mol P atoms (one in each phosphate)
  • 8 mol O atoms (4 in each phosphate, times 2)

Decide first whether the question asks about the ion (PO43−) or an element in it (P or O); the factor is different.

Counting All the Atoms

Sometimes a problem just says "atoms," meaning atoms of every kind. Add the subscripts to get the atoms in one formula, and use that total as the ratio. Glucose, C6H12O6, has 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 atoms per molecule, so 0.100 mol of glucose contains 0.100 × 24 = 2.40 mol of atoms, or 2.40 × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.45 × 1024 atoms.

Mass of an Element in a Compound

To find the mass of one element inside a sample, chain the road map: grams of compound → moles of compound → moles of element (subscript ratio) → grams of element.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Chlorine in table salt

Problem. What mass of chlorine is in 0.750 mol of NaCl?

Solution.

\( 0.750\ \text{mol NaCl} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Cl}}{1\ \text{mol NaCl}} \times \dfrac{35.45\ \text{g Cl}}{1\ \text{mol Cl}} = 26.6\ \text{g Cl} \)

Check. 0.750 mol of NaCl weighs 43.8 g, and chlorine is the heavier of its two elements, so a little over half of that, 26.6 g, makes sense.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Hydrogen atoms in glucose

Problem. How many hydrogen atoms are in 9.00 g of glucose, C6H12O6 (180.156 g/mol)?

Solution.

\( 9.00\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6}{180.156\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{12\ \text{mol H}}{1\ \text{mol C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{H}}{1\ \text{mol H}} = 3.61 \times 10^{23}\ \text{H atoms} \)

Check. 9.00 g is about 0.05 mol of glucose; 12 H per molecule gives about 0.6 mol H, a little over half a mole of atoms.

The Diatomic Trap

Dr. Dan calls this one of the biggest mistakes students make in this chapter. Seven elements exist as diatomic molecules (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2; "Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Beer," Section 5.2). When a problem says "oxygen gas" or "chlorine gas," the sample is made of molecules, and there is a ratio hidden in plain sight: 1 mol Cl2 = 2 mol Cl atoms.

Example \(\PageIndex{5}\): Atoms in a diatomic gas

Problem. A cylinder of chlorine gas used to disinfect a city's water supply releases 25.0 g of Cl2. How many chlorine atoms is that?

Given: 25.0 g Cl2. Desire: ??? Cl atoms. Map: g Cl2 → mol Cl2 → mol Cl → Cl atoms. Equalities: 1 mol Cl2 = 2(35.45) = 70.90 g; 1 mol Cl2 = 2 mol Cl; 1 mol Cl = 6.0221 × 1023 Cl atoms.

Solution.

\( 25.0\ \text{g Cl}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Cl}_2}{70.90\ \text{g Cl}_2} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol Cl}}{1\ \text{mol Cl}_2} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{Cl atoms}}{1\ \text{mol Cl}} = 4.25 \times 10^{23}\ \text{Cl atoms} \)

Check. The two common wrong answers are 2.12 × 1023 (the number of Cl2 molecules: the ×2 step was skipped) and 8.49 × 1023 (molar mass of a single Cl atom used and ×2 applied, which counts every atom twice). Use the molecule's molar mass with the ×2 ratio, or the atom's molar mass (35.45) with no ratio; both routes give 4.25 × 1023.

Example \(\PageIndex{6}\): Nitrogen in laughing gas

Problem. Dinitrogen monoxide, N2O (nitrous oxide, "laughing gas"), is used for sedation in dental offices. How many grams of nitrogen are in a 125 g sample of N2O?

Given: 125 g N2O. Desire: ??? g N. Map: g N2O → mol N2O → mol N → g N. Equalities: 1 mol N2O = 2(14.007) + 15.999 = 44.013 g; 1 mol N2O = 2 mol N; 1 mol N = 14.007 g.

Solution.

\( 125\ \text{g N}_2\text{O} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol N}_2\text{O}}{44.013\ \text{g N}_2\text{O}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol N}}{1\ \text{mol N}_2\text{O}} \times \dfrac{14.007\ \text{g N}}{1\ \text{mol N}} = 79.6\ \text{g N} \)

Check. Nitrogen supplies 28.014 of the 44.013 g in each mole, about 64%, and 64% of 125 g is about 80 g. The nitrogen here is part of the N2O molecule, so it is counted as N atoms (14.007 g/mol), not as N2 gas. (Context only; sedation is given by trained, licensed providers.)

Professor Tip

Name the particle in every unit: "mol CO2," "mol O," "g Cl." Students who write only "mol" cannot tell which moles they have, and the subscript factor gets skipped or flipped. With the particle written in, the factor 2 mol O / 1 mol CO2 almost writes itself.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Iron supplement labels list two different masses. A common tablet contains 325 mg of ferrous sulfate (as the heptahydrate, FeSO4·7H2O, 278.01 g/mol) and states "65 mg elemental iron." You can check it: each mole of the hydrate holds one mole of Fe (55.85 g), so 325 mg × (55.85 ÷ 278.01) = 65.3 mg of iron. Orders and nutrition guidelines are written for elemental iron, so mixing up the salt mass and the iron mass would change the amount of iron a patient receives five-fold. The same idea applies to calcium (calcium carbonate is about 40% calcium) and to potassium salts. (Context only; follow the order and the product label.)

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste is labeled "1.1% sodium fluoride," while the fluoride level is often quoted as about 5000 ppm fluoride. Both describe the same paste. In 100 g of paste there are 1.1 g of NaF (41.99 g/mol), and each mole of NaF contains one mole of F (18.998 g), so the fluoride mass is 1.1 g × (18.998 ÷ 41.99) = 0.50 g, which is 0.50 g per 100 g, or about 5000 parts per million. Over-the-counter pastes contain about one-fifth as much. Hygienists use these numbers when recommending products, especially for children, who should use only a small amount.

Valley Connection: Buying Nitrogen by the Ton

San Joaquin Valley growers apply nitrogen fertilizer to almonds, pistachios, tomatoes, and other crops, but plants use the nitrogen, not the whole compound. That is why every fertilizer bag carries a grade such as 46-0-0 for urea, CO(NH2)2, or 34-0-0 for ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3: the first number is the percent nitrogen by mass. You can check those grades with this section's method. Urea (60.056 g/mol) contains 2 mol of N (28.014 g) per mole, so it is 28.014 ÷ 60.056 = 46.6% nitrogen. Ammonium nitrate (80.043 g/mol) also contains 2 mol of N per mole, so it is 28.014 ÷ 80.043 = 35.0% nitrogen (commercial products are slightly less pure, hence 34). Using the label grades, a grower who needs 100 lb of nitrogen per acre needs about 217 lb of urea (100 ÷ 0.46) but about 294 lb of ammonium nitrate (100 ÷ 0.34). Matching nitrogen to what the trees actually take up is also how growers keep excess nitrate out of groundwater (Section 4.2). California's Fertilizer Research and Education Program and UC Cooperative Extension publish nitrogen guidelines for Valley crops.

Sources: California Department of Food and Agriculture, Fertilizer Research and Education Program (cdfa.ca.gov/is/ffldrs/frep); UC Agriculture and Natural Resources, nitrogen management resources for orchard crops.

Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting the subscript step. Moles of CO2 are not moles of O.
  • Using the element's molar mass for the compound (or the reverse). Check which particle each unit names.
  • Missing parentheses. Ca(NO3)2 has 6 mol O per mol of compound.
  • Using the diatomic molar mass for atoms. Oxygen atoms in a compound use 15.999 g/mol, not 31.998.
  • Forgetting that a gas is diatomic. "Chlorine gas" is Cl2: 1 mol Cl2 holds 2 mol Cl atoms.
  • Mixing up an ion and its atoms. 1 mol Ca3(PO4)2 has 2 mol PO43− ions but 8 mol O atoms.

Summary

  • Subscripts give exact mole ratios between a compound and each element (or ion) in it.
  • Moles of compound × subscript ratio = moles of element; then use molar mass or Avogadro's number as needed.
  • The mass of an element in a compound follows the road map: g compound → mol compound → mol element → g element.
  • Diatomic gases (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2) have 2 mol of atoms per mole of gas.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many moles of nitrogen atoms are in 0.40 mol of Ca(NO3)2?

Hint

Use the subscripts as a mole ratio: how many N atoms are in one Ca(NO3)2? Remember the 2 outside the parentheses.

Answer

0.40 mol × 2 mol N / 1 mol Ca(NO3)2 = 0.80 mol N.

Each formula unit holds two nitrate ions with one N each, so the ratio is 2 mol N per 1 mol Ca(NO3)2, which doubles the moles.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

How many grams of oxygen are in 2.00 mol of H2SO4?

Hint

Moles of compound to moles of O (subscript ratio from the formula), then moles of O to grams with the molar mass of O atoms.

Answer

2.00 mol × 4 mol O / 1 mol H2SO4 = 8.00 mol O; × 15.999 g/mol = 128 g O.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

How many chloride ions are in 0.0500 mol of CaCl2?

Hint

Two steps: a subscript ratio (how many Cl− per CaCl2?) gives moles of chloride, then Avogadro's number gives ions.

Answer

0.0500 mol × 2 mol Cl−/mol CaCl2 = 0.100 mol Cl−; × 6.0221 × 1023 = 6.02 × 1022 Cl− ions.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

How many moles of phosphate ions, and how many moles of oxygen atoms, are in 0.0250 mol of Ca3(PO4)2?

Hint

Decide whether each part asks about the ion or an element inside it. One formula unit has two phosphate ions, and each phosphate holds four O atoms.

Answer

0.0250 × 2 = 0.0500 mol PO43−; 0.0250 × 8 = 0.200 mol O atoms.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

How many oxygen atoms are in 16.0 g of oxygen gas?

Hint

Diatomic trap: oxygen gas is O2. Grams to moles of O2, then moles of O atoms (2 per molecule), then atoms.

Answer

Oxygen gas is O2 (31.998 g/mol). 16.0 g ÷ 31.998 g/mol = 0.500 mol O2; × 2 = 1.00 mol O; × 6.0221 × 1023 = 6.02 × 1023 O atoms.
