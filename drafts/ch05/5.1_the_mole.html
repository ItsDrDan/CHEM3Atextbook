Learning Objectives

  • Explain why chemists count particles by weighing them.
  • State Avogadro's number and define the mole.
  • Convert between moles and numbers of atoms, molecules, or formula units.

Suppose a hardware store sells nails by the pound, and you need to know how many nails are in a 25-pound box. You would not count them. You would weigh one dozen nails, find that a dozen weighs, say, 0.17 lb, and use that as a conversion factor. Chemists face the same problem with atoms, but far worse: atoms are so small and so numerous that a single drop of water (about 0.05 mL) contains roughly 2 × 1021 water molecules. So chemists count atoms the same way, by weighing them in a standard-sized group. That group is the mole.

The Mole: A Chemist's Dozen

A dozen always means 12 of something: 12 eggs, 12 nails, 12 puppies. A mole (unit symbol mol) always means the same very large number of something:

1 mol = 6.0221 × 1023 particles

This number is Avogadro's number, NA, named for the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro. It is the value on your yellow sheet. Written out, it is 602,210,000,000,000,000,000,000. The "particles" can be anything, and you should always say which:

  • 1 mol of copper = 6.0221 × 1023 Cu atoms
  • 1 mol of water = 6.0221 × 1023 H2O molecules
  • 1 mol of sodium chloride = 6.0221 × 1023 NaCl formula units (ionic compounds have no molecules; Section 4.1)
  • 1 mol of sodium ions = 6.0221 × 1023 Na+ ions

A mole is a count of anything, not only atoms. One mole of puppies is 6.0221 × 1023 puppies, and 6.0221 × 1023 blue boxes is one mole of blue boxes. The unit only tells you how many; the word after it tells you what. If the name is hard to remember, Dr. Dan's trick is that "Avogadro" sounds like "avocado."

Why such an odd number? It was chosen so that the mass of one mole of an element in grams equals its atomic mass from the periodic table. One mole of carbon-12 atoms has a mass of 12 g, and one mole of natural carbon has a mass of 12.011 g. That link between counting and weighing is the subject of Section 5.2. (The mole is one of the seven SI base units, alongside the meter, kilogram, and second from Chapter 1.)

Converting Between Moles and Particles

Avogadro's number is a conversion factor, and it can be written either way up:

\( \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{particles}}{1\ \text{mol}} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{particles}} \)

Choose the form that cancels the unit you start with, just as in the dimensional analysis of Chapter 1.

Set Up Every Problem the Same Way: Given, Desire, Equality

Before you touch a calculator, write three things:

  1. Given: the number and unit the problem hands you, with the substance named (for example, "1.5 mol H2O").
  2. Desire: the unit you want, also with the substance named ("??? H2O molecules").
  3. Equality: the fact that links them, written as an equation first ("1 mol H2O = 6.0221 × 1023 H2O molecules").

Then start the setup with the given, the term that stands by itself, and turn the equality into a fraction with the given's unit on the bottom so it cancels. The worked examples in this chapter all use this layout. It looks slow for a one-step problem, but it is what keeps the three- and four-step problems in Sections 5.4 and 5.5 from falling apart.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Moles to molecules

Problem. How many water molecules are in 1.5 mol of water?

Given: 1.5 mol H2O. Desire: ??? H2O molecules. Equality: 1 mol H2O = 6.0221 × 1023 H2O molecules.

Solution. Moles are in the given, so mol goes on the bottom of the factor:

\( 1.5\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{H}_2\text{O molecules}}{1\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}} = 9.0 \times 10^{23}\ \text{H}_2\text{O molecules} \)

Check. More than one mole should give more than 6 × 1023. The answer has 2 significant figures, like 1.5 (Avogadro's number has 5).

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Atoms to moles

Problem. A gold bar contains 8.3 × 1025 gold atoms. How many moles of gold is that?

Given: 8.3 × 1025 Au atoms. Desire: ??? mol Au. Equality: 1 mol Au = 6.0221 × 1023 Au atoms.

Solution. This time atoms are in the given, so the same equality is flipped, atoms on the bottom:

\( 8.3 \times 10^{25}\ \text{Au atoms} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Au}}{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{Au atoms}} = 1.4 \times 10^{2}\ \text{mol Au} \)

Check. 8.3 × 1025 is about 100 times Avogadro's number, so about 100 mol makes sense (138 mol, rounded to 2 significant figures, is 1.4 × 102 mol).

Professor Tip

On the TI-36X Pro, enter Avogadro's number as 6.0221E23 using the ×10n key, never as 6.0221 × 10 ^ 23 typed out (that can break the order of operations when you divide). Then ask the sanity question every time: should my answer be a huge number (particles) or a small number (moles)? Moles in a lab sample are usually between about 0.001 and 10; particles are usually 1020 or more.

Biology Connection: Anatomy, Physiology, and Microbiology

Avogadro's number dwarfs even the biggest counts in biology. An adult human body has roughly 3.7 × 1013 cells, and each red blood cell carries roughly 270 million (2.7 × 108) hemoglobin molecules. In the microbiology lab, a dense overnight culture of E. coli may hold about 109 bacteria per milliliter, counted as colony-forming units (CFU). Yet one mole of water, only about 18 mL (a little over a tablespoon), contains about 16 billion times as many molecules as there are cells in your whole body. That is why molecules are counted in moles, while cells and bacteria are counted directly.

Common Mistakes

  • Using the factor upside down. If your answer for moles is near 1047 or your particle count is near 10−23, flip the factor.
  • Forgetting to say which particle. "1 mol of oxygen" could mean O atoms or O2 molecules; write which.
  • Skipping the "Desire." If you do not write what you are solving for, it is easy to stop one step early (at moles when the question wanted atoms).
  • Thinking a mole is a mass. A mole is a number of particles; its mass depends on the substance (Section 5.2).

Summary

  • 1 mol = 6.0221 × 1023 particles (Avogadro's number, NA).
  • Particles may be atoms, molecules, formula units, or ions; name them.
  • Multiply moles by NA to get particles; divide particles by NA to get moles.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many atoms are in 0.250 mol of iron?

Hint

Given: moles of Fe. Desire: Fe atoms. Equality: 1 mol = Avogadro's number of atoms. Write the factor with mol on the bottom so moles cancel.

Answer

0.250 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 atoms/mol = 1.51 × 1023 Fe atoms.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

How many moles of CO2 molecules are in 3.01 × 1024 molecules?

Hint

Particles are given, so put particles on the bottom of the Avogadro factor. Sanity check: is the count bigger or smaller than one mole's worth?

Answer

3.01 × 1024 ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 per mol = 5.00 mol CO2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which contains more particles: 1 mol of sand grains or 1 mol of hydrogen atoms?

Hint

A mole is a count, like a dozen. Does the kind of particle change how many are in one mole?

Answer

Neither. Both contain 6.0221 × 1023 particles; a mole is a number, like a dozen. (A mole of sand grains would just be enormously heavier.)
