Patient Case: Counting Mateo's Ions

Two numbers from Mateo Herrera's emergency visit (Chapters 2–4) are really counts of particles. His blood test reported sodium 138 mmol/L: 138 millimoles of sodium ions in every liter of blood plasma. And the IV bag of magnesium sulfate on his medication list was labeled with a mass in grams. Nobody can count 1021 ions one by one, and no balance weighs a single ion. Chemists connect the two worlds, the mass you can weigh and the number of particles you cannot see, with one unit: the mole.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to turn a laboratory value in millimoles into grams, find how many moles of magnesium ions are in a gram of magnesium sulfate, and see why a "mole" of salt and a "mole" of water have very different masses. (The case is for learning chemistry only; it is not treatment advice.)

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Explain the mole as a counting unit and convert between moles and numbers of particles with Avogadro's number (Section 5.1).
  • Calculate formula masses and molar masses of elements, compounds, and hydrates (Section 5.2).
  • Convert among mass, moles, and number of particles using the mole road map (Section 5.3).
  • Use subscripts as mole ratios to find the moles, mass, or number of atoms of an element in a compound (Section 5.4).
  • Plan and solve multi-step mole problems, including ones that use density or work backward from an element (Section 5.5).

Why This Chapter Matters

The mole is the bridge between the atoms of Chapters 2–4 and every calculation that follows: reaction amounts (Chapter 8), solution concentrations and IV fluids (Chapter 11), gas volumes, and acid doses. Health care uses it every day. Electrolytes, glucose, and cholesterol are reported in millimoles per liter in most of the world, and many drug and fluid orders are written in millimoles or milliequivalents. If you can move between grams and moles, you can read those numbers and check whether they make sense.
