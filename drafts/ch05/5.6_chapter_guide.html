Learning Objectives

  • Use the mole as a counting unit and Avogadro's number, 6.0221 × 1023, as a conversion factor.
  • Calculate formula masses (amu) and molar masses (g/mol), including hydrates.
  • Convert among grams, moles, and particles with the mole road map.
  • Use subscripts as mole ratios to find the moles, atoms, ions, or mass of an element in a compound.
  • Plan and solve multi-step mole problems with a four-step routine.

Key Ideas

"The goal is the mole": almost every problem in this chapter turns what you measured into moles first, then turns moles into what you want.

1. A mole is a number

1 mol = 6.0221 × 1023 particles, just as 1 dozen = 12. Always name the particle (atoms, molecules, formula units, ions). Multiply moles by NA to get particles; divide particles by NA to get moles. (Section 5.1)

2. Molar mass links grams and moles

Formula mass = sum of atomic masses in one formula (amu). Molar mass = the same number in g/mol. Count atoms carefully (parentheses, hydrates); round the sum to the fewest decimal places of the masses used. The numbers match because every atom is weighed against carbon-12. Read the formula: O is 15.999 g/mol, O2 is 31.998. Grams ÷ moles identifies an unknown element. (Section 5.2)

3. Moles are the hub

grams ÷ molar mass = moles; moles × NA = particles, and the reverse. Grams to particles always takes two steps. 1 mmol = 10−3 mol. Cue words pick the factor: a mass calls for molar mass, atoms or molecules call for Avogadro's number, another substance calls for a subscript ratio. For equal masses, the smaller molar mass gives more particles. (Section 5.3)

4. Subscripts are mole ratios

1 mol CO2 contains 1 mol C and 2 mol O. Use the ratio to move from moles of a compound to moles of an element or ion, then on to its mass or number of atoms. These ratios are exact. Watch for diatomic gases ("Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Beer": 1 mol Cl2 = 2 mol Cl) and for the difference between a polyatomic ion and the atoms in it. (Section 5.4)

5. Plan before you calculate

Given and Desire; map it and count the arrows; write each equality and flip it so units cancel; multiply the top, divide the bottom, round once. Density bridges grams and milliliters, the map runs backward from an element to its compound, and distractor numbers stay out. (Section 5.5)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Antacid calcium

Problem. A chewable antacid tablet contains 1.25 g of calcium carbonate, CaCO3. (a) Find the molar mass of CaCO3. (b) How many moles is that? (c) What mass of calcium does it contain?

Solution. (a) 40.08 + 12.011 + 3(15.999) = 100.088 → 100.09 g/mol. (b) 1.25 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 0.0125 mol. (c) 1 mol Ca per mol CaCO3: 0.01249 mol × 40.08 g/mol = 0.501 g Ca.

Check. Calcium is about 40% of the mass of CaCO3 (40.08 of 100.09), and 40% of 1.25 g is 0.50 g.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Three answers from one mass

Problem. For 3.40 g of ammonia, NH3, find (a) the moles of NH3, (b) the number of NH3 molecules, and (c) the number of H atoms.

Solution. Molar mass: 14.007 + 3(1.008) = 17.031 g/mol. (a) 3.40 ÷ 17.031 = 0.200 mol. (b) 0.1996 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.20 × 1023 molecules. (c) 3 H per molecule: 3.61 × 1023 H atoms.

Check. Part (c) is three times part (b) before rounding (3 × 1.2022 × 1023 = 3.61 × 1023), as the subscript requires.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Lab value in two units

Problem. A potassium level of 3.9 mmol/L (Mateo's value from Chapter 2) corresponds to what mass of potassium ions per liter?

Solution. 3.9 mmol = 0.0039 mol; 0.0039 mol × 39.10 g/mol = 0.15 g K+ per liter (150 mg/L).

Check. Potassium is mostly inside cells, so plasma carries far less K+ than Na+ (3.17 g/L in Section 5.3).

Common Mistakes

  • Moles are a count, not a mass; always name the particle.
  • Grams ÷ molar mass gives moles (check that grams cancel).
  • Grams to particles is two steps, through moles.
  • Multiply everything inside parentheses; add the water mass for hydrates.
  • Moles of compound are not moles of an element: use the subscript ratio.
  • Use 15.999 g/mol for O atoms, 31.998 g/mol only for O2 molecules; "oxygen gas" means O2.
  • Do not turn a mass into moles by relabeling it ("12 g" is not "12 mol").
  • Use the yellow-sheet masses as printed; round once at the end.
