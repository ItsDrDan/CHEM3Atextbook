These practice problems cover everything in Chapter 5: the mole as a counting unit and Avogadro's number; formula masses and molar masses of elements, compounds, and hydrates; conversions among mass, moles, and number of particles on the mole road map; and the subscripts of a formula as mole ratios, used to find the moles, number of atoms or ions, or mass of one element in a compound. They are grouped by section, and the last group mixes ideas from several sections (and from Chapter 4 naming and Chapter 1 unit conversions) the way an exam does. Keep your yellow sheet and your calculator next to you; use the atomic masses exactly as printed there and Avogadro's number, \( 6.0221 \times 10^{23} \) particles per mole. Try each problem on paper before you open the answer. Every answer shows the reasoning, and for multiple-choice problems it also explains why each wrong choice is wrong, so read the answer even when you got it right.

5.1 The Mole and Avogadro's Number

Problem 5.1

One mole of sodium chloride, NaCl, contains

A. 58.44 formula units of NaCl.
B. 6.0221 × 1023 formula units of NaCl.
C. 6.0221 × 1023 ions in total, half Na+ and half Cl−.
D. exactly 1 g of NaCl.

Answer

B. A mole of anything is 6.0221 × 1023 of that thing. NaCl is ionic, so its particles are formula units (Section 4.1), and one mole is 6.0221 × 1023 of them.

  • A mixes up the molar mass with the count: 58.44 is the mass of one mole in grams, not the number of formula units.
  • C undercounts the ions. Each formula unit holds one Na+ and one Cl−, so one mole of NaCl holds 6.0221 × 1023 of each ion, 1.2044 × 1024 ions in total.
  • D treats the mole as a mass. One mole of NaCl has a mass of 58.44 g; a mole is a number, not a mass.

Problem 5.2

An iron nail contains 0.0450 mol of iron. How many iron atoms is that?

Answer

Multiply moles by Avogadro's number, written with moles on the bottom so they cancel:

\( 0.0450\ \text{mol Fe} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{Fe atoms}}{1\ \text{mol Fe}} = 2.71 \times 10^{22}\ \text{Fe atoms} \)

The answer is 2.71 × 1022 Fe atoms, three significant figures like 0.0450 (the leading zeros are not significant; the trailing zero after the decimal point is). Less than one mole gives fewer than 6 × 1023 atoms, as expected.

Problem 5.3

A respiratory therapist collects the breath a patient exhales over about one minute; it contains 4.25 × 1021 molecules of carbon dioxide. How many moles of CO2 is that?

Answer

Divide by Avogadro's number (particles on the bottom of the factor):

\( 4.25 \times 10^{21}\ \text{molecules} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol CO}_2}{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}} = 7.06 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol CO}_2 \)

The answer is 7.06 × 10−3 mol (0.00706 mol, or 7.06 mmol). A count smaller than Avogadro's number must give less than one mole.

Problem 5.4

A glass of water contains 1.20 × 1024 water molecules. How many moles of water is that?

A. 7.23 × 1047 mol
B. 1.99 mol
C. 0.502 mol
D. 35.9 mol

Answer

B.

\( 1.20 \times 10^{24}\ \text{molecules} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}} = 1.99\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O} \)

The count is about twice Avogadro's number, so about 2 mol makes sense.

  • A multiplies by Avogadro's number instead of dividing. A number of moles near 1047 is the sign of an upside-down factor.
  • C divides the wrong way round, 6.0221 × 1023 ÷ 1.20 × 1024. The units come out as “per mole,” not moles, and the answer is too small for a count about twice Avogadro's number.
  • D goes one step too far: 1.99 mol × 18.015 g/mol = 35.9, which is the mass in grams, not the number of moles.

Problem 5.5

Each red blood cell carries roughly 2.7 × 108 hemoglobin molecules. How many moles of hemoglobin are in one red blood cell?

Answer

\( 2.7 \times 10^{8}\ \text{molecules} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}} = 4.5 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{mol hemoglobin} \)

The answer is 4.5 × 10−16 mol (two significant figures, set by 2.7). Even 270 million molecules is a tiny fraction of a mole, which is why biologists count cells directly but chemists count molecules in moles.

Problem 5.6

Compare 1.00 mol of hydrogen gas, H2, with 1.00 mol of oxygen gas, O2. Which statement is true?

A. They have the same number of molecules and the same mass.
B. They have the same number of molecules, but the O2 sample has the greater mass.
C. The O2 sample has more molecules, because oxygen molecules are heavier.
D. The H2 sample has more molecules, because hydrogen molecules are smaller.

Answer

B. One mole is always 6.0221 × 1023 particles, so both samples hold the same number of molecules. Each O2 molecule is heavier, so the oxygen sample weighs more (31.998 g versus 2.016 g; Section 5.2).

  • A is wrong: the same number of heavier particles has more mass, like a dozen bricks versus a dozen marbles.
  • C and D are wrong for the same reason: a mole is a fixed count, like a dozen. The size or mass of the particles does not change how many are in a mole.

Problem 5.7

One amoxicillin capsule (an antibiotic) contains 1.37 × 10−3 mol of amoxicillin. How many amoxicillin molecules are in the capsule?

Answer

\( 1.37 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 8.25 \times 10^{20}\ \text{molecules} \)

The answer is 8.25 × 1020 molecules. A small number of moles still holds an enormous number of molecules. On the TI-36X Pro, enter both numbers with the ×10n key: 1.37E-3 × 6.0221E23.

Problem 5.8

The table below lists samples of several substances, each with either the number of moles or the number of particles given.

Moles and particles to complete (blanks marked ?)
SampleKind of particleMolesNumber of particles
Copper wireCu atoms0.125 mol?
Neon signNe atoms?3.50 × 1022
Dry iceCO2 molecules2.40 mol?
Calcium ions in a lab solutionCa2+ ions?9.03 × 1020
Glucose in a sports drink sipC6H12O6 molecules0.00575 mol?

Fill in each blank.

Answer

Moles × 6.0221 × 1023 = particles; particles ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 = moles. Each answer has three significant figures, like the given value.

  • Cu: 0.125 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 7.53 × 1022 Cu atoms.
  • Ne: 3.50 × 1022 ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 = 0.0581 mol Ne.
  • CO2: 2.40 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.45 × 1024 CO2 molecules.
  • Ca2+: 9.03 × 1020 ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.50 × 10−3 mol Ca2+ (1.50 mmol).
  • Glucose: 0.00575 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 3.46 × 1021 glucose molecules.

Check: every sample under one mole has fewer than 6 × 1023 particles, and the 2.40 mol sample has more.

Problem 5.9

Which sample contains the greatest number of sodium atoms?

A. 0.10 mol of Na
B. 5.0 × 1022 Na atoms
C. 9.0 × 1021 Na atoms
D. 0.050 mol of Na

Answer

A. Put every choice in the same unit before comparing. 0.10 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 6.0 × 1022 atoms, which is more than any other choice.

  • B looks biggest only because 5.0 × 1022 is a large-looking number next to "0.10." In the same unit, 5.0 × 1022 is less than 6.0 × 1022.
  • C is 9.0 × 1021 atoms, about one-seventh of choice A.
  • D is half of choice A: 0.050 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 3.0 × 1022 atoms.

Problem 5.10

A student is asked how many atoms are in 1.50 mol of silver and reports 2.49 × 10−24 atoms. (a) What mistake did the student make? (b) What is the correct answer? (c) How could the student have caught the mistake?

Answer

(a) The student divided by Avogadro's number instead of multiplying: \( 1.50 \div (6.0221 \times 10^{23}) = 2.49 \times 10^{-24} \). The units show it: mol ÷ (atoms/mol) gives mol2/atom, not atoms.

(b) \( 1.50\ \text{mol Ag} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{Ag atoms}}{1\ \text{mol Ag}} = 9.03 \times 10^{23}\ \text{Ag atoms} \)

(c) A number of atoms can never be less than 1, and more than one mole must be more than 6 × 1023 atoms. Any particle count with a negative exponent is a flipped factor.

Problem 5.11

In the microbiology lab, a dense overnight culture of E. coli contains about 2.0 × 109 bacteria per milliliter. (a) What volume of culture, in milliliters, would contain one mole of bacteria? (b) Convert that volume to liters. (c) What does your answer say about why bacteria are counted directly rather than in moles?

Answer

(a) \( 6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{bacteria} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mL}}{2.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{bacteria}} = 3.0 \times 10^{14}\ \text{mL} \)

(b) \( 3.0 \times 10^{14}\ \text{mL} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \text{mL}} = 3.0 \times 10^{11}\ \text{L} \)

Two significant figures, set by 2.0 × 109 (1 L = 1000 mL is exact).

(c) Three hundred billion liters of culture is far more than any lab will ever grow. Cells and bacteria come in counts that are tiny compared with a mole, so they are counted directly (as cells or colony-forming units). The mole is useful only for things as small as atoms, ions, and molecules.

Problem 5.12

A balloon holds 0.20 mol of helium atoms. Which answer reports the number of helium atoms with the correct significant figures?

A. 1.2044 × 1023 atoms
B. 1.20 × 1023 atoms
C. 1.2 × 1023 atoms
D. 1 × 1023 atoms

Answer

C. In multiplication, the answer keeps the fewest significant figures of the measured values. 0.20 has 2 significant figures (the trailing zero after the decimal point counts), and Avogadro's number as given has 5, so the answer has 2: 0.20 × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.2 × 1023.

  • A copies every digit from the calculator (5 significant figures), more precision than 0.20 mol supports.
  • B has 3 significant figures; it would be right for 0.200 mol.
  • D has only 1 significant figure; it treats 0.20 as if it were 0.2.

5.2 Formula Mass and Molar Mass

Problem 5.13

Calculate the molar mass of (a) ammonia, NH3; (b) methane, CH4, the main gas in the natural gas burned in Valley homes and power plants (and released by dairy cattle); (c) sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO3, the antacid in baking soda. Use H 1.008, C 12.011, N 14.007, O 15.999, Na 22.99.

Answer

Multiply each atomic mass by its subscript (exact), add, and round the sum to the fewest decimal places among the atomic masses used.

(a) \( 14.007 + 3(1.008) = 14.007 + 3.024 = 17.031\ \text{g/mol} \)

(b) \( 12.011 + 4(1.008) = 12.011 + 4.032 = 16.043\ \text{g/mol} \)

(c) \( 22.99 + 1.008 + 12.011 + 3(15.999) = 84.006 \rightarrow 84.01\ \text{g/mol} \)

NH3 and CH4 use only masses with 3 decimal places, so they keep 3: 17.031 g/mol and 16.043 g/mol. NaHCO3 includes Na (22.99, 2 decimal places), so it keeps 2: 84.01 g/mol.

Problem 5.14

Calcium nitrate, Ca(NO3)2, is sold to Valley growers as a fertilizer that supplies both calcium and nitrogen. What is its molar mass? (Ca 40.08, N 14.007, O 15.999)

A. 82 g/mol
B. 102.08 g/mol
C. 116.09 g/mol
D. 164.09 g/mol

Answer

D. The 2 outside the parentheses multiplies everything inside, so one formula unit has 1 Ca, 2 N, and 6 O:

\( 40.08 + 2(14.007) + 6(15.999) = 40.08 + 28.014 + 95.994 = 164.088 \rightarrow 164.09\ \text{g/mol} \)

  • A adds atomic numbers (20 + 2 × 7 + 6 × 8 = 82) instead of atomic masses.
  • B ignores the subscript 2 after the parentheses and calculates CaNO3 (1 N, 3 O): 102.08.
  • C applies the 2 to nitrogen only, giving 2 N but just 3 O: 116.09.

Problem 5.15

A patient on supplemental oxygen breathes O2 molecules. What is the mass of one mole of O2 molecules?

A. 15.999 g
B. 31.998 g
C. 16 g
D. 5.3134 × 10−23 g

Answer

B. Each O2 molecule has two oxygen atoms, so its molar mass is 2(15.999) = 31.998 g/mol, and one mole has a mass of 31.998 g.

  • A is the mass of one mole of oxygen atoms, not O2 molecules.
  • C doubles oxygen's atomic number (8 × 2 = 16) instead of using its atomic mass.
  • D is the mass of a single O2 molecule (31.998 g ÷ 6.0221 × 1023), not of a whole mole.

Problem 5.16

Calcium chloride dihydrate, CaCl2·2H2O, is the form of calcium chloride usually stocked in the chemistry stockroom. Find its molar mass. (Ca 40.08, Cl 35.45, H 1.008, O 15.999)

Answer

The dot means "plus": add the mass of two water molecules to the mass of CaCl2.

\( \text{CaCl}_2:\ 40.08 + 2(35.45) = 110.98 \qquad 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}:\ 2(18.015) = 36.030 \)

\( 110.98 + 36.030 = 147.010 \rightarrow 147.01\ \text{g/mol} \)

The molar mass is 147.01 g/mol (2 decimal places, set by Ca and Cl). A common error is to multiply 110.98 by 2 or by 36.030; the waters are added, not multiplied.

Problem 5.17

Find the molar mass of each compound. (H 1.008, C 12.011, N 14.007, O 15.999, S 32.06, Cl 35.45, K 39.10, Ca 40.08)

(a) potassium chloride, KCl, a salt substitute and electrolyte replacement
(b) calcium carbonate, CaCO3, in antacid tablets and calcium supplements
(c) ascorbic acid (vitamin C), C6H8O6
(d) aspirin, C9H8O4
(e) ammonium sulfate, (NH4)2SO4, a nitrogen fertilizer

Answer
  • (a) 39.10 + 35.45 = 74.55 g/mol
  • (b) 40.08 + 12.011 + 3(15.999) = 40.08 + 12.011 + 47.997 = 100.088 → 100.09 g/mol
  • (c) 6(12.011) + 8(1.008) + 6(15.999) = 72.066 + 8.064 + 95.994 = 176.124 g/mol
  • (d) 9(12.011) + 8(1.008) + 4(15.999) = 108.099 + 8.064 + 63.996 = 180.159 g/mol
  • (e) 2 N, 8 H, 1 S, 4 O: 2(14.007) + 8(1.008) + 32.06 + 4(15.999) = 28.014 + 8.064 + 32.06 + 63.996 = 132.134 → 132.13 g/mol

Compounds made only of H, C, N, and O (3-decimal masses) keep 3 decimal places; those containing K, Ca, Cl, or S (2-decimal masses) keep 2. In (e), the 2 outside the parentheses doubles both N and H.

Problem 5.18

Caffeine has the molecular formula C8H10N4O2. (a) What is the molecular mass of one caffeine molecule, in amu? (b) What is the mass of one mole of caffeine, in grams? (Use your yellow sheet.)

Answer

(a) \( 8(12.011) + 10(1.008) + 4(14.007) + 2(15.999) = 96.088 + 10.080 + 56.028 + 31.998 = 194.194\ \text{amu} \)

(b) The same number, with the unit changed: 194.194 g. One molecule averages 194.194 amu, and one mole of molecules has a mass of 194.194 g. That is exactly what Avogadro's number was chosen to do.

Problem 5.19

Potassium sulfate, K2SO4, is used as a potassium fertilizer on Valley orchards. Using K 39.10, S 32.06, and O 15.999, which answer reports its molar mass correctly?

A. 135.16 g/mol
B. 174.256 g/mol
C. 174.26 g/mol
D. 174.3 g/mol

Answer

C. 2(39.10) + 32.06 + 4(15.999) = 78.20 + 32.06 + 63.996 = 174.256. This is an addition, so the answer keeps the fewest decimal places: K and S have 2, so the answer has 2, 174.26 g/mol.

  • A counts only one potassium: 39.10 + 32.06 + 63.996 = 135.16. The subscript 2 on K is missing.
  • B keeps 3 decimal places, but 39.10 and 32.06 are known only to the hundredths place.
  • D applies the multiplication rule (fewest significant figures, 4 from 39.10) to a sum. Sums follow the decimal-place rule, and subscripts are exact, so they never limit the answer.

Problem 5.20

Before an X-ray study of the stomach and intestines, a patient drinks a suspension of barium sulfate, BaSO4, which coats the lining of the digestive tract. (a) Find the molar mass of BaSO4. (Ba 137.33, S 32.06, O 15.999) (b) What fraction of that molar mass comes from barium? (c) Barium (56 electrons per atom) absorbs X-rays strongly. Which other element from Chapter 5 is used in contrast agents for the same reason?

Answer

(a) \( 137.33 + 32.06 + 4(15.999) = 137.33 + 32.06 + 63.996 = 233.386 \rightarrow 233.39\ \text{g/mol} \)

(b) \( 137.33 \div 233.39 = 0.5884 \): barium is about 59% of the mass.

(c) Iodine (53 electrons per atom), the element that iohexol and other CT contrast agents are built to carry (Section 5.2). Heavy atoms with many electrons block X-rays, so the coated organs show up bright on the image.

Problem 5.21

Why is the molar mass of an element, in g/mol, the same number as its average atomic mass, in amu?

A. Because Avogadro's number was chosen so that one mole of atoms has a mass in grams equal to the atomic mass in amu.
B. Because 1 amu and 1 g are the same mass.
C. Because every atom has a mass of 1 g.
D. It is true only for carbon-12; for every other element the numbers are different.

Answer

A. The mole is defined so that 6.0221 × 1023 atoms of mass x amu have a total mass of x grams. That is why the number under each symbol on the yellow sheet serves both as the atomic mass (amu) and the molar mass (g/mol).

  • B is far off: 1 g is 6.0221 × 1023 amu. A single atom is unimaginably lighter than a gram.
  • C is false: one atom has a mass of about 10−23 g or less (for example, one iron atom is 9.274 × 10−23 g).
  • D is false: carbon-12 sets the scale, but the match holds for every element and compound (for example, Na 22.99 amu and 22.99 g/mol).

Problem 5.22

Two common nitrogen fertilizers in the San Joaquin Valley are urea, CO(NH2)2, and ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3. (a) How many atoms of each element are in one formula of each compound? (b) Find each molar mass. (H 1.008, C 12.011, N 14.007, O 15.999)

Answer

(a) Urea: 1 C, 1 O, 2 N, and 4 H (the 2 after the parentheses doubles both N and H). Ammonium nitrate: 2 N (one in NH4+, one in NO3−), 4 H, and 3 O.

(b) Urea: \( 12.011 + 15.999 + 2(14.007) + 4(1.008) = 12.011 + 15.999 + 28.014 + 4.032 = 60.056\ \text{g/mol} \)

Ammonium nitrate: \( 2(14.007) + 4(1.008) + 3(15.999) = 28.014 + 4.032 + 47.997 = 80.043\ \text{g/mol} \)

Both compounds contain 2 mol of N per mole, but urea packs them into less mass, which is why urea has the higher nitrogen grade (46-0-0 versus 34-0-0; Section 5.4).

Problem 5.23

Calcium phosphate, Ca3(PO4)2, is related to the mineral in bones and teeth and is used in some calcium supplements. A student reports its molar mass as 215.21 g/mol. (a) What mistake did the student make? (b) What is the correct molar mass? (Ca 40.08, P 30.97, O 15.999)

Answer

(a) The student left out the subscript 2 after the parentheses and calculated Ca3PO4: 3(40.08) + 30.97 + 4(15.999) = 215.206 → 215.21.

(b) The 2 doubles everything in the parentheses: 3 Ca, 2 P, and 8 O.

\( 3(40.08) + 2(30.97) + 8(15.999) = 120.24 + 61.94 + 127.992 = 310.172 \rightarrow 310.17\ \text{g/mol} \)

The correct molar mass is 310.17 g/mol. Writing an atom-count table (element, number, mass, subtotal) before adding catches this mistake.

Problem 5.24

Fluoride toothpastes contain either sodium fluoride, NaF, or tin(II) fluoride (stannous fluoride), SnF2. Find the molar mass of each. (F 18.998, Na 22.99, Sn 118.71)

Answer

\( \text{NaF}:\ 22.99 + 18.998 = 41.988 \rightarrow 41.99\ \text{g/mol} \qquad \text{SnF}_2:\ 118.71 + 2(18.998) = 156.706 \rightarrow 156.71\ \text{g/mol} \)

Both answers keep 2 decimal places, set by Na (22.99) and Sn (118.71). The two F atoms in SnF2 come from the 2+ charge of tin(II) (Section 4.3).

5.3 Converting Between Mass, Moles, and Particles

Problem 5.25

A teaspoon of baking soda used as an antacid contains about 5.00 g of sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO3 (84.01 g/mol). How many moles is that?

Answer

\( 5.00\ \text{g NaHCO}_3 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol NaHCO}_3}{84.01\ \text{g NaHCO}_3} = 0.0595\ \text{mol NaHCO}_3 \)

The answer is 0.0595 mol (59.5 mmol), three significant figures like 5.00 g. Five grams is a small fraction of 84 g, so a small fraction of a mole makes sense.

Problem 5.26

A lab procedure calls for 0.0250 mol of calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (100.09 g/mol). What mass should you weigh out?

Answer

\( 0.0250\ \text{mol CaCO}_3 \times \dfrac{100.09\ \text{g CaCO}_3}{1\ \text{mol CaCO}_3} = 2.50\ \text{g CaCO}_3 \)

Weigh 2.50 g. One-fortieth of a mole should be about one-fortieth of 100 g, or 2.5 g.

Problem 5.27

An ibuprofen tablet contains 200. mg of ibuprofen, C13H18O2 (206.285 g/mol). How many millimoles of ibuprofen is that?

A. 0.970 mmol
B. 970. mmol
C. 4.13 × 104 mmol
D. 9.70 × 10−4 mmol

Answer

A. Because 1 mmol of ibuprofen has a mass of 206.285 mg (both units are scaled by 10−3), the molar mass can be used as 206.285 mg/mmol:

\( 200.\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol}}{206.285\ \text{mg}} = 0.970\ \text{mmol} \)

(The decimal point in "200." makes all three digits significant.) The same answer comes from 0.200 g ÷ 206.285 g/mol = 9.70 × 10−4 mol = 0.970 mmol.

  • B is a mg/g slip: it treats 200. mg as 200. g, which gives 0.970 mol, or 970. mmol, a thousand times too much.
  • C multiplies by the molar mass instead of dividing; the units would be mg2/mmol, not mmol.
  • D is the answer in moles (9.70 × 10−4 mol) mislabeled as millimoles, or divided by 1000 one time too many.

Problem 5.28

A patient's fasting blood glucose is reported as 126 mg/dL (milligrams per deciliter). Glucose, C6H12O6, has a molar mass of 180.156 g/mol. Convert the result to millimoles per liter (mmol/L), the unit used in most of the world. (1 L = 10 dL)

Answer

Convert dL to L, then mg to mmol (180.156 mg/mmol):

\( \dfrac{126\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{dL}} \times \dfrac{10\ \text{dL}}{1\ \text{L}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol}}{180.156\ \text{mg}} = 6.99\ \text{mmol/L} \)

The result is 6.99 mmol/L. Check with the shortcut from Section 5.3: 126 ÷ 18 ≈ 7. (Context only; results are always read in the units on the lab report.)

Problem 5.29

A total serum calcium result is 9.4 mg/dL. Convert it to mmol/L. (Ca 40.08; 1 L = 10 dL)

Answer

\( \dfrac{9.4\ \text{mg Ca}}{1\ \text{dL}} \times \dfrac{10\ \text{dL}}{1\ \text{L}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol Ca}}{40.08\ \text{mg Ca}} = 2.3\ \text{mmol/L} \)

The result is 2.3 mmol/L, two significant figures like 9.4 (the 10 dL per L is exact). The "divide by 18" shortcut works only for glucose; every substance needs its own molar mass.

Problem 5.30

Averaged over a day, an adult exhales roughly 38.5 g of carbon dioxide each hour. (a) How many moles of CO2 is that? (b) How many CO2 molecules? (C 12.011, O 15.999)

Answer

Molar mass of CO2: 12.011 + 2(15.999) = 44.009 g/mol.

(a) \( 38.5\ \text{g CO}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol CO}_2}{44.009\ \text{g CO}_2} = 0.875\ \text{mol CO}_2 \)

(b) \( 0.8748\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 5.27 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules} \)

(a) 0.875 mol; (b) 5.27 × 1023 molecules. Carrying one extra digit (0.8748) into part (b) and rounding once at the end avoids rounding error.

Problem 5.31

What is the mass, in grams, of a single iron atom? (Fe 55.85)

Answer

One mole of iron atoms (6.0221 × 1023 atoms) has a mass of 55.85 g, so

\( \dfrac{55.85\ \text{g Fe}}{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{Fe atoms}} = 9.274 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{g per atom} \)

The answer is 9.274 × 10−23 g (four significant figures, set by 55.85). In atomic mass units it is simply 55.85 amu.

Problem 5.32

The table below gives one quantity for each of four substances, with its molar mass.

Mass, moles, and particles to complete (blanks marked ?)
SubstanceMolar mass (g/mol)Mass (g)MolesNumber of particles
Water, H2O18.01545.0?? molecules
Sodium chloride, NaCl58.44?0.200? formula units
Glucose, C6H12O6180.156??3.00 × 1021 molecules
Carbon dioxide, CO244.0092.20?? molecules

Fill in each blank. Find your starting box on the mole road map first.

Answer

Grams ÷ molar mass = moles; moles × 6.0221 × 1023 = particles; and the reverse. Every answer has three significant figures, like the given values.

  • Water: 45.0 g ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 2.50 mol; 2.498 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.50 × 1024 molecules.
  • NaCl: 0.200 mol × 58.44 g/mol = 11.7 g; 0.200 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.20 × 1023 formula units.
  • Glucose: 3.00 × 1021 ÷ 6.0221 × 1023 = 4.98 × 10−3 mol; 4.982 × 10−3 mol × 180.156 g/mol = 0.897 g.
  • CO2: 2.20 g ÷ 44.009 g/mol = 0.0500 mol; 0.04999 mol × 6.0221 × 1023 = 3.01 × 1022 molecules.

For glucose, the route is particles → moles → grams: two steps, always through moles.

Problem 5.33

You have 10.0 g of each of the four substances below. Which sample contains the greatest number of molecules?

A. water, H2O (18.015 g/mol)
B. carbon dioxide, CO2 (44.009 g/mol)
C. methane, CH4 (16.043 g/mol)
D. All four contain the same number of molecules.

Answer

C. For the same mass, the substance with the smallest molar mass has the most moles, and so the most molecules: 10.0 g ÷ 16.043 g/mol = 0.623 mol CH4, or 3.75 × 1023 molecules. (Water: 0.555 mol, 3.34 × 1023; CO2: 0.227 mol, 1.37 × 1023.)

  • A is close but second: water's molar mass (18.015) is a little larger than methane's (16.043), so 10.0 g of water is slightly fewer moles.
  • B has it backward. Heavier molecules mean fewer of them fit into 10.0 g, so CO2 has the fewest.
  • D confuses equal masses with equal moles. Equal moles would have equal numbers of molecules; equal masses do not.

Problem 5.34

A low-dose aspirin tablet contains 81 mg of aspirin, C9H8O4 (180.159 g/mol). (a) How many millimoles of aspirin is that? (b) How many aspirin molecules?

Answer

(a) \( 81\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol}}{180.159\ \text{mg}} = 0.45\ \text{mmol} \)

(b) \( 0.4496\ \text{mmol} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{1000\ \text{mmol}} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 2.7 \times 10^{20}\ \text{molecules} \)

(a) 0.45 mmol; (b) 2.7 × 1020 molecules. Both answers have two significant figures, set by 81 mg. Avogadro's number is per mole, so millimoles must be changed to moles first.

Problem 5.35

A student is asked how many moles are in 12.0 g of methane, CH4 (16.043 g/mol), and writes "12.0 × 16.043 = 193 mol." (a) Explain the error using units. (b) Give the correct answer.

Answer

(a) Multiplying grams by grams per mole gives g2/mol, not mol: the grams do not cancel. A sanity check also fails: 12.0 g is less than the mass of one mole (16.043 g), so the answer must be less than 1 mol, not 193 mol.

(b) \( 12.0\ \text{g CH}_4 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol CH}_4}{16.043\ \text{g CH}_4} = 0.748\ \text{mol CH}_4 \)

The correct answer is 0.748 mol.

Problem 5.36

An 8 oz cup of brewed coffee contains about 95 mg of caffeine, C8H10N4O2 (194.194 g/mol). About how many caffeine molecules is that?

Answer

Change mg to g, then go through moles:

\( 95\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{g}}{1000\ \text{mg}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{194.194\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 2.9 \times 10^{20}\ \text{molecules} \)

About 2.9 × 1020 caffeine molecules (4.9 × 10−4 mol along the way; two significant figures, set by 95 mg).

Problem 5.37

What is the mass of 0.150 mol of carbon dioxide, CO2 (44.009 g/mol)?

A. 3.41 × 10−3 g
B. 6.60 g
C. 293 g
D. 9.03 × 1022 g

Answer

B. Moles to grams: multiply by the molar mass.

\( 0.150\ \text{mol CO}_2 \times \dfrac{44.009\ \text{g CO}_2}{1\ \text{mol CO}_2} = 6.60\ \text{g CO}_2 \)

  • A divides moles by molar mass (0.150 ÷ 44.009); the units come out mol2/g, not g.
  • C divides molar mass by moles (44.009 ÷ 0.150). Less than one mole must weigh less than 44 g, not more.
  • D multiplies by Avogadro's number. That gives the number of molecules (9.03 × 1022), not a mass in grams.

5.4 Moles and Mass Within a Formula

Problem 5.38

How many moles of oxygen atoms are in 0.50 mol of calcium nitrate, Ca(NO3)2?

A. 0.50 mol
B. 1.5 mol
C. 3.0 mol
D. 1.0 mol

Answer

C. One formula unit of Ca(NO3)2 contains 2 × 3 = 6 O atoms, so

\( 0.50\ \text{mol Ca(NO}_3)_2 \times \dfrac{6\ \text{mol O}}{1\ \text{mol Ca(NO}_3)_2} = 3.0\ \text{mol O} \)

  • A skips the subscript step: 0.50 mol is the amount of the compound, not of oxygen atoms.
  • B uses 3 O per formula unit and ignores the 2 outside the parentheses.
  • D is the moles of nitrogen atoms (2 N per formula unit), not oxygen.

Problem 5.39

A beaker holds 2.00 mol of water. (a) How many moles of hydrogen atoms does it contain? (b) How many hydrogen atoms? (c) How many oxygen atoms?

Answer

(a) Each H2O has 2 H atoms: 2.00 mol H2O × (2 mol H / 1 mol H2O) = 4.00 mol H.

(b) \( 4.00\ \text{mol H} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{H atoms}}{1\ \text{mol H}} = 2.41 \times 10^{24}\ \text{H atoms} \)

(c) 1 O per molecule: 2.00 mol O × 6.0221 × 1023 = 1.20 × 1024 O atoms, half as many as H atoms, matching the formula.

Problem 5.40

An iron supplement tablet contains 325 mg of ferrous fumarate, FeC4H2O4. Labels for iron products state the elemental iron, the mass of iron itself. (a) Find the molar mass of ferrous fumarate. (b) How many millimoles of ferrous fumarate are in the tablet? (c) What mass of elemental iron, in mg, does the tablet contain? (H 1.008, C 12.011, O 15.999, Fe 55.85)

Answer

(a) \( 55.85 + 4(12.011) + 2(1.008) + 4(15.999) = 55.85 + 48.044 + 2.016 + 63.996 = 169.906 \rightarrow 169.91\ \text{g/mol} \)

(b) \( 325\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol FeC}_4\text{H}_2\text{O}_4}{169.91\ \text{mg}} = 1.91\ \text{mmol} \)

(c) \( 1.913\ \text{mmol FeC}_4\text{H}_2\text{O}_4 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol Fe}}{1\ \text{mmol FeC}_4\text{H}_2\text{O}_4} \times \dfrac{55.85\ \text{mg Fe}}{1\ \text{mmol Fe}} = 107\ \text{mg Fe} \)

The tablet contains about 107 mg of elemental iron, about one-third of the 325 mg salt mass. That is why labels list the two masses separately. (Context only; follow the order and the product label.)

Problem 5.41

A calcium supplement tablet contains 1.25 g of calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (100.09 g/mol). What mass of calcium does it contain? (Ca 40.08)

A. 1.25 g
B. 0.501 g
C. 3.12 g
D. 0.250 g

Answer

B. Grams of compound → moles of compound → moles of Ca (1 : 1) → grams of Ca:

\( 1.25\ \text{g CaCO}_3 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol CaCO}_3}{100.09\ \text{g CaCO}_3} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Ca}}{1\ \text{mol CaCO}_3} \times \dfrac{40.08\ \text{g Ca}}{1\ \text{mol Ca}} = 0.501\ \text{g Ca} \)

That is about 501 mg, which is why a 1250 mg calcium carbonate tablet is labeled as providing about 500 mg of calcium.

  • A treats the mass of the salt as the mass of calcium. The carbonate is most of the tablet's mass.
  • C flips the last factors (1.25 × 100.09 ÷ 40.08). The calcium in a tablet can never weigh more than the whole compound.
  • D uses calcium's atomic number (20) in place of its molar mass (40.08 g/mol).

Problem 5.42

A stannous fluoride toothpaste contains 0.454% SnF2 by mass, so each 1.00 g of paste contains 4.54 mg of SnF2 (156.71 g/mol). (a) How many millimoles of SnF2 are in 1.00 g of paste? (b) How many millimoles of fluorine? (c) What mass of fluorine, in mg? (F 18.998) (d) Express your answer to (c) in parts per million (ppm), using 1 mg per g = 1000 ppm.

Answer

(a) \( 4.54\ \text{mg SnF}_2 \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol SnF}_2}{156.71\ \text{mg SnF}_2} = 0.0290\ \text{mmol SnF}_2 \)

(b) \( 0.02897\ \text{mmol SnF}_2 \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mmol F}}{1\ \text{mmol SnF}_2} = 0.0579\ \text{mmol F} \)

(c) \( 0.05794\ \text{mmol F} \times \dfrac{18.998\ \text{mg F}}{1\ \text{mmol F}} = 1.10\ \text{mg F} \)

(d) 1.10 mg of F per 1.00 g of paste is about 1100 ppm fluoride, the usual fluoride level of an over-the-counter toothpaste. Forgetting the 2 in SnF2 gives half the right answer, 0.550 mg.

Problem 5.43

A CT contrast agent labeled "300" contains 647 mg of iohexol, C19H26I3N3O9 (821.13 g/mol; Section 5.2), in each milliliter. (a) How many millimoles of iohexol are in 1 mL? (b) How many millimoles of iodine atoms? (c) What mass of iodine is in 1 mL? (I 126.90) (d) What does the "300" on the label mean?

Answer

(a) \( 647\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol iohexol}}{821.13\ \text{mg}} = 0.788\ \text{mmol iohexol} \)

(b) \( 0.7879\ \text{mmol iohexol} \times \dfrac{3\ \text{mmol I}}{1\ \text{mmol iohexol}} = 2.36\ \text{mmol I} \)

(c) \( 2.364\ \text{mmol I} \times \dfrac{126.90\ \text{mg I}}{1\ \text{mmol I}} = 3.00 \times 10^{2}\ \text{mg I} \)

(c) 300. mg of iodine (three significant figures, set by 647 mg). (d) The "300" is the iodine content, 300 mg of iodine per mL, not the iohexol content. Radiologic technologists read contrast strength as iodine, because the iodine atoms are what absorb the X-rays. Using 1 I per molecule instead of 3 gives 100. mg, a third of the true value.

Problem 5.44

Ionic compounds separate into their ions when they dissolve (Section 4.2), and the total number of dissolved particles matters for osmolality (Chapter 11). Suppose 0.100 mol of each compound in the table dissolves in water.

Moles of ions released by 0.100 mol of each compound (blanks marked ?)
CompoundMoles of cationMoles of anionTotal moles of ions
NaCl???
CaCl2???
K2SO4???
Na3PO4???
(NH4)2SO4???

Fill in each blank, and name the ions.

Answer

Use the subscripts as mole ratios; each polyatomic ion counts as one particle.

  • NaCl: 0.100 mol Na+, 0.100 mol Cl−, 0.200 mol of ions.
  • CaCl2: 0.100 mol Ca2+, 0.200 mol Cl−, 0.300 mol of ions.
  • K2SO4: 0.200 mol K+, 0.100 mol SO42−, 0.300 mol of ions.
  • Na3PO4: 0.300 mol Na+, 0.100 mol PO43−, 0.400 mol of ions.
  • (NH4)2SO4: 0.200 mol NH4+, 0.100 mol SO42−, 0.300 mol of ions.

A common mistake is to break sulfate or phosphate into separate S, P, and O atoms. In solution, SO42− and PO43− stay together as single ions.

Problem 5.45

What mass of oxygen is contained in 0.250 mol of CO2? (C 12.011, O 15.999)

A. 4.00 g
B. 8.00 g
C. 16.0 g
D. 11.0 g

Answer

B. Moles of CO2 → moles of O atoms → grams of O, using the molar mass of O atoms:

\( 0.250\ \text{mol CO}_2 \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol O}}{1\ \text{mol CO}_2} \times \dfrac{15.999\ \text{g O}}{1\ \text{mol O}} = 8.00\ \text{g O} \)

  • A forgets the subscript 2: 0.250 mol × 15.999 g/mol = 4.00 g.
  • C uses the molar mass of O2 (31.998 g/mol) for oxygen atoms, after already doubling the moles: 0.500 mol × 31.998 = 16.0 g. That counts every oxygen twice.
  • D is the mass of the whole CO2 sample (0.250 × 44.009 = 11.0 g), carbon included.

Problem 5.46

A grower in Fresno County buys a 25.0 kg bag of urea, CO(NH2)2 (60.056 g/mol). (a) How many moles of urea are in the bag? (b) How many moles of nitrogen atoms? (c) What mass of nitrogen, in kg, does the bag supply? (d) A 25.0 kg bag of ammonium sulfate, (NH4)2SO4 (132.13 g/mol), also has 2 N per formula unit. How many kg of nitrogen does it supply? (N 14.007)

Answer

(a) \( 25.0\ \text{kg} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol urea}}{60.056\ \text{g}} = 416\ \text{mol urea} \)

(b) \( 416.3\ \text{mol urea} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol N}}{1\ \text{mol urea}} = 833\ \text{mol N} \)

(c) \( 832.6\ \text{mol N} \times \dfrac{14.007\ \text{g N}}{1\ \text{mol N}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \text{g}} = 11.7\ \text{kg N} \)

(d) \( 25.0\ \text{kg} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{kg}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{132.13\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{2\ \text{mol N}}{1\ \text{mol}} \times \dfrac{14.007\ \text{g N}}{1\ \text{mol N}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \text{g}} = 5.30\ \text{kg N} \)

The urea bag supplies 11.7 kg of N; the ammonium sulfate bag only 5.30 kg, less than half as much. Both have 2 N per formula unit, but ammonium sulfate's formula is heavier (the sulfate adds mass but no nitrogen), so the same 25.0 kg holds fewer moles of it.

Problem 5.47

Each hemoglobin molecule holds 4 iron atoms, and hemoglobin has a molar mass of about 6.45 × 104 g/mol. An adult's blood contains about 7.0 × 102 g of hemoglobin. (a) How many moles of hemoglobin is that? (b) How many moles of iron? (c) What mass of iron is in the adult's hemoglobin? (Fe 55.85)

Answer

(a) \( 7.0 \times 10^{2}\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Hb}}{6.45 \times 10^{4}\ \text{g}} = 0.011\ \text{mol hemoglobin} \)

(b) \( 0.01085\ \text{mol Hb} \times \dfrac{4\ \text{mol Fe}}{1\ \text{mol Hb}} = 0.043\ \text{mol Fe} \)

(c) \( 0.04341\ \text{mol Fe} \times \dfrac{55.85\ \text{g Fe}}{1\ \text{mol Fe}} = 2.4\ \text{g Fe} \)

About 2.4 g of iron, two significant figures set by 7.0 × 102 g. That is most of the iron in an adult body (roughly two-thirds), which is why blood loss and iron deficiency are so closely linked.

Problem 5.48

How many atoms in total (C, H, and O combined) are in 0.100 mol of glucose, C6H12O6?

A. 6.02 × 1022 atoms
B. 3.61 × 1023 atoms
C. 7.23 × 1023 atoms
D. 1.45 × 1024 atoms

Answer

D. Each glucose molecule has 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 atoms, so

\( 0.100\ \text{mol C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \times \dfrac{24\ \text{mol atoms}}{1\ \text{mol C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{atoms}}{1\ \text{mol atoms}} = 1.45 \times 10^{24}\ \text{atoms} \)

  • A is the number of glucose molecules, not atoms; it skips the subscripts.
  • B counts only the 6 carbon atoms per molecule (0.600 mol C).
  • C counts only the 12 hydrogen atoms per molecule (1.20 mol H).

Problem 5.49

What mass of magnesium chloride, MgCl2, contains 0.100 mol of chloride ions? (Mg 24.31, Cl 35.45)

Answer

Work backward: moles of Cl− → moles of MgCl2 (2 Cl− per formula unit) → grams. The molar mass is 24.31 + 2(35.45) = 95.21 g/mol.

\( 0.100\ \text{mol Cl}^- \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol MgCl}_2}{2\ \text{mol Cl}^-} \times \dfrac{95.21\ \text{g MgCl}_2}{1\ \text{mol MgCl}_2} = 4.76\ \text{g MgCl}_2 \)

The answer is 4.76 g. Each formula unit supplies two chloride ions, so only half as many moles of MgCl2 (0.0500 mol) are needed. Skipping the ratio gives 9.52 g, twice too much.

Mixed Review

Problem 5.50

For each name, write the formula (Chapter 4) and find the molar mass. (N 14.007, O 15.999, P 30.97, K 39.10, Fe 55.85)

(a) potassium phosphate, a fertilizer and food additive
(b) iron(III) oxide, the main compound in rust
(c) dinitrogen monoxide ("laughing gas"), used for sedation in dental offices

Answer

(a) K+ and PO43− combine 3 : 1 to balance charge: K3PO4. Molar mass: 3(39.10) + 30.97 + 4(15.999) = 117.30 + 30.97 + 63.996 = 212.266 → 212.27 g/mol.

(b) Iron(III) is Fe3+ and oxide is O2−; 2 Fe3+ (6+) balance 3 O2− (6−): Fe2O3. Molar mass: 2(55.85) + 3(15.999) = 111.70 + 47.997 = 159.697 → 159.70 g/mol.

(c) A molecular compound: the prefixes give the subscripts directly, di- = 2 N and mono- = 1 O: N2O. Molar mass: 2(14.007) + 15.999 = 44.013 g/mol.

Problem 5.51

A potassium chloride extended-release tablet contains 750. mg of the salt. (a) Write the formula of potassium chloride. (b) Find its molar mass. (Cl 35.45, K 39.10) (c) How many millimoles of K+ does one tablet contain?

Answer

(a) K+ and Cl− combine 1 : 1: KCl.

(b) 39.10 + 35.45 = 74.55 g/mol (74.55 mg/mmol).

(c) \( 750.\ \text{mg KCl} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol KCl}}{74.55\ \text{mg KCl}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol K}^+}{1\ \text{mmol KCl}} = 10.1\ \text{mmol K}^+ \)

About 10.1 mmol of K+ (three significant figures; the decimal point in "750." makes the zero significant). Such tablets are labeled "10 mEq" (milliequivalents); for an ion with a 1+ charge, 1 mEq is the same as 1 mmol. (Context only; this is not dosing guidance.)

Problem 5.52

A 50.0 mL vial of 8.4% sodium hydrogen carbonate (sodium bicarbonate) injection contains 4.20 g of the compound. (a) Write its formula. (b) How many millimoles of the compound are in the vial? (c) How many millimoles per milliliter is that? (H 1.008, C 12.011, O 15.999, Na 22.99)

Answer

(a) Na+ and the hydrogen carbonate ion HCO3− (green sheet) combine 1 : 1: NaHCO3, 84.01 g/mol (found earlier in these problems).

(b) \( 4.20\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{84.01\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mmol}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 50.0\ \text{mmol NaHCO}_3 \)

(c) \( \dfrac{49.99\ \text{mmol}}{50.0\ \text{mL}} = 1.00\ \text{mmol/mL} \)

(b) 50.0 mmol; (c) 1.00 mmol/mL. Conveniently, the 8.4% strength makes each milliliter hold 1 mmol of bicarbonate, an easy number to work with. (Context only.)

Problem 5.53

A student is asked for the molar mass of ammonium phosphate. Which value is correct? (H 1.008, N 14.007, O 15.999, P 30.97)

A. 113.01 g/mol
B. 131.04 g/mol
C. 133.08 g/mol
D. 149.08 g/mol

Answer

D. Ammonium is NH4+ and phosphate is PO43−, so it takes three ammonium ions to balance one phosphate: (NH4)3PO4, with 3 N, 12 H, 1 P, and 4 O.

\( 3(14.007) + 12(1.008) + 30.97 + 4(15.999) = 42.021 + 12.096 + 30.97 + 63.996 = 149.083 \rightarrow 149.08\ \text{g/mol} \)

  • A is the molar mass of "NH4PO4," which ignores the charges: one 1+ ion cannot balance a 3− ion.
  • B is "(NH4)2PO4," which treats phosphate as 2− (like sulfate).
  • C is "(NH4)3PO3," which uses phosphite (PO33−) in place of phosphate. Check the ending: -ate is PO43− on the green sheet.

Problem 5.54

A vitamin C tablet contains 500. mg of ascorbic acid, C6H8O6 (176.124 g/mol). (a) Write the mass in grams and in kilograms, in scientific notation. (b) How many millimoles of ascorbic acid are in the tablet? (c) How many molecules?

Answer

(a) 500. mg × (1 g / 1000 mg) = 5.00 × 10−1 g, and 0.500 g × (1 kg / 1000 g) = 5.00 × 10−4 kg. (The decimal point in "500." makes all three digits significant.)

(b) \( 500.\ \text{mg} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mmol}}{176.124\ \text{mg}} = 2.84\ \text{mmol} \)

(c) \( 2.839 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 1.71 \times 10^{21}\ \text{molecules} \)

(b) 2.84 mmol; (c) 1.71 × 1021 molecules. In (c), 2.839 mmol was changed to 2.839 × 10−3 mol before using Avogadro's number.

Problem 5.55

Mateo's IV bag (chapter opener) contained 2.00 g of magnesium sulfate. (a) Write the formula of magnesium sulfate. (b) How many Mg2+ ions does the bag contain? (c) What mass of magnesium, in mg, is that? (d) How many ions in total are released when it dissolves? (O 15.999, Mg 24.31, S 32.06)

Answer

(a) Mg2+ and SO42− combine 1 : 1: MgSO4, molar mass 24.31 + 32.06 + 4(15.999) = 120.37 g/mol.

(b) \( 2.00\ \text{g} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol MgSO}_4}{120.37\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Mg}^{2+}}{1\ \text{mol MgSO}_4} \times \dfrac{6.0221 \times 10^{23}\ \text{ions}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 1.00 \times 10^{22}\ \text{Mg}^{2+}\ \text{ions} \)

(c) \( 0.01662\ \text{mol Mg}^{2+} \times \dfrac{24.31\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mol}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{g}} = 404\ \text{mg Mg} \)

(d) Each formula unit gives 2 ions (one Mg2+, one SO42−): 2 × 1.00 × 1022 = 2.00 × 1022 ions (0.0332 mol, as in Section 5.4).

(b) 1.00 × 1022 Mg2+ ions; (c) 404 mg of magnesium, about one-fifth of the 2.00 g salt mass. (Context only; this is not dosing guidance.)

Problem 5.56

"Normal saline," the most common IV fluid, contains 9.00 g of sodium chloride in each 1.00 L. (a) Write the formula of sodium chloride. (b) How many millimoles of Na+ are in 1.00 L? (c) What mass of sodium (the element) is in 1.00 L? (Na 22.99, Cl 35.45)

Answer

(a) NaCl (Na+ and Cl−, 1 : 1), 58.44 g/mol.

(b) \( 9.00\ \text{g NaCl} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol NaCl}}{58.44\ \text{g}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{mol Na}^+}{1\ \text{mol NaCl}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{mmol}}{1\ \text{mol}} = 154\ \text{mmol Na}^+ \)

(c) \( 0.1540\ \text{mol Na}^+ \times \dfrac{22.99\ \text{g Na}}{1\ \text{mol Na}} = 3.54\ \text{g Na} \)

(b) 154 mmol of Na+ per liter (and also 154 mmol of Cl−), close to the sodium level of blood plasma (about 135–145 mmol/L), which is one reason this fluid is used so often. (c) 3.54 g of sodium; the other 5.46 g of the 9.00 g is chlorine. (Context only.)
