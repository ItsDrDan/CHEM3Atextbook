Learning Objectives

  • Calculate the formula mass of a compound in atomic mass units.
  • Explain why the molar mass of an element in g/mol equals its atomic mass in amu.
  • Calculate molar masses of elements, molecular compounds, ionic compounds, and hydrates, with correct significant figures.

A dozen long nails weighs more than a dozen short nails, even though both are twelve nails. In the same way, a mole of mercury atoms weighs much more than a mole of carbon atoms: same number of particles, heavier particles. The mass of one mole of a substance is its molar mass, and it is the conversion factor that lets you count particles with a balance.

Formula Mass

Atoms are not created or destroyed when they combine, so the mass of a compound's formula is the sum of the masses of its atoms. The formula mass is the sum of the average atomic masses (from the periodic table, in amu) of all the atoms in one formula. For a molecular compound it is also called the molecular mass. You will also see the older names formula weight and molecular weight on labels, in lab manuals, and in other courses. They all refer to the same idea: add up the atoms.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Formula mass of calcium nitrate

Problem. What is the formula mass of calcium nitrate, Ca(NO3)2? Use the yellow-sheet masses Ca 40.08, N 14.007, O 15.999.

Solution. Count the atoms (Section 4.1): 1 Ca, 2 N, and 6 O. Then add:

\( 1(40.08) + 2(14.007) + 6(15.999) = 40.08 + 28.014 + 95.994 = 164.088 \rightarrow 164.09\ \text{amu} \)

Check. The subscripts are exact counts, so they do not limit significant figures. This is an addition, so the answer keeps the fewest decimal places: Ca has 2, so the answer has 2 (164.09 amu).

Why the Numbers Match: Weighing Atoms Against Carbon

Section 5.1 said that Avogadro's number was chosen so that grams and amu line up. Here is why that works. Start with carbon: 6.0221 × 1023 is (very nearly) the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12, so 1 mol C ≈ 12 g. Now compare other atoms with carbon, keeping the number of atoms the same (one mole of each):

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): One mole of each atom, compared with one mole of carbon
AtomHow heavy is one atom, compared with C?So one mole weighs aboutYellow-sheet atomic mass
Carbon, Cthe standard12 g12.011
Hydrogen, Habout 1/12 as heavy12 g × 1/12 ≈ 1 g1.008
Helium, Heabout 1/3 as heavy12 g × 1/3 ≈ 4 g4.003
Magnesium, Mgabout twice as heavy12 g × 2 ≈ 24 g24.31

Look at the last two columns. Because every atom was measured against the same carbon standard, the mass of a mole in grams comes out equal to the atomic mass printed on the periodic table. This is the same reason a dozen long nails weighs more than a dozen short nails: same count, heavier pieces.

From amu to Grams: Molar Mass

So one mole of atoms has a mass in grams equal to the atomic mass in amu:

  • One carbon atom averages 12.011 amu, and one mole of carbon atoms has a mass of 12.011 g.
  • One sodium atom averages 22.99 amu, and one mole of sodium has a mass of 22.99 g.

So the molar mass of an element, in grams per mole (g/mol, or g mol−1), is simply the number under its symbol on the periodic table. The molar mass of a compound is its formula mass with the unit g/mol. The same number answers two questions: "how heavy is one particle, in amu?" and "how heavy is one mole of particles, in grams?"

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Molar masses of compounds

Problem. Calculate the molar mass of (a) water, H2O; (b) sodium chloride, NaCl; (c) glucose, C6H12O6.

Solution. Use the yellow-sheet masses H 1.008, C 12.011, O 15.999, Na 22.99, Cl 35.45.

  • (a) 2(1.008) + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol
  • (b) 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol
  • (c) 6(12.011) + 12(1.008) + 6(15.999) = 72.066 + 12.096 + 95.994 = 180.156 g/mol

Check. Water and glucose use only masses with 3 decimal places, so their molar masses keep 3; NaCl uses masses with 2 decimal places, so it keeps 2.

Same Element, Different Molar Mass

Read the formula, not just the symbol. Oxygen atoms, the oxygen gas you breathe, and ozone are all "oxygen," but their particles contain 1, 2, and 3 atoms:

  • O atoms: 15.999 g/mol
  • O2 molecules (oxygen gas): 2(15.999) = 31.998 g/mol
  • O3 molecules (ozone): 3(15.999) = 47.997 g/mol

The subscript does all the work. The same is true for the other elements that exist as diatomic molecules: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, and I2. Dr. Dan's memory aid for the seven diatomics is "Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Beer": hydrogen, nitrogen, fluorine, oxygen, iodine, chlorine, bromine. When a problem says "oxygen gas" or "chlorine gas," it means the diatomic molecule, and you will see in Section 5.4 why that matters.

Professor Tip

Always use the atomic masses exactly as they are printed on your yellow sheet; do not round them first. Set up molar mass problems as a small table: element, number of atoms, atomic mass, and subtotal. It keeps parentheses straight (Ca(NO3)2 has six oxygen atoms) and makes your work easy to check. On the TI-36X Pro, type the whole sum on one line, for example 40.08+2×14.007+6×15.999, and round once at the end.

Hydrates

For a hydrate (Section 4.3), add the mass of the water molecules. Picture the waters sitting around the salt in the crystal, not multiplying it: the dot means "plus," not "times." Hydrates are usually the longest molar-mass sums you will do, so the table method in the Professor Tip below is worth using here.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Molar mass of a hydrate

Problem. Find the molar mass of copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate, CuSO4·5H2O. (Cu 63.55, S 32.06, O 15.999, H 1.008)

Solution. CuSO4: 63.55 + 32.06 + 4(15.999) = 159.606. Five waters: 5(18.015) = 90.075. Total: 159.606 + 90.075 = 249.681, or 249.68 g/mol.

Check. About 36% of the hydrate's mass is water (90.075 ÷ 249.68), which is why heating blue copper sulfate crystals noticeably reduces their mass.

Working Backward: Which Element Is It?

Molar mass is grams divided by moles. If a problem gives you both the mass and the number of moles of an unknown element, divide to get the molar mass, then find the element on the periodic table whose atomic mass matches.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Identify an unknown metal

Problem. A sample of 0.420 mol of a pure metal has a mass of 26.7 g. Which metal is it?

Given: 26.7 g and 0.420 mol of the same sample. Desire: molar mass (g/mol), then the element.

Solution.

\( \text{molar mass} = \dfrac{26.7\ \text{g}}{0.420\ \text{mol}} = 63.6\ \text{g/mol} \)

The closest atomic mass on the yellow sheet is copper, 63.55. The metal is copper.

Check. The neighbors are nickel (58.69) and zinc (65.39), both well away from 63.6, so the answer is clear.

Biology Connection: Molar Mass from a Structure

In biology and pharmacology you are often handed a drawing of a molecule instead of a formula. Count each kind of atom in the drawing (the carbons at each corner, the hydrogens, the nitrogens and oxygens) to write the formula, then add the masses. Serotonin, a neurotransmitter involved in mood, sleep, and digestion, has the formula C10H12N2O. With yellow-sheet masses its molar mass is 10(12.011) + 12(1.008) + 2(14.007) + 15.999 = 120.110 + 12.096 + 28.014 + 15.999 = 176.219 g/mol (every mass used has three decimal places, so the answer keeps three).

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

Iodine-based contrast agents for CT scans are large molecules built to carry several iodine atoms, because iodine, with 53 electrons per atom, absorbs X-rays strongly. Iohexol, a widely used contrast agent, has the formula C19H26I3N3O9. Using yellow-sheet masses, its molar mass is 19(12.011) + 26(1.008) + 3(126.90) + 3(14.007) + 9(15.999) = 821.13 g/mol, and the three iodine atoms contribute 380.70 g of that, almost half. Product labels state strength as milligrams of iodine per milliliter (for example, "300" means 300 mg of iodine per mL), so technologists must keep the drug's mass and the iodine's mass separate, a skill you will practice in Section 5.4.

Common Mistakes

  • Missing atoms inside parentheses. Al2(SO4)3 has 3 S and 12 O.
  • Reading "oxygen gas" as O. Oxygen gas is O2, 31.998 g/mol. Remember the seven diatomics.
  • Using the atomic number. The molar mass uses the decimal atomic mass (Na 22.99), not the atomic number (11).
  • Multiplying by the hydrate's water count wrongly. CuSO4·5H2O adds 5 × 18.015, it does not multiply the whole formula by 5.
  • Writing amu for a molar mass. One particle: amu. One mole: g/mol.

Summary

  • Formula (or molecular) mass = sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in the formula, in amu.
  • Molar mass = mass of one mole, in g/mol; numerically equal to the formula mass.
  • Molar mass = grams ÷ moles, so an unknown element can be identified from its mass and moles.
  • For hydrates, add the mass of the waters. Subscripts are exact; round the sum to the fewest decimal places of the masses used.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Find the molar mass of (a) CO2; (b) MgSO4; (c) Al2(SO4)3. (C 12.011, O 15.999, Mg 24.31, S 32.06, Al 26.98)

Hint

Count the atoms of each element (watch the parentheses in (c)), multiply each count by its atomic mass, add, and round to the fewest decimal places.

Answer

(a) 12.011 + 2(15.999) = 44.009 g/mol. (b) 24.31 + 32.06 + 4(15.999) = 120.366 → 120.37 g/mol. (c) 2(26.98) + 3(32.06) + 12(15.999) = 53.96 + 96.18 + 191.988 = 342.128 → 342.13 g/mol.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

What is the mass of one mole of oxygen molecules, O2? Of oxygen atoms?

Hint

Read the formula, not just the symbol: how many O atoms are in one O2 molecule?

Answer

O2: 2(15.999) = 31.998 g/mol. O atoms: 15.999 g/mol.

Each O2 molecule holds two O atoms, so a mole of O2 weighs twice oxygen's atomic mass, while a mole of O atoms weighs the atomic mass itself.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Epsom salt is MgSO4·7H2O. Find its molar mass.

Hint

The dot means add: find the molar mass of MgSO4, then add seven times the molar mass of water.

Answer

120.366 + 7(18.015) = 120.366 + 126.105 = 246.471 → 246.47 g/mol.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A 0.155 mol sample of a metal used in jewelry has a mass of 30.5 g. Identify the metal.

Hint

Molar mass is grams per mole. Divide the mass by the moles, then find the jewelry metal whose atomic mass on the yellow sheet is closest.

Answer

30.5 g ÷ 0.155 mol = 197 g/mol, which matches gold (196.97). Platinum (195.08) is close but further away.
