Learning Objectives

  • Describe and explain the trends in atomic radius down a group and across a period.
  • Define ionization energy and describe its trends.
  • Compare the sizes of atoms and their ions, and of ions with different charges.
  • Rank elements from different rows and columns by size or ionization energy by finding the extremes.
  • Relate the trends to metallic character.

The periodic table is more than a list. Because electron configurations repeat in a pattern, properties such as atomic size and how tightly an atom holds its electrons change in a predictable way as you move across a row or down a column. These patterns are periodic trends, and two of them explain most of what you need: atomic radius and ionization energy.

Two Ideas Behind Every Trend

  • Shells. Going down a group, each element adds a new shell. Valence electrons are in a higher shell, farther from the nucleus, and the inner (core) electrons partly shield them from the nucleus's pull.
  • Nuclear charge. Going across a period, each element adds one proton to the nucleus while its electrons go into the same shell. The added electrons shield each other poorly, so the valence electrons feel a stronger pull, called a larger effective nuclear charge.

Atomic Radius

The atomic radius is a measure of an atom's size: half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms that are bonded together.

  • Down a group, atomic radius increases. Each step down adds a shell, so the valence electrons are farther out. Lithium is smaller than sodium, which is smaller than potassium.
  • Across a period (left to right), atomic radius decreases. The growing nuclear charge pulls the same shell in more tightly. Carbon (6 protons), nitrogen (7), and oxygen (8) all keep their valence electrons in shell 2, but each extra proton pulls that shell in a little more, so the atoms get "smushed": C > N > O in size. Likewise, sodium is larger than magnesium, which is larger than chlorine.

So the largest atoms are at the lower left of the table (cesium, francium) and the smallest at the upper right (helium, fluorine).

Plotting atomic radius against atomic number shows both trends at once: the radius jumps up at the start of each new period and falls across it.

Ionization Energy

The ionization energy is the energy needed to remove one electron from a gaseous atom, forming a 1+ ion:

\( \text{Na}(g) + \text{energy} \rightarrow \text{Na}^{+}(g) + e^{-} \)

It measures how tightly an atom holds its outermost electron, and it runs opposite to atomic radius:

  • Down a group, ionization energy decreases. The valence electron is farther from the nucleus and better shielded, so it is easier to remove.
  • Across a period, ionization energy increases. The valence electrons are held more tightly by the larger effective nuclear charge.

Noble gases have the highest ionization energies in their rows (helium has the highest of all), which fits their full shells and lack of reactivity. Alkali metals have the lowest, which is why they lose an electron so easily. Hydrogen is the exception on the left side: it sits in group 1, but its single electron is in the first shell, very close to the nucleus, so its ionization energy is high, much higher than lithium's.

Ionization energy is effort, like getting yourself to the gym: the harder it is to pull an electron off, the more energy it takes. Compare sodium and fluorine through the octet rule. Sodium needs to lose just one electron to reach an octet. Fluorine could reach an octet only by losing seven, or by gaining one. Which is easier: handing over one $10 bill, or handing over seven of them? Giving away one electron is easy, so sodium's ionization energy is low. Fluorine needs only one more electron and holds its seven tightly (its atoms are also small, so the electrons sit close to the nucleus), so its ionization energy is high.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Ranking atomic radius

Problem. Rank Mg, S, and Ba in order of increasing atomic radius.

Solution. Mg and S are both in period 3; S is farther right, so S is smaller than Mg. Ba is below Mg in group 2 (period 6), so Ba is larger than Mg. Order: S < Mg < Ba.

Check. The smallest is the one farthest up and right (S); the largest is the one farthest down and left (Ba).

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Ranking ionization energy

Problem. Which has the highest first ionization energy: K, Ca, or Br? Which has the lowest?

Solution. All three are in period 4, so ionization energy increases left to right: K < Ca < Br. Br has the highest and K the lowest.

Check. K is an alkali metal that easily forms K+; Br is a halogen that would rather gain an electron than lose one.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Mixing a row and a column

Problem. Rank Ga, P, and S from smallest to largest atomic radius.

Solution. P and S are both in period 3, and S is farther right, so S < P. Ga (gallium) is in period 4, one shell lower, and farther left (group 13). Down and to the left both mean bigger, so Ga is the largest. Order: S < P < Ga.

Check. Look for the extremes: the element nearest the upper right (S) is smallest, and the one nearest the lower left (Ga) is largest.

Professor Tip

Learn one trend and get the other free. Remember "atoms get bigger going down and to the left." Ionization energy is the opposite: big atoms hold their outer electrons loosely, so ionization energy is highest at the upper right. When a question mixes a row and a column, sketch a tiny periodic table, place each element, and use the arrows. On multiple-choice questions, look for the extremes: the answer is usually the element farthest toward a corner (lower left or upper right). If you find yourself picking an element in the middle, recheck the trend.

The Size of Ions

Gaining or losing electrons changes an atom's size.

  • Cations are smaller than their atoms. Na+ has lost its only 3s electron, so its outermost electrons are in shell 2, and the 11 protons now pull on just 10 electrons.
  • Anions are larger than their atoms. Cl− has one more electron than protons, so the electrons repel each other more and the cloud spreads out.

A money picture helps. Suppose you have only $5 to your name and someone takes $1. That is 20% of everything you have, so you hold on tight to the rest and keep it close. A cation is like that: after losing electrons, it has more protons than electrons, and the nucleus pulls the remaining electrons in. Now suppose an extra paycheck arrives. You relax and spread out a little. An anion is like that: the extra electrons push on each other, each wants more personal space, and the electron cloud grows.

A bigger charge makes the effect bigger. With the same number of electrons, a 2+ ion is smaller than a 1+ ion, and a 2− ion is larger than a 1− ion. Na+, Mg2+, F−, and O2− all have 10 electrons, yet their sizes run Mg2+ < Na+ < F− < O2−, because the number of protons pulling on those 10 electrons drops from 12 to 11 to 9 to 8. For the same element, too, Fe3+ is smaller than Fe2+.

Metallic Character

Metals tend to lose electrons, so metallic character is greatest where ionization energy is lowest: toward the lower left of the table. That matches what you saw in Chapter 2, where metals sit on the left and nonmetals on the upper right, with the metalloids along the staircase between them.

Biology Connection: Physiology

Ion size matters inside your body. Na+ and K+ carry the same 1+ charge, but K+ has one more shell, so it is larger. Cell membranes contain protein ion channels that are remarkably selective: potassium channels let K+ through more than a thousand times more readily than Na+, even though Na+ is smaller. The channel's narrow filter is lined with oxygen atoms spaced to fit a K+ ion once it sheds the water molecules around it; a smaller Na+ ion does not fit as snugly and stays with its water. This size-based sorting is what lets nerve and heart cells keep different concentrations of Na+ and K+ on each side of the membrane and fire electrical signals.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking more electrons always means a bigger atom. Across a period, electrons are added but the atom shrinks, because protons are added too.
  • Running ionization energy the same way as radius. They run in opposite directions.
  • Thinking cations are larger than their atoms. Losing electrons (often a whole shell) makes the ion smaller, and a 2+ ion is smaller still.
  • Picking a middle element on a ranking question. Look for the extremes; the largest atom and lowest ionization energy are toward the lower left.
  • Forgetting hydrogen. It sits in group 1 but has a high ionization energy.

Summary

  • Atomic radius increases down a group (more shells) and decreases across a period (greater effective nuclear charge).
  • Ionization energy, the energy to remove an electron, decreases down a group and increases across a period.
  • Cations are smaller than their atoms (they "hoard" what is left); anions are larger (more repulsion). A larger charge exaggerates the effect: for 10-electron ions, Mg2+ < Na+ < F− < O2−.
  • Metallic character increases toward the lower left.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which is larger: (a) N or P; (b) Na or Cl; (c) Ca or Ca2+?

Hint

Atoms get bigger going down and to the left. For (c), a cation is smaller than its atom: which electrons did Ca lose?

Answer

(a) P (one more shell). (b) Na (same shell, smaller nuclear charge). (c) Ca (the ion has lost its 4s shell).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Rank Li, Cs, and F in order of increasing ionization energy.

Hint

Ionization energy is highest at the upper right and lowest at the lower left. Place each element on a tiny sketch of the table.

Answer

Cs < Li < F. Cs is farthest down group 1; F is farthest right in period 2.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Explain in one sentence why atoms get smaller from left to right across a period.

Hint

Across a period, protons are added but the electrons stay in the same shell. How does the nucleus's pull change?

Answer

Each step adds a proton while the electrons go into the same shell, so the stronger nuclear pull draws the valence electrons closer.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Put these ions in order from smallest to largest: S2−, K+, Cl−, Ca2+. (All have 18 electrons.)

Hint

All four have 18 electrons, so compare the protons pulling on them. More protons pull the same electrons in tighter.

Answer

Ca2+ < K+ < Cl− < S2−. The protons pulling on the same 18 electrons drop from 20 to 19 to 17 to 16, so the ions get larger in that order.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Which has the lower ionization energy, magnesium or chlorine? Explain with the octet rule.

Hint

Count each element's steps to the nearest noble gas: which one reaches an octet by losing electrons, and which by gaining?

Answer

Magnesium. It reaches an octet by losing just two electrons, so they come off easily; chlorine is one electron short of an octet and holds its own electrons tightly in a smaller atom.
