Learning Objectives

  • Name the parts of a wave (wavelength, frequency, amplitude); relate wavelength, frequency, and photon energy; use \( c = \lambda\nu \) and \( E = h\nu \); order the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum.
  • Explain line spectra with the Bohr model (excitation and relaxation between fixed energy levels); calculate an energy gap from a line's wavelength; draw Bohr diagrams for atoms and ions.
  • Describe shells, subshells, and orbitals; draw orbital diagrams with the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle, and Hund's rule; tell ground-state, excited-state, and impossible configurations apart.
  • Write full and noble-gas electron configurations for main-group atoms and ions, plus the chromium and copper exceptions.
  • Count valence electrons, draw electron-dot (Lewis) symbols for atoms and simple ions, and use the octet rule to explain main-group ion charges.
  • Predict trends in atomic radius, ionization energy, ion size, and metallic character.

Key Ideas

1. Short wavelength, high energy

Light travels at \( c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1} \), and \( c = \lambda\nu \), so wavelength and frequency are inversely related. One photon carries \( E = h\nu = hc/\lambda \), with \( h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s} \). Amplitude is brightness (a dimmer switch), not energy per photon. Calm-day waves (long wavelength) arrive rarely; storm waves (short wavelength) arrive often. Convert nanometers to meters first. From radio waves to gamma rays: wavelength goes down, frequency and energy go up. Visible light runs from red (about 700 nm) to violet (about 400 nm). (Section 3.1)

2. Electrons live on energy "stairs"

In the Bohr model, electrons occupy fixed energy levels (n = 1, 2, 3, ...). Absorbing energy moves an electron up (excitation); falling back down releases a photon (relaxation). Each element's fixed gaps give its own line spectrum. A bigger drop gives a shorter-wavelength, higher-energy photon, and \( \Delta E = hc/\lambda \) (656 nm → 3.03 × 10−19 J). Like exact change at a toll booth, an electron can come down in one step or several, which is why there are several lines. Every dark absorption line sits at the same wavelength as a bright emission line (an atom can emit any photon it absorbs), but a cool gas does not absorb every line it can emit. Bohr diagrams put 2, 8, 8 electrons on the first three rings; Na+ (2, 8) looks like neon, Cl− (2, 8, 8) like argon. (Section 3.2)

3. Shells, subshells, orbitals

Shell n contains n kinds of subshell (s, p, d, f) with 1, 3, 5, and 7 orbitals, holding 2, 6, 10, and 14 electrons. Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons. Fill order (diagonal method): 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s .... Aufbau: lowest energy first. Pauli: 2 per orbital, opposite spins. Hund: one per orbital before pairing, like roommates who each take a bedroom before anyone doubles up. Ground state follows Aufbau (except Cr and Cu, Section 3.4); He* = 1s12s1 is an excited state; 2s3, 3p7, 1p, and 2d are impossible. (Section 3.3)

4. The periodic table is a configuration map

s block = groups 1–2 (and He); p block = groups 13–18; d block = groups 3–12 (shell = row − 1); f block = below the table (shell = row − 2). Read across the rows like a book to write a configuration, and check that the superscripts add to the electron count. Shorthand: [previous noble gas] + the rest. Write the full configuration only when asked for "complete" or "full." Cations lose electrons from the highest shell (and highest subshell) first; anions add them. Main-group ions are usually isoelectronic with a noble gas. Know the two exceptions: Cr is [Ar] 4s1 3d5 and Cu is [Ar] 4s1 3d10, because a half-filled or filled d subshell is extra stable, so one 4s electron moves to 3d (never end in s2 d4 or s2 d9). (Section 3.4)

5. Valence electrons decide the chemistry

Valence electrons are in the outermost shell: groups 1–2 have 1–2; groups 13–18 have 3–8 (group − 10; helium: 2). Same group, same valence pattern, similar chemistry. Octet rule: metals lose electrons (1+, 2+, Al 3+); nonmetals gain them (3−, 2−, 1−) to reach a noble-gas configuration. Lewis symbols show only valence electrons, one dot per side before pairing; anions get extra dots in brackets with the charge, and cations are written as empty brackets with the charge. (Section 3.5)

6. Size and grip run opposite

Atomic radius increases down a group (more shells) and to the left across a period (less nuclear pull). Ionization energy does the opposite: highest at the upper right, lowest at the lower left. Cations are smaller than their atoms (lose $1 of your last $5 and you hold the rest close); anions are larger (an extra paycheck spreads you out), and bigger charges exaggerate both. On ranking questions, look for the extremes; hydrogen has a high ionization energy despite sitting in group 1. Metallic character increases toward the lower left. (Section 3.6)

Worked Examples

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Newborn jaundice phototherapy

Problem. Phototherapy lights for newborn jaundice use blue light; one lamp is rated at 458 nm, a wavelength the yellow pigment bilirubin absorbs. Find the frequency and the energy of one photon.

Solution. \( \lambda = 458\ \text{nm} = 4.58 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} \).

\( \nu = \dfrac{c}{\lambda} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}}{4.58 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 6.55 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1} \)

\( E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(6.550 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 4.34 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \)

Check. Blue light has a shorter wavelength than the 660 nm red light of a pulse oximeter, so it should carry more energy per photon: \( 4.34 \times 10^{-19} > 3.01 \times 10^{-19} \) J ✓.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): From orbital diagram to ion

Problem. For aluminum, (a) draw the orbital diagram, (b) write the noble-gas configuration, (c) give the number of valence electrons, and (d) predict the ion and its configuration.

Solution. Al has 13 electrons. (a) 1s [↑↓] 2s [↑↓] 2p [↑↓][↑↓][↑↓] 3s [↑↓] 3p [↑][  ][  ]. (b) [Ne] 3s2 3p1. (c) 3 valence electrons (group 13). (d) Al loses its 3 valence electrons: Al3+, configuration [Ne].

Check. 13 − 3 = 10 electrons, the same as neon.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Identify from a configuration

Problem. An atom has the configuration [Ar] 4s2 3d10 4p4. Identify the element, its group, its valence electrons, and its common ion.

Solution. Electrons: 18 + 2 + 10 + 4 = 34, so Z = 34, selenium. It ends in p4, so it is in group 16 with 2 + 4 = 6 valence electrons (the 3d electrons are in a lower shell). It gains 2 electrons to form Se2−.

Check. Oxygen and sulfur, above it in group 16, also end in p4 and form 2− ions.

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Trends side by side

Problem. For Na, Cl, and K, rank (a) atomic radius and (b) first ionization energy from smallest to largest.

Solution. Na and Cl are in period 3 (Cl farther right); K is below Na in group 1. (a) Radius: Cl < Na < K. (b) Ionization energy runs the opposite way: K < Na < Cl.

Check. The largest atom (K) holds its valence electron most loosely, so it has the lowest ionization energy.

Common Mistakes

  • Convert nm to m before using \( c = \lambda\nu \).
  • Longer wavelength means lower energy.
  • Bright lines come from electrons dropping; going up absorbs energy.
  • Hund's rule: one electron per orbital before pairing; paired electrons have opposite spins.
  • 4s fills before 3d; in row 4 the d block is 3d.
  • Chromium and copper are exceptions: [Ar] 4s1 3d5 and [Ar] 4s1 3d10, not s2 d4 or s2 d9.
  • Superscripts must add up to the number of electrons.
  • Valence electrons for groups 13–18 = group number minus 10 (helium: 2); d electrons do not count for main-group elements.
  • Atomic radius and ionization energy run in opposite directions; cations are smaller and anions larger than their atoms.
  • An excited state (an electron moved up) is not "impossible"; impossible means a subshell over capacity or one that does not exist.
  • Lewis symbols: valence electrons only, one dot per side before pairing, brackets and charge on ions.
  • Amplitude changes brightness, not the energy of each photon.
