Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish a continuous spectrum from a line (emission) spectrum, and explain why absorption and emission spectra of an element match.
  • Describe the Bohr model: electrons in fixed energy levels, excitation, and relaxation.
  • Explain why each element gives off its own pattern of colors, and relate a larger energy jump to a shorter wavelength.
  • Calculate the energy gap between two levels from the wavelength of the light emitted.
  • Draw Bohr diagrams (electrons per ring) for the first 20 elements and their simple ions.
  • State what the Bohr model gets right and where it falls short.

Heat a pinch of table salt in a flame and the flame turns bright yellow-orange. Copper salts turn it blue-green, and strontium salts turn it red, the red of fireworks. Each element gives off its own colors, as dependable as a fingerprint. Explaining why took a new picture of the atom, one in which electrons can have only certain energies.

Continuous and Line Spectra

When white light from the sun or a light bulb passes through a prism, it spreads into a smooth rainbow with every color blending into the next: a continuous spectrum. When the light from a glowing gas, such as hydrogen in an electric discharge tube, passes through a prism, you see something very different: a few sharp, bright lines of color on a dark background. This is a line spectrum, or emission spectrum. Each element has its own set of lines, like a bar code, so chemists can identify elements by the light they give off. The same idea lets astronomers tell what distant stars are made of.

The Bohr Model

In 1913 Niels Bohr explained hydrogen's line spectrum with a new model of the atom. Its main ideas are:

  • The electron moves around the nucleus only in certain allowed orbits, called energy levels, numbered n = 1, 2, 3, and so on. An electron can be in one level or another, but never in between. Energy that comes only in certain fixed amounts is called quantized, like standing on the steps of a staircase but never between steps.
  • Level n = 1 is closest to the nucleus and lowest in energy. The lowest-energy arrangement of an atom's electrons is its ground state.
  • Excitation: when the atom absorbs exactly the right amount of energy (from heat, electricity, or light), the electron jumps up to a higher level, an excited state. Think of an elevator: going from the first floor to the second takes some work from the motor, and going from the first floor to the fifth takes much more. Does moving an electron to a bigger orbit, farther from the nucleus, need energy? Yes: the negative electron is attracted to the positive nucleus (Section 1.7), so pulling them farther apart takes energy.
  • Relaxation: an excited electron soon drops back to a lower level and gives off the energy difference as one photon of light. An excited atom, like an anxious person, always looks for a way to relax; for an atom, the way to relax is to release a photon.

Because the levels have fixed energies, the energy gaps between them are fixed too. A photon given off in a jump has an energy equal to the gap, so it has one particular wavelength. That is why an element emits only certain colors. In hydrogen, the red line at 656 nm comes from electrons dropping from n = 3 to n = 2, and the blue-green line at 486 nm comes from n = 4 to n = 2.

Follow one hydrogen lamp from the wall plug to your eye. Plugging in the lamp sends electricity through the hydrogen gas, which excites electrons, for example from n = 1 up to n = 3. An electron at n = 3 relaxes by dropping to n = 2 and releasing one photon, and that photon's energy matches the 3 → 2 gap, which is red light at 656 nm. Billions of atoms doing the same thing make the red glow you see.

The toll-road analogy

Why does an atom give several lines instead of one? Picture a toll road where the booth accepts exact change only, and you carry only $5, $10, and $20 bills. If the toll is $5, there is only one way to pay: one $5 bill. If the toll is $20, there are several ways: one $20, two $10s, a $10 and two $5s, or four $5s. An electron coming down from a high level is like paying a large toll. It can drop all the way in one step, giving off one high-energy photon, or in several smaller steps, giving off several lower-energy photons. Each step must match one gap exactly (exact change; no partial payments), so each step gives one particular wavelength. Add up all the ways electrons can step down, and you get the set of lines in the element's spectrum, its bar code.

Professor Tip

Connect the jump size to the color. A bigger drop in energy gives a higher-energy photon, which means a shorter wavelength. In hydrogen, the drop from n = 4 to n = 2 is bigger than the drop from n = 3 to n = 2, so its line (486 nm, blue-green) has a shorter wavelength than the red 656 nm line. Up arrows absorb energy; down arrows give off light.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Absorb or emit?

Problem. For each electron jump in a hydrogen atom, say whether energy is absorbed or emitted: (a) n = 1 to n = 3; (b) n = 5 to n = 2; (c) n = 2 to n = 1.

Solution. Moving up to a higher level needs energy; moving down releases it. (a) up: absorbed. (b) down: emitted as a photon. (c) down: emitted.

Check. Only downward jumps produce the bright lines of an emission spectrum.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Which line has more energy?

Problem. Hydrogen's visible lines are at 410, 434, 486, and 656 nm. Which line comes from the photon with the most energy, and which energy-level drop is the biggest?

Solution. The shortest wavelength has the most energy: the 410 nm (violet) line. The most energetic photon comes from the biggest drop, so 410 nm belongs to the biggest jump of the four (it is the drop from n = 6 to n = 2).

Check. Using \( E = hc/\lambda \) for the 410 nm line:

\( E = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^{8})}{4.10 \times 10^{-7}} = 4.8 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \)

That is larger than the \( 3.0 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \) of the 656 nm line.

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): The energy gap behind a spectral line

Problem. A hydrogen atom emits red light with a wavelength of 656 nm. How big is the energy gap between the two levels involved?

Given: \( \lambda = 656\ \text{nm} \). Desire: the energy gap, \( \Delta E \), in J. Key idea: the photon carries away exactly the energy of the gap, so \( \Delta E = E_{\text{photon}} \).

Solution. Convert the wavelength to meters, \( 656\ \text{nm} \times \dfrac{10^{-9}\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{nm}} = 6.56 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} \), then use \( E = hc/\lambda \) from Section 3.1:

\( \Delta E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1})}{6.56 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 3.03 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \)

On the TI-36X Pro, type it on one line: 6.626E-34 × 3E8 ÷ 656E-9.

Check. Meters and seconds cancel, leaving joules. The answer is close to the 3.01 × 10−19 J of Section 3.1's 660 nm red photon; 656 nm is a slightly shorter wavelength, so slightly more energy is expected. It has 3 significant figures, set by 656 nm and 3.00 × 108.

Absorption and Emission Spectra Match

Light can also go the other way. If white light shines through a cool gas, the atoms absorb exactly the photons whose energies match their gaps, and those colors go missing. The result is an absorption spectrum: a full rainbow with a few dark lines. For mercury (and every other element), every dark line in the absorption spectrum sits at exactly the same wavelength as a bright line in its emission spectrum, because the same gaps are involved: an atom can emit any photon it absorbs. Excitation uses the gap going up; relaxation releases it coming down. The match does not run the other way for every line, though. A cool gas does not absorb every line it can emit: its atoms sit in the ground state, so they absorb mainly the photons that lift an electron up from there. An absorption spectrum usually shows fewer dark lines than the emission spectrum has bright ones.

In the Lab: Experiment 3a, Electrons in Atoms

In the CHEM 3A lab you look at gas discharge tubes through a spectroscope, a small instrument with a slit, a diffraction grating that spreads light into its colors, and a scale in nanometers. You record the wavelengths of the bright lines you see for hydrogen and other elements, compare them with accepted values, and connect each line to an electron dropping between Bohr energy levels. You also put the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum in order. Read the lines carefully: the scale gives you a measured wavelength with its own uncertainty, the same idea as reading a buret in Chapter 1.

Drawing Bohr Diagrams for Atoms and Ions

A Bohr diagram is a quick sketch that organizes all three subatomic particles: the nucleus in the center, labeled with its protons and neutrons, and the electrons as dots on rings (the energy levels) around it. The Bohr model's physics works only for hydrogen, but the diagram is still a handy way to count electrons by level, and it sets up the idea of valence electrons in Section 3.5.

To draw one for any of the first 20 elements:

  1. Nucleus. Protons = atomic number Z. Neutrons = mass number − Z (round the atomic mass to a whole number if no isotope is given).
  2. Electrons. For a neutral atom, electrons = Z. For an ion, electrons = Z − charge.
  3. Fill the rings like reading a book. Each ring matches a row (period) of the periodic table, and for the first 20 elements it holds as many electrons as there are elements in that row of the main groups: 2 in the first ring (H, He), 8 in the second (Li to Ne), 8 in the third (Na to Ar), and the last 1 or 2 in the fourth ring (K, Ca). Fill the inner ring before starting the next, and stop when you run out of electrons.

The table above shows the pattern that the next sections explain. Sodium loses its single outer electron and is left with neon's arrangement; chlorine gains one electron and reaches argon's. The nucleus does not change when the ion forms: only the electrons do (Section 2.4).

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Bohr diagrams for magnesium and its ion

Problem. Describe the Bohr diagrams of a magnesium-24 atom and of the Mg2+ ion.

Solution. Magnesium has Z = 12, so the nucleus holds 12 protons and 24 − 12 = 12 neutrons in both diagrams. The atom has 12 electrons: 2 in ring 1, 8 in ring 2, and the last 2 in ring 3, written 2, 8, 2. Mg2+ has 12 − 2 = 10 electrons, so the third ring is empty: 2, 8, the same as neon.

Check. The atom's rings add to 2 + 8 + 2 = 12 = Z, and the ion's add to 10 = 12 − 2. Magnesium is in group 2, and the two electrons it loses are exactly the two in its outer ring.

Beyond the Bohr Model

The Bohr model predicts hydrogen's lines very well, and its central idea, that electrons have only certain allowed energies, is still correct. But it fails for atoms with more than one electron, and electrons do not actually travel in neat circular orbits. The modern quantum-mechanical model keeps the numbered energy levels but describes where an electron is likely to be found as a cloud-shaped region called an orbital. Section 3.3 uses that model.

Biology Connection: Anatomy, Physiology, and Microbiology

Your eyes work because molecules absorb only certain wavelengths, the same idea as electrons jumping between fixed levels. In the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye, rods handle dim light and three kinds of cones respond best to different ranges of wavelength (roughly blue, green, and red). Each holds a pigment molecule, retinal, that changes shape when it absorbs a photon of the right energy, which starts a nerve signal. In the microbiology lab, fluorescence microscopy works in a similar way: a fluorescent stain absorbs light of one wavelength and almost immediately gives off light of a longer, lower-energy wavelength, so stained bacteria or cell parts glow against a dark background.

Major Connection: Engineering

A light-emitting diode (LED) is a solid-state version of an emission spectrum: electrons drop across a fixed energy gap in a semiconductor and give off photons of one color. Engineers choose materials with bigger gaps for blue LEDs and smaller gaps for red ones, the same "bigger drop, shorter wavelength" rule you just used.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking an absorbed photon produces a bright line. Bright emission lines come from electrons dropping down; going up absorbs energy.
  • Linking bigger jumps to longer wavelengths. A bigger energy drop gives a shorter wavelength.
  • Thinking electrons can sit between levels. In the Bohr model the energies are quantized; there is nothing in between. Exact change only.
  • Thinking absorption and emission lines are different. Every dark absorption line sits at the same wavelength as a bright emission line of the same element (though a cool gas does not absorb every line it can emit).
  • Putting too many electrons in a Bohr ring. For the first 20 elements the rings hold 2, 8, 8 (then 2 more in the fourth ring for K and Ca), read like a book from the rows of the periodic table.

Summary

  • White light gives a continuous spectrum; a glowing element gives a line (emission) spectrum unique to that element.
  • In the Bohr model, electrons occupy fixed energy levels. Absorbing energy moves an electron up (excitation); dropping down releases a photon (relaxation).
  • Each line corresponds to one energy gap, \( \Delta E = hc/\lambda \); bigger gaps give higher-energy, shorter-wavelength light. Like exact change at a toll booth, an electron can come down in one big step or several small ones, which is why there are several lines.
  • Every dark line in an element's absorption spectrum sits at the same wavelength as a bright line in its emission spectrum (an atom can emit any photon it absorbs), but a cool gas does not absorb every line it can emit.
  • Bohr diagrams put 2, 8, 8 electrons in the first three rings; an ion's outer ring matches a noble gas.
  • The Bohr model works for hydrogen; the quantum-mechanical model with orbitals works for all atoms.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Why does a sodium street lamp give off light of only a few colors instead of a full rainbow?

Hint

In the Bohr model, electrons can sit only in certain energy levels. What does that mean for the possible energy drops, and so for the wavelengths?

Answer

Sodium's electrons can only occupy certain energy levels, so only certain energy drops, and therefore only certain wavelengths, are possible. Sodium's strongest lines are yellow-orange, near 589 nm.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

An electron drops from n = 4 to n = 1 in one atom and from n = 4 to n = 3 in another. Which photon has the longer wavelength?

Hint

Which drop is the smaller energy gap? Less energy means a longer wavelength (E = hc/λ).

Answer

The n = 4 to n = 3 drop is much smaller, so its photon has less energy and the longer wavelength.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

What is the difference between the ground state and an excited state?

Hint

Think about which energy levels the electrons occupy. What must happen for an electron to reach a higher level?

Answer

In the ground state the electrons are in the lowest energy levels available. In an excited state at least one electron has absorbed energy and moved to a higher level.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

A sodium lamp gives off yellow light at 589 nm. What is the energy gap, in joules, between the two levels involved?

Hint

The photon's energy equals the gap between the levels. Convert 589 nm to meters and use ΔE = hc/λ.

Answer

\( \Delta E = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1})}{5.89 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 3.37 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \)

Slightly larger than the 656 nm red gap, as a shorter wavelength should be.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{5}\)

Give the electrons per ring for (a) a nitrogen atom, (b) the N3− ion, (c) a potassium atom, and (d) the K+ ion.

Hint

Count the electrons first (a charge changes them: Z − charge), then fill the rings 2, then 8, then 8, then the rest.

Answer

(a) N, 7 electrons: 2, 5. (b) N3−, 10 electrons: 2, 8 (like neon). (c) K, 19 electrons: 2, 8, 8, 1. (d) K+, 18 electrons: 2, 8, 8 (like argon).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{6}\)

A gas absorbs light at 434 nm when white light passes through it. At what wavelength would you expect a bright line when the same gas glows in a discharge tube?

Hint

Absorption and emission use the same energy gaps. Going back down across the same gap gives what wavelength?

Answer

At 434 nm. Absorption and emission use the same energy gaps, and an atom can emit any photon it absorbs, so the dark absorption line has a matching bright emission line at the same wavelength.
