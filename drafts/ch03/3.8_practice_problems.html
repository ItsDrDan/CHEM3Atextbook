3.1 Light and the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Problem 3.1

Moving through the electromagnetic spectrum from radio waves toward gamma rays, which statement is true?

A. Wavelength increases and photon energy increases.
B. Wavelength decreases and frequency increases.
C. Frequency decreases and photon energy increases.
D. The waves travel faster.

Hint

Use c = λν (c is fixed) and E = hν. Which way does the wavelength change from radio waves to gamma rays?

Answer

B. Radio waves are the longest waves and gamma rays the shortest. Because \( c = \lambda\nu \) and \( c \) is fixed, a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency, and by \( E = h\nu \) a higher photon energy.

  • A is wrong: wavelength gets shorter toward gamma rays. Wavelength and energy never rise together.
  • C is wrong: frequency and energy always change in the same direction, because \( E = h\nu \). Both increase toward gamma rays.
  • D is wrong: all electromagnetic radiation travels at the same speed in a vacuum, \( 3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1} \).

Problem 3.2

Many newborns develop jaundice, a yellow color of the skin caused by a buildup of bilirubin, a yellow pigment made when old red blood cells are broken down. In phototherapy, the baby lies under blue light. Suppose a phototherapy unit's blue LEDs (light-emitting diodes) give off light with a peak wavelength of 458 nm. Bilirubin in the skin absorbs these photons, which changes it into forms the body can get rid of more easily. (a) What is the frequency of 458 nm light? (b) What is the energy of one photon? (c) Would a photon from a red 660 nm lamp carry more or less energy? Explain without a calculation.

Hint

(a) Convert nm to meters, then ν = c/λ. (b) E = hν. (c) Compare wavelengths: a longer wavelength means a lower frequency and energy.

Answer

(a) Convert to meters first: \( 458\;\text{nm} \times \dfrac{10^{-9}\;\text{m}}{1\;\text{nm}} = 4.58 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m} \). Then

\( \nu = \dfrac{c}{\lambda} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1}}{4.58 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m}} = 6.55 \times 10^{14}\;\text{s}^{-1} \)

The frequency is 6.55 × 1014 Hz (three significant figures, set by 458 and 3.00).

(b) \( E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(6.550 \times 10^{14}\;\text{s}^{-1}) = 4.34 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} \)

(Carrying one extra digit of the frequency and rounding once at the end avoids rounding error.)

(c) Less. Red light has a longer wavelength, so a lower frequency and less energy per photon. (Section 3.1 found 3.01 × 10−19 J for a 660 nm photon.)

Problem 3.3

A radiologic technologist takes a chest radiograph. One of the X-ray photons in the beam has a wavelength of 0.0250 nm. (a) Write the wavelength in meters, in scientific notation. (b) Find its frequency. (c) Find its energy. (d) About how many times more energy does it carry than a 458 nm phototherapy photon, which carries 4.34 × 10−19 J?

Hint

(a) Multiply by 10−9 m per nm. (b) ν = c/λ. (c) E = hν. (d) Divide the X-ray photon's energy by the phototherapy photon's energy.

Answer

(a) \( 0.0250\;\text{nm} \times \dfrac{10^{-9}\;\text{m}}{1\;\text{nm}} = 2.50 \times 10^{-11}\;\text{m} \)

(b) \( \nu = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1}}{2.50 \times 10^{-11}\;\text{m}} = 1.20 \times 10^{19}\;\text{Hz} \)

(c) \( E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(1.20 \times 10^{19}\;\text{s}^{-1}) = 7.95 \times 10^{-15}\;\text{J} \)

(d) \( \dfrac{7.95 \times 10^{-15}\;\text{J}}{4.34 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J}} = 1.83 \times 10^{4} \)

The X-ray photon carries about 18,000 times as much energy. That is enough to knock electrons out of atoms, which is why X-rays are ionizing radiation and why exposure is kept as low as possible.

Problem 3.5

A 1.5-tesla MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) scanner uses radio waves with a frequency of 63.9 MHz (1 MHz = 106 Hz). (a) What is the wavelength, in meters? (b) What is the energy of one of these photons? (c) Use your answer to explain why MRI is not ionizing radiation, even though it uses electromagnetic waves.

Hint

(a) Change MHz to Hz, then λ = c/ν. (b) E = hν. (c) Compare that energy with the visible and ultraviolet photons in 3.1.

Answer

(a) \( 63.9\;\text{MHz} = 6.39 \times 10^{7}\;\text{Hz} \), and \( \lambda = \dfrac{c}{\nu} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1}}{6.39 \times 10^{7}\;\text{s}^{-1}} = 4.69\;\text{m} \)

(b) \( E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(6.39 \times 10^{7}\;\text{s}^{-1}) = 4.23 \times 10^{-26}\;\text{J} \)

(c) Each radio photon carries about ten million times less energy than a 458 nm visible photon (4.34 × 10−19 J ÷ 4.23 × 10−26 J ≈ 1 × 107), and far less than an X-ray photon. That is much too little energy to knock an electron out of an atom or break a bond, so radio waves are not ionizing.

Problem 3.6

Compare a 254 nm photon from a germicidal UV-C lamp with a 940 nm photon from a pulse oximeter. Which statement is correct?

A. The UV-C photon has a higher frequency and more energy.
B. The infrared photon has more energy, because its wavelength is longer.
C. The two photons have the same energy, because both travel at the speed of light.
D. The UV-C photon has a lower frequency but more energy.

Hint

Use ν = c/λ and E = hν to decide how frequency and energy change as wavelength changes. Then compare 254 nm with 940 nm.

Answer

A. The UV-C photon has the shorter wavelength, so it has the higher frequency (\( \nu = c/\lambda \)) and the higher energy (\( E = h\nu \)). By the ratio of wavelengths, 940 ÷ 254 = 3.7, it carries about 3.7 times the energy. (The zero in 940 has no decimal point, so 940 has two significant figures.)

  • B has the relationship backward: longer wavelength means less energy.
  • C is wrong: all light has the same speed, but energy depends on frequency, and these frequencies are different.
  • D is impossible: a photon with more energy must have a higher frequency, since \( E = h\nu \).

Problem 3.7

A phototherapy lamp is switched to a higher intensity setting, so it is brighter, but its color stays at 458 nm. What changes?

A. Each photon carries more energy.
B. More photons reach the skin each second, but each photon has the same energy.
C. The wavelength becomes shorter.
D. The frequency increases.

Hint

Which property of light sets the energy of one photon (E = hν)? Did that property change when the lamp got brighter but kept its color?

Answer

B. Brightness (intensity) is the number of photons. The energy of each photon depends only on its wavelength, and the wavelength has not changed.

  • A is wrong: the photon energy is set by \( E = hc/\lambda \), and \( \lambda \) is still 458 nm.
  • C is wrong: the color, and so the wavelength, stays the same.
  • D is wrong: with the same wavelength, \( \nu = c/\lambda \) gives the same frequency.

(b) Which band has the more energetic photons, and by what factor? (c) Healthy leaves absorb most of one band and reflect most of the other. Which band do they reflect strongly?

(a) Convert 865 nm to meters; ν = c/λ, then E = hν. (b) The energy ratio is the inverse of the wavelength ratio. (c) Which band does chlorophyll use for photosynthesis?

Answer

(a) \( 865\;\text{nm} = 8.65 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m} \)

\( \nu = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1}}{8.65 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m}} = 3.47 \times 10^{14}\;\text{Hz} \)

(c) Near-infrared. Leaves absorb red light for photosynthesis and reflect near-infrared, so a healthy canopy looks bright in the 865 nm image and dark in the red one.

3.2 Emission Spectra and the Bohr Model

Problem 3.10

Which light source, viewed through a prism or spectroscope, gives a line spectrum?

A. sunlight
B. the glowing filament of an old-style incandescent light bulb
C. a sodium vapor street lamp
D. a red-hot electric stove burner

Hint

Line spectra come from separate atoms whose electrons drop between fixed energy levels. For each source, is the glowing material a hot solid or a gas of one element?

Answer

C. The lamp holds sodium atoms as a glowing gas. Their electrons can drop only between certain energy levels, so the lamp gives off only certain wavelengths: a few bright lines, the strongest near 589 nm.

  • A is wrong: sunlight spreads into a continuous rainbow.
  • B is wrong: a hot solid filament gives a continuous spectrum.
  • D is wrong: the hot burner is also a hot solid. It glows with a continuous spread of wavelengths, mostly infrared plus some red.

Problem 3.12

A hydrogen electron absorbs a photon and jumps from n = 2 to n = 4. A moment later it drops straight back from n = 4 to n = 2. How does the photon it gives off compare with the photon it absorbed?

A. It has the same energy and the same wavelength.
B. It has more energy, because the electron is falling.
C. It has a longer wavelength, because emitted light is always redder.
D. No photon is given off when the electron drops.

Hint

Both jumps connect the same two levels. What sets the energy of a photon that is absorbed or given off?

Answer

A. The energy gap between n = 4 and n = 2 is fixed. Going up takes exactly that much energy, and coming down gives back exactly that much, as one photon of the same wavelength (486 nm, blue-green).

  • B is wrong: the electron cannot give off more energy than the gap between the two levels.
  • C is wrong for a single atom dropping back between the same two levels. (A fluorescent molecule can give off a longer wavelength because it loses some energy first, as in the fluorescent stain problem later in this section, but that is not what happens here.)
  • D is wrong: dropping to a lower level is exactly how the atom gives off a photon.

Problem 3.13

Many older street lights in Central Valley towns are sodium vapor lamps, which glow yellow-orange at 589 nm. (a) What is the energy of one 589 nm photon? (b) In one sentence, explain why a sodium lamp gives off nearly one color instead of a full rainbow.

Hint

(a) Convert to meters and use E = hc/λ. (b) What does the Bohr model say about sodium's allowed energy levels?

Answer

(a) \( 589\;\text{nm} = 5.89 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m} \), and

\( E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1})}{5.89 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m}} = 3.37 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} \)

(b) Sodium's electrons can occupy only certain energy levels, so only certain energy drops are possible, and the strongest of them always gives a photon of the same energy, which is light of the same wavelength, 589 nm.

Problem 3.14

Hydrogen's blue-green line at 486 nm comes from the drop n = 4 to n = 2, and its violet line at 434 nm comes from n = 5 to n = 2. (a) Find the energy of each photon. (b) Which of the two drops is bigger? (c) Use your answers to find the energy gap between n = 5 and n = 4. (d) An electron drops from n = 5 to n = 4. Find the wavelength of the photon, and name its region of the spectrum.

Hint

(a) E = hc/λ for each line, in meters. (b) More energy means a bigger drop. (c) Subtract the two energies. (d) λ = hc/E for that gap; then check the spectrum region.

Answer

(a) \( E_{486} = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1})}{4.86 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m}} = 4.09 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} \)

\( E_{434} = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1})}{4.34 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m}} = 4.58 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} \)

(b) The n = 5 to n = 2 drop: its photon has more energy (shorter wavelength).

(c) Both drops end at n = 2. The 5 → 2 drop is the 5 → 4 step plus the 4 → 2 step, so the difference between them is the 5 → 4 gap:

\( 4.58 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} - 4.09 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} = 0.49 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} = 4.9 \times 10^{-20}\;\text{J} \)

The subtraction leaves two decimal places in the 10−19 numbers, so the gap has two significant figures.

(d) \( \lambda = \dfrac{hc}{E} = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1})}{4.9 \times 10^{-20}\;\text{J}} = 4.1 \times 10^{-6}\;\text{m} \)

That is about 4100 nm, far longer than red light (700 nm): infrared. A small drop gives a low-energy, long-wavelength photon, and the levels get closer together as n increases.

Problem 3.15

In flame tests, a strontium salt turns the flame red, a sodium salt turns it yellow-orange, and a copper salt turns it blue-green. (a) Rank the three colors from lowest to highest photon energy. (b) Which element's light comes from the largest energy drop? (c) Why does each element give its own color?

Hint

Order the colors by wavelength (ROY G BV runs long to short); shorter wavelength means more energy. Each element has its own set of energy levels.

(a) From long wavelength to short (ROY G BV): red (strontium) < yellow-orange (sodium) < blue-green (copper).

Problem 3.16

Which statement about the Bohr model is correct?

A. It explains hydrogen's line spectrum, and its idea of fixed energy levels is still used today.
B. It correctly predicts the line spectra of all the elements.
C. It says an electron can have any amount of energy.
D. It describes electrons as clouds of probability called orbitals.

Bohr's model fit hydrogen but failed for atoms with more than one electron. Which of its ideas survived, and which choice describes the later orbital model instead?

Which of these can hold the most electrons?

C. the 3p subshell

One orbital holds 2 electrons; s, p, and d subshells have 1, 3, and 5 orbitals. A shell includes all of its subshells.

3.6 Periodic Trends

Problem 3.43

Rank Cl, Mg, Na, and K in order of increasing atomic radius, and explain the order.

Hint

Three are in period 3 and one is below sodium. Radius decreases left to right across a period and increases down a group.

Answer

Cl < Mg < Na < K. Cl, Mg, and Na are all in period 3, and radius decreases left to right across a period (more protons pulling on the same shell), so Cl < Mg < Na. K is directly below Na in group 1, and it has one more shell, so it is the largest.

(d) A sulfur atom ([Ne] 3s2 3p4) has 6 valence electrons, two short of an octet, so it gains 2.

Problem 3.54

Mateo's pulse oximeter (chapter opener) shines red light at 660 nm and infrared light at 940 nm through his fingertip. Section 3.1 found that a 660 nm photon carries 3.01 × 10−19 J and a 940 nm photon carries 2.11 × 10−19 J. Suppose that in one flash the red light source gives off 1.5 × 10−6 J of light energy. (a) How many photons is that? (b) If the infrared source gives off the same amount of energy in one flash, does it give off more photons or fewer? How many? (c) Which of the two wavelengths does oxyhemoglobin absorb more strongly?

Hint

(a) Number of photons = total energy ÷ energy of one photon. (b) Each infrared photon carries less energy. (c) See the pulse oximeter discussion in 3.1.

Answer

(a) Section 3.1 found the energy of one 660 nm photon, 3.01 × 10−19 J. Divide the total energy by the energy of one photon:

\( \dfrac{1.5 \times 10^{-6}\;\text{J}}{3.01 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J/photon}} = 5.0 \times 10^{12}\;\text{photons} \)

(Two significant figures, set by 1.5 × 10−6 J.)

(b) More. Each infrared photon carries less energy (2.11 × 10−19 J, Section 3.1), so it takes more of them to carry the same total:

\( \dfrac{1.5 \times 10^{-6}\;\text{J}}{2.11 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J/photon}} = 7.1 \times 10^{12}\;\text{photons} \)

(c) The 940 nm infrared light (Section 3.1). Deoxyhemoglobin absorbs more of the red; comparing the two lets the device estimate SpO2.

Problem 3.55

Five elements on a typical electrolyte panel are Na, K, Mg, Ca, and Cl. (a) Of these five atoms, which is largest and which is smallest? (b) Which atom has the lowest first ionization energy? (c) Which two of the ions they form are isoelectronic with neon? (d) Which element forms an anion, and why is it the only one?

Hint

(a) Place all five on a tiny table: bigger down and left. (b) Lowest ionization energy is toward the lower left. (c) Ion electrons = Z − charge. (d) Which is a nonmetal?

Answer

(a) Largest: K. Smallest: Cl. K and Ca are in period 4 (four shells), and K is farther left, so K is the largest. Na, Mg, and Cl are in period 3, and Cl is the farthest right, so it is the smallest.

(b) K. It is the lowest and farthest left of the five, so its valence electron is farthest from the nucleus and easiest to remove.

(c) Na+ and Mg2+, with 10 electrons each. (K+, Ca2+, and Cl− have 18, like argon.)

(d) Chlorine, forming Cl−. It is the only nonmetal of the five: with 7 valence electrons it gains one to reach an octet, while the four metals have 1 or 2 valence electrons and lose them.

Problem 3.56

Which particle has 12 protons, 12 neutrons, and 10 electrons?

A. \( ^{24}_{12}\text{Mg} \)
B. \( ^{23}_{11}\text{Na}^{+} \)
C. \( ^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}^{2+} \)
D. \( ^{22}_{10}\text{Ne} \)

Hint

Use protons = Z, neutrons = A − Z, and electrons = Z − charge for each choice. Which one gives all three counts?

Answer

C. Z = 12 gives 12 protons (magnesium); 24 − 12 = 12 neutrons; a 2+ charge means 12 − 2 = 10 electrons.

  • A is the neutral atom: 12 protons, 12 neutrons, but 12 electrons.
  • B has 10 electrons and 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons, but only 11 protons.
  • D has 10 electrons and 22 − 10 = 12 neutrons, but only 10 protons.

All four particles have 12 neutrons, and B, C, and D are isoelectronic (10 electrons), but only the proton count identifies the element.

Putting It Together: More Problems

These problems practice the skills added to Sections 3.1–3.6: amplitude, energy gaps, the toll-road picture of spectral lines, Bohr diagrams, excited and impossible configurations, the chromium and copper exceptions, Lewis symbols, and ion sizes.

Problem 3.57

A dental assistant turns a curing light to a higher power setting. The light gets brighter but stays the same blue. Which property of the light wave increased?

A. Amplitude
B. Wavelength
C. Frequency
D. Energy of each photon

Hint

Which wave property do we see as brightness? A change in wavelength or frequency would change the color.

Answer

A. Amplitude is the height of the wave, which we see as brightness (intensity): turning up the power is like turning up a dimmer switch.

  • B: a change in wavelength would change the color.
  • C: frequency is tied to wavelength by \( c = \lambda\nu \), so it also would change the color.
  • D: each photon's energy depends only on its frequency (\( E = h\nu \)); a brighter beam has more photons, not more energetic ones.

Problem 3.58

A sodium streetlamp shows a bright yellow line at 589 nm. This line comes from electrons dropping back to sodium's ground state. (a) What is the energy gap, in joules, between the two levels that produce this line? (b) If white light passed through cool sodium vapor, would you expect a dark line at 589 nm? Explain.

Hint

(a) The photon's energy equals the gap: convert 589 nm to meters and use ΔE = hc/λ. (b) Absorption and emission use the same gaps.

Answer

(a) The photon carries exactly the energy of the gap. Convert 589 nm to 5.89 × 10−7 m:

\( \Delta E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\;\text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1})}{5.89 \times 10^{-7}\;\text{m}} = 3.37 \times 10^{-19}\;\text{J} \)

(b) Yes. Cool sodium atoms sit in the ground state, and this line's gap starts at the ground state, so the atoms can absorb a 589 nm photon and jump up across the same gap. Sodium's absorption spectrum has a dark line at 589 nm. (Not every emission line shows up this way: a cool gas absorbs mainly the lines that start from the ground state.)

Problem 3.59

An electron in a hydrogen atom is excited to n = 4. On its way back to n = 1 it may drop in one step or in several. (a) List every possible single drop that could happen on the way down. (b) How many different spectral lines could these atoms produce? (c) Which drop gives the shortest-wavelength line?

Hint

(a) List every pair of levels from 4 down to 1. (b) Each gap gives one line (the toll-road picture). (c) The biggest drop gives the most energy.

Answer

(a) From 4: 4 → 3, 4 → 2, 4 → 1. From 3: 3 → 2, 3 → 1. From 2: 2 → 1.

(b) Six different lines, one for each gap. This is the toll-road picture: like paying a large toll with exact change, the electron can come down all at once or in smaller steps, and each step matches one gap exactly.

(c) 4 → 1, the biggest drop: the most energetic photon has the shortest wavelength.

Problem 3.60

Which electron configuration is impossible?

A. 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s1
B. 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s3
C. 1s2 2s2 2p5 3s1
D. 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p4

Hint

Check every subshell against its capacity: s holds 2, p holds 6. An excited state is possible; an overfilled subshell is not.

Answer

B. An s subshell holds at most 2 electrons, so 3s3 cannot exist. Work it out yourself before reading the choices, then check every subshell against its capacity.

  • A is the ground state of potassium (19 electrons).
  • C is an excited state of neon (10 electrons): one 2p electron has moved up to 3s. Unusual, but possible.
  • D is the ground state of sulfur (16 electrons).

Problem 3.61

Which configuration describes a magnesium atom in an excited state?

A. [Ne] 3s2
B. [Ne] 3s1 3p1
C. [Ne] 3s3
D. [Ne] 3s2 3p1

Hint

An excited state keeps magnesium's 12 electrons but moves one up. Check each choice's electron count and each subshell's capacity.

Answer

B. It still has 10 + 2 = 12 electrons (magnesium), but one 3s electron has moved up to 3p.

  • A is magnesium's ground state.
  • C is impossible (an s subshell holds only 2).
  • D has 13 electrons: that is the ground state of aluminum, not magnesium.

Problem 3.62

Give the number of electrons on each Bohr ring for (a) an oxygen atom, (b) the oxide ion O2−, (c) an aluminum atom, (d) the Al3+ ion, and (e) a calcium atom. Which of these have the same rings as neon?

Hint

Count electrons for each (a charge changes them), then fill the rings 2, 8, 8, then the rest. Compare each set of rings with neon's.

Answer

Fill 2, then 8, then 8, then the rest.

(a) O, 8 electrons: 2, 6. (b) O2−, 10 electrons: 2, 8. (c) Al, 13 electrons: 2, 8, 3. (d) Al3+, 10 electrons: 2, 8. (e) Ca, 20 electrons: 2, 8, 8, 2.

O2− and Al3+ have neon's rings (2, 8). One gained electrons to fill its outer ring; the other lost its whole outer ring.

Problem 3.63

Which description of the electron-dot (Lewis) symbol of the phosphide ion, P3−, is correct?

A. P with 5 dots and no brackets
B. P with 8 dots, in brackets, with a 3− charge outside
C. P in empty brackets with a 3− charge outside
D. P with 3 dots, in brackets, with a 3− charge outside

Hint

How many valence electrons does phosphorus have, and how many does the 3− ion gain? Ions are drawn in brackets with the charge outside.

Answer

B. Phosphorus (group 15) has 5 valence electrons; the 3− ion gained 3 more, for 8 dots (an octet). Ions are written in brackets with the charge outside.

  • A is the neutral phosphorus atom.
  • C is how a cation is drawn (its valence electrons are gone); an anion keeps and adds dots.
  • D shows only the 3 gained electrons and forgets the original 5.

Problem 3.64

Na+, Mg2+, Al3+, and F− all have 10 electrons. Which list puts them in order from smallest to largest?

A. Al3+ < Mg2+ < Na+ < F−
B. F− < Na+ < Mg2+ < Al3+
C. Na+ < Mg2+ < Al3+ < F−
D. They are all the same size, because they have the same number of electrons.

Hint

All four ions have the same 10 electrons. What is different about them, and how does that difference change how tightly the electrons are held?

Answer

A. The same 10 electrons are pulled by 13, 12, 11, and 9 protons. The more protons, the tighter the pull and the smaller the ion: a 3+ ion "hoards" its electrons most, and the anion, with more electrons than protons, spreads out most.

  • B is the exact reverse: it treats more positive charge as bigger.
  • C orders the cations backward; a larger positive charge makes a cation smaller, not larger.
  • D ignores the nucleus. Equal electron counts do not mean equal sizes when the proton counts differ.

Problem 3.65

Which atom has the lowest first ionization energy?

A. Cs
B. Ca
C. Se
D. F

Hint

Ionization energy is lowest toward the lower left. Look for the extreme: which choice is farthest down and to the left?

Answer

A. Look for the extreme: cesium is the farthest down and to the left (group 1, period 6). Its one valence electron is far from the nucleus, and giving it away reaches an octet, so it comes off easily.

  • B, calcium, is higher up (period 4) and one group farther right, so it is smaller and holds its electrons more tightly.
  • C, selenium, is a nonmetal on the right side; it tends to gain electrons, not lose them.
  • D, fluorine, is near the upper right corner, with one of the highest ionization energies of all.

Problem 3.66

Which is the correct ground-state electron configuration of chromium (Cr, Z = 24)?

A. [Ar] 4s2 3d4
B. [Ar] 4s1 3d5
C. [Ar] 3d6
D. [Ar] 4s2 4d4

Hint

Never end a configuration in s2d4. Which arrangement of the d subshell is extra stable for chromium?

Answer

B. Chromium is one of the two famous exceptions. The filling order would end in 4s2 3d4, but a half-filled d subshell is extra stable, so one 4s electron moves to 3d, giving every one of the six outer electrons its own orbital. Check: 18 + 1 + 5 = 24.

  • A follows the filling order but misses the exception. A configuration that ends in s2 d4 is the signal to move one s electron.
  • C moves both 4s electrons. Only one moves; 3d6 is neither half-filled nor filled, so it gains no extra stability.
  • D uses 4d in row 4. The d block is n − 1, so row 4 fills 3d; it also misses the exception.

Problem 3.67

Copper wire carries the current in most electrical wiring. (a) Write the full electron configuration of copper (Cu, Z = 29). (b) Draw the orbital diagram for its 4s and 3d subshells. (c) In one sentence, explain why copper does not follow the normal filling order. (d) How many unpaired electrons does a copper atom have?

Hint

(a) Fill to Z = 29, remembering copper's exception. (b) One arrow per box before pairing. (c) What arrangement of 3d is extra stable? (d) Count single arrows.

Answer

(a) 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s1 3d10 (or [Ar] 4s1 3d10). Check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 1 + 10 = 29.

(b) 4s [↑]   3d [↑↓][↑↓][↑↓][↑↓][↑↓]

(c) A completely filled d subshell is extra stable, and 4s and 3d are close in energy, so one 4s electron moves to fill 3d instead of leaving it at 3d9.

(d) 1, the lone 4s electron; every 3d orbital holds a pair. (The element below copper, silver, follows the same pattern: [Kr] 5s1 4d10.)
