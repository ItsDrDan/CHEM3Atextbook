Learning Objectives

  • Describe shells, subshells, and orbitals, and give the number of electrons each can hold.
  • Describe the shapes of s and p orbitals.
  • Use the Aufbau principle, the Pauli exclusion principle, and Hund's rule.
  • Draw orbital diagrams for atoms of the first 20 elements.

The quantum-mechanical model keeps Bohr's idea of numbered energy levels but describes each electron by the region of space where it is most likely to be found. To keep track of many electrons at once, it helps to picture the atom as a hotel. The shells are the floors, the subshells are the types of rooms on each floor, and the orbitals are the rooms themselves. Each room holds at most two guests.

Shells, Subshells, and Orbitals

  • A shell (principal energy level) is numbered n = 1, 2, 3, ... Higher n means the electrons are farther from the nucleus on average and higher in energy.
  • Each shell contains one or more subshells, labeled s, p, d, and f. Shell n has n kinds of subshells: shell 1 has only 1s; shell 2 has 2s and 2p; shell 3 has 3s, 3p, and 3d; shell 4 has 4s, 4p, 4d, and 4f.
  • Each subshell contains a set number of orbitals, and each orbital holds at most 2 electrons.

Adding up the subshells gives the capacity of each shell: shell 1 holds 2 electrons, shell 2 holds 2 + 6 = 8, shell 3 holds 2 + 6 + 10 = 18, and shell 4 holds 2 + 6 + 10 + 14 = 32. (In general, shell n holds 2n2 electrons.)

Orbital shapes

An s orbital is a sphere centered on the nucleus; a 2s orbital is a larger sphere than a 1s. A p orbital has two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus, like a dumbbell, and the three p orbitals in a subshell point along three perpendicular directions (x, y, and z). The d and f orbitals have more complicated shapes, which this course does not need.

Filling Orbitals: Three Rules

To find where an atom's electrons go in its ground state, place them one at a time using three rules.

  1. Aufbau principle ("building up" in German): electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals first. The order of subshell energies is not simply 1, 2, 3; for example, 4s fills before 3d. The diagonal method in the figure gives the order.
  2. Pauli exclusion principle: an orbital holds at most two electrons, and if it holds two they must have opposite spins, drawn as one up arrow and one down arrow (↑↓).
  3. Hund's rule: when filling orbitals of equal energy (such as the three 2p orbitals), put one electron in each orbital, all with the same spin, before pairing any of them. Like passengers on a bus, electrons take empty seats before sharing.

Orbital Diagrams

An orbital diagram shows each orbital as a box (or line) and each electron as an arrow. The figure shows nitrogen and oxygen.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Orbital diagram for carbon

Problem. Draw the orbital diagram for a carbon atom.

Solution. Carbon has Z = 6, so a neutral atom has 6 electrons. Fill in order: 1s gets 2, 2s gets 2, and the last 2 go into 2p. By Hund's rule they go into two different 2p orbitals with the same spin:

1s [↑↓]   2s [↑↓]   2p [↑] [↑] [  ]

Check. 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 electrons. Carbon has two unpaired electrons, which you will meet again when carbon forms four bonds in Chapter 6.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Orbital diagram for sulfur

Problem. Draw the orbital diagram for sulfur and count its unpaired electrons.

Solution. S has Z = 16. Filling order: 1s (2), 2s (2), 2p (6), 3s (2), and the last 4 electrons go into 3p. Three 3p electrons go in one per orbital; the fourth pairs up in the first orbital:

1s [↑↓]   2s [↑↓]   2p [↑↓][↑↓][↑↓]   3s [↑↓]   3p [↑↓][↑][↑]

Sulfur has 2 unpaired electrons.

Check. 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 4 = 16. Oxygen, in the same group, has the same pattern in its outer subshell (2p4).

Professor Tip

Before drawing arrows, write the number of electrons and the filling order on scratch paper, and cross off subshells as you fill them. When you reach a p subshell, put in all the "up" arrows first, one per box, and only then go back and add "down" arrows. Finally, count the arrows: they must equal the number of electrons.

Clinical Connection: Radiologic Technology

An X-ray tube works because atoms have shells. Inside the tube, electrons are accelerated by tens of thousands of volts and slam into a metal target, usually tungsten. Most of their energy becomes heat, but some collisions knock an electron out of a tungsten atom's innermost shell (n = 1, called the K shell in radiology). An electron from a higher shell immediately drops into the empty spot, and because the energy gap between these inner shells is very large, the photon it gives off is an X-ray. These characteristic X-rays have energies set by tungsten's shell structure, just as hydrogen's visible lines are set by its levels. The rest of the beam, a smooth spread of energies called bremsstrahlung ("braking radiation," from electrons slowing near nuclei), makes up most of a diagnostic X-ray beam.

Common Mistakes

  • Pairing p electrons too early. Hund's rule: one per orbital first, then pair.
  • Putting two arrows the same way in one box. Paired electrons must have opposite spins (Pauli).
  • Filling 3d before 4s. Use the diagonal method: 4s comes first.
  • Confusing orbitals with subshells. A p subshell is three orbitals and holds 6 electrons; one p orbital holds 2.

Summary

  • Shells (n = 1, 2, 3, ...) contain subshells (s, p, d, f), which contain orbitals (1, 3, 5, 7). Each orbital holds up to 2 electrons, so s, p, d, and f subshells hold 2, 6, 10, and 14.
  • Aufbau: fill lowest energy first (1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p ...). Pauli: at most 2 electrons per orbital, opposite spins. Hund: spread out in equal-energy orbitals before pairing.
  • Orbital diagrams show each orbital as a box and each electron as an arrow.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many electrons can the third shell (n = 3) hold, and how are they divided among its subshells?

Answer

18: 3s holds 2, 3p holds 6, and 3d holds 10.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Draw the orbital diagram for fluorine. How many unpaired electrons does it have?

Answer

F has 9 electrons: 1s [↑↓] 2s [↑↓] 2p [↑↓][↑↓][↑]. One unpaired electron.

A student draws phosphorus's 3p subshell as [↑↓][↑][  ]. Which rule is broken, and what is the correct drawing?
