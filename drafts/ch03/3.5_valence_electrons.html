Learning Objectives

  • Identify valence electrons and count them for main-group elements from the group number.
  • Explain why elements in the same group have similar chemical properties.
  • State the octet rule and use it to predict the charges of main-group ions.
  • Explain why noble gases are unreactive.

In Chapter 2 you learned that sodium forms Na+, magnesium forms Mg2+, and chlorine forms Cl−, and you used the group number to predict these charges. Now you can see why those charges appear. It comes down to the electrons in the outermost shell.

Valence Electrons

The valence electrons are the electrons in an atom's outermost (highest-numbered) shell. They are the electrons that atoms gain, lose, or share when they react, so they decide an element's chemistry. All the other electrons are core electrons, held tightly and not involved in reactions.

For a main-group element, the number of valence electrons follows from the group number:

  • Groups 1 and 2: valence electrons = group number (1 or 2).
  • Groups 13 to 18: valence electrons = group number minus 10 (3 to 8). Helium is the exception, with 2.

In the noble-gas configuration, the valence electrons are the s and p electrons of the highest shell. For bromine, [Ar] 4s2 3d10 4p5, the highest shell is n = 4, so the valence electrons are 4s2 4p5: 7. The 3d electrons belong to a lower shell and do not count.

Because every element in a group has the same number of valence electrons in the same kind of arrangement, elements in the same group have similar chemical properties. Lithium, sodium, and potassium all react with water in the same way; fluorine, chlorine, and bromine all form 1− ions. This is the reason the periodic table works.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Counting valence electrons

Problem. How many valence electrons do (a) calcium, (b) nitrogen, (c) iodine, and (d) argon have?

Solution. (a) Ca, group 2: 2. (b) N, group 15: 15 − 10 = 5. (c) I, group 17: 7. (d) Ar, group 18: 8.

Check. N is 1s2 2s2 2p3; the highest shell (n = 2) has 2 + 3 = 5 electrons ✓.

The Octet Rule and Ion Charges

The noble gases (group 18) barely react. Their valence shells are full: 8 electrons, an octet (ns2 np6), or 2 for helium. The octet rule says that main-group atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until they have eight valence electrons, the same configuration as a noble gas. (Hydrogen and lithium aim for two, like helium.)

  • Metals, with only 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons, lose them. Losing a few electrons is easier than gaining five, six, or seven. The ion is left with the full shell underneath, the configuration of the previous noble gas. Group 1 forms 1+ ions, group 2 forms 2+ ions, and aluminum forms 3+ ions.
  • Nonmetals in groups 15 to 17, with 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons, gain enough to reach 8, ending up with the configuration of the next noble gas. Group 15 forms 3−, group 16 forms 2−, and group 17 forms 1− ions.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Predicting an ion's charge

Problem. Use valence electrons and the octet rule to predict the ion formed by (a) potassium and (b) selenium, and name the noble gas each ion matches.

Solution. (a) K is in group 1 with 1 valence electron ([Ar] 4s1). Losing it leaves [Ar]: K+, isoelectronic with argon. (b) Se is in group 16 with 6 valence electrons ([Ar] 4s2 3d10 4p4). Gaining 2 gives 4s2 4p6 in the valence shell, the configuration of krypton: Se2−, called selenide.

Check. K+: 19 − 1 = 18 electrons (Ar has 18). Se2−: 34 + 2 = 36 electrons (Kr has 36). These match the group rules from Section 2.4.

Professor Tip

When you predict an ion, count to the nearest noble gas. Oxygen is 2 steps away from neon (gain 2) but 6 steps away from helium (lose 6), so it gains 2 and forms O2−. Magnesium is 2 steps past neon (lose 2), not 6 steps short of argon, so it forms Mg2+. Group 14 is halfway, which is one reason carbon usually shares electrons (Chapter 6) instead of forming ions.

A first look at electron-dot symbols

Chemists often show just the valence electrons as dots around the element symbol, called an electron-dot (Lewis) symbol. Sodium has one dot, chlorine has seven, and argon has eight. You will use these symbols to build molecules in Chapter 6.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The electrolytes on Mateo's basic metabolic panel in Chapter 2 (sodium 138, potassium 3.9, and chloride 102 mmol/L) are all ions with an octet, which is why each is reported as a single value with no charge to choose. Other common lab values follow the same pattern: calcium is measured as Ca2+ and magnesium as Mg2+. Nurses also see the octet rule's predictable charges in IV fluids and replacement medications, whose labels give the amount of each ion in milliequivalents (mEq), a unit that accounts for charge. One millimole of Ca2+ counts as 2 mEq because each ion carries a 2+ charge, while one millimole of K+ is 1 mEq. The charge comes straight from the number of valence electrons the atom gave up.

Common Mistakes

  • Counting d electrons as valence electrons for main-group elements. Only the s and p electrons of the highest shell count.
  • Using the group number directly for groups 13 to 18. Subtract 10: chlorine (group 17) has 7, not 17.
  • Getting the sign backward. Losing electrons makes a positive ion; gaining electrons makes a negative ion.
  • Thinking the ion "becomes" a noble gas. Na+ has neon's electron configuration but still has 11 protons; it is still sodium.

Summary

  • Valence electrons are in the outermost shell. For main-group elements: groups 1–2 have 1–2; groups 13–18 have 3–8 (group number minus 10).
  • Elements in a group share a valence configuration, so they have similar chemistry.
  • Octet rule: atoms tend to reach 8 valence electrons. Metals lose electrons (1+, 2+, 3+); nonmetals gain them (3−, 2−, 1−), reaching a noble-gas configuration.

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

How many valence electrons does a phosphorus atom have? What ion does it form?

Answer

5 (group 15, [Ne] 3s2 3p3). It gains 3 to reach an octet: P3−, phosphide.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Why do magnesium and calcium have similar chemical properties?

Answer

Both are in group 2 with two valence electrons in an s subshell (3s2 and 4s2), so both lose two electrons to form 2+ ions.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Which ion is isoelectronic with neon: K+, F−, or Cl−?

Answer

F−: 9 + 1 = 10 electrons, the same as Ne. K+ and Cl− each have 18 electrons, like argon.
