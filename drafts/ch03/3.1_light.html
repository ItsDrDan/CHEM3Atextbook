Learning Objectives

  • Name the three parts of a wave (wavelength, frequency, amplitude), and state how wavelength and frequency are related.
  • Use \( c = \lambda\nu \) to convert between wavelength and frequency.
  • Use \( E = h\nu \) to find the energy of a photon, and explain why shorter wavelengths carry more energy.
  • Put the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum in order of wavelength and energy.

Visible light, the infrared light in a pulse oximeter, the microwaves that heat your lunch, and the X-rays in a radiology department are all the same kind of thing: electromagnetic radiation, energy that travels as a wave of electric and magnetic fields. They differ only in the size of the wave. That size decides how much energy the light carries, and whether it warms a cup of coffee, lets you see, or damages DNA.

Wavelength and Frequency

Like a wave on water, a light wave has a repeating pattern of peaks.

  • The wavelength, symbol \( \lambda \) (Greek lambda), is the distance from one peak to the next. Visible light has wavelengths of about 400 to 700 nanometers (1 nm = 10−9 m).
  • The frequency, symbol \( \nu \) (Greek nu), is the number of peaks that pass a point each second. Its unit is the hertz (Hz), which means "per second": 1 Hz = 1 s−1.
  • The amplitude is the height of each peak (crest) above the middle of the wave. For light, amplitude is intensity: it is what we see as brightness. Turning a dimmer switch up makes the waves taller (brighter); turning it down makes them lower (dimmer). Amplitude does not change the color. Color is set by the wavelength.

All electromagnetic radiation travels through a vacuum at the same speed, the speed of light, \( c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1} \). Because the speed is fixed, wavelength and frequency are tied together:

\( c = \lambda \nu \)

You can prove the equation makes sense with dimensional analysis, the same unit check you used in Chapter 1. Wavelength is a distance (m) and frequency is "per second" (s−1), so

\( \lambda \times \nu = \text{m} \times \text{s}^{-1} = \dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \)

which is a speed, the units of \( c \).

Wavelength and frequency are inversely related: if one goes up, the other goes down. Picture yourself standing in the ocean. On a calm day the waves are long and gentle, the crests are far apart, and one reaches you only every so often: long wavelength, low frequency. In a storm the waves are short and choppy, the crests are close together, and they slam into you one after another: short wavelength, high frequency. All light moves at the same speed, so the closer together the crests are, the more of them pass you each second.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\): Frequency of a pulse oximeter's red light

Problem. A pulse oximeter uses red light with a wavelength of 660. nm. What is its frequency?

Solution. The decimal point in "660." shows that the final zero is significant (Chapter 1), so the wavelength has 3 significant figures. First convert nanometers to meters, so the units match the speed of light: \( 660.\ \text{nm} \times \dfrac{10^{-9}\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{nm}} = 6.60 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} \). Then solve \( c = \lambda\nu \) for \( \nu \):

\( \nu = \dfrac{c}{\lambda} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}}{6.60 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 4.55 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}\ (\text{Hz}) \)

Check. Meters cancel and leave s−1, a frequency. Visible light has frequencies around 1014 Hz, so the size is reasonable.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\): Wavelength of a microwave oven

Problem. A microwave oven runs at a frequency of 2.45 × 109 Hz. What is the wavelength, in centimeters?

Solution.

\( \lambda = \dfrac{c}{\nu} = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}}{2.45 \times 10^{9}\ \text{s}^{-1}} = 0.122\ \text{m} = 12.2\ \text{cm} \)

Check. A lower frequency than visible light should give a much longer wavelength, and 12 cm is about the width of your hand, much longer than 660 nm.

Professor Tip

The most common mistake with \( c = \lambda\nu \) is forgetting to change nanometers to meters. The speed of light is in meters per second, so the wavelength must be in meters. On the TI-36X Pro, enter 660 nm as 660E-9 using the ×10n key, and type the whole division on one line: 3E8 ÷ 660E-9.

Light as Photons: \( E = h\nu \)

Light also behaves like a stream of tiny packets of energy called photons. The energy of one photon depends only on its frequency:

\( E = h\nu \)

where \( h \) is Planck's constant, \( 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s} \). Higher frequency means higher energy. Because higher frequency also means shorter wavelength, shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon. If you know the wavelength, you can find the frequency first with \( c = \lambda\nu \), or combine the two equations into \( E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} \).

Example \(\PageIndex{3}\): Energy of a photon

Problem. What is the energy of one photon of the 660. nm red light from Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)?

Solution. Use the frequency found there:

\( E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(4.545 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 3.01 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \)

Check. Seconds cancel, leaving joules. One photon carries a tiny amount of energy, so a very small number makes sense. (Carrying an extra digit of the frequency and rounding once at the end avoids rounding error.)

Example \(\PageIndex{4}\): Red or infrared?

Problem. The same pulse oximeter also uses infrared light at 940. nm. Without a calculator, which photon has more energy: 660 nm or 940 nm? Then check with \( E = hc/\lambda \).

Solution. Shorter wavelength means more energy, so the 660 nm red photon has more energy. Checking the 940. nm photon:

\( E = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^{8})}{9.40 \times 10^{-7}} = 2.11 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \)

That is smaller than \( 3.01 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \).

Check. Energy and wavelength are inversely related, so the ratio of energies should be about 940/660 ≈ 1.4, and 3.01/2.11 ≈ 1.4.

Professor Tip: Three Formulas, Plug and Play

Write all three light equations in your notebook, side by side: \( c = \lambda\nu \), \( E = h\nu \), and \( E = hc/\lambda \). Every light problem in this course uses one of them. Find which two quantities you are given and which one you want, pick the equation that contains all three, and plug in, with the wavelength in meters.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The full range of electromagnetic radiation is the electromagnetic spectrum. The figure lists its regions from the longest, lowest-energy waves (radio) to the shortest, highest-energy ones (gamma rays). Visible light is a narrow band in the middle; within it, red has the longest wavelength and lowest energy, and violet has the shortest wavelength and highest energy. A memory aid for the visible colors from long to short wavelength is ROY G BV (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet).

You use most of these regions every day: radio waves carry the station in your car, microwaves heat your food, infrared is the warmth of a heat lamp, ultraviolet comes from the sun, X-rays make radiographs, and gamma rays are used to destroy cancer cells in radiation therapy. Astronauts need shielding in space partly because, above the atmosphere, high-energy radiation is no longer filtered out before it reaches them. The rule that ties it together: shorter wavelength = higher frequency = more energy, and the more energy a photon has, the more damage it can do to tissue.

The energy per photon explains why the regions act so differently on the body. Radio waves and microwaves can warm tissue but cannot break chemical bonds. Ultraviolet photons have enough energy to damage DNA, and X-ray and gamma-ray photons have enough to knock electrons out of atoms, which is why they are called ionizing radiation and why radiology staff wear dosimeters and lead aprons.

Clinical Connection: Respiratory Therapy

A pulse oximeter estimates arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2, the percent of hemoglobin carrying oxygen) without drawing blood. A light source on one side of the probe shines red light near 660 nm and infrared light near 940 nm through a fingertip or earlobe, and a detector on the other side measures what gets through. Oxyhemoglobin (hemoglobin carrying oxygen) absorbs more of the infrared light, and deoxyhemoglobin absorbs more of the red. The device uses the pulsing part of the signal, from arterial blood, and compares the two wavelengths to calculate SpO2. Respiratory therapists know the reading's limits: cold fingers, movement, nail polish, and carbon monoxide can throw it off, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has warned that pulse oximeters can be less accurate for people with darker skin pigmentation. A low or surprising reading is checked, often with an arterial blood gas test.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth-colored composite fillings are soft until they are "cured" with a handheld curing light. The light is blue, mostly about 450 to 470 nm, because the photoinitiator in the composite (commonly camphorquinone) absorbs blue photons; each absorbed photon has enough energy to start the reaction that hardens the filling. A 470 nm photon carries about \( 4.2 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \), roughly twice the energy of a 940 nm infrared photon. Bright blue light can also harm the retina, which is why the dental team uses an orange filter shield or orange-tinted glasses while curing.

Biology Connection: Microbiology and Cell Biology

Ultraviolet light from the sun has photons energetic enough to change DNA directly: UV can join two neighboring thymine bases into a thymine dimer, a kink that can cause copying errors if the cell does not repair it. Sunburn is the skin's response to this damage, and repeated damage raises the risk of skin cancer. Hospitals and labs use the same chemistry on purpose. Germicidal UV-C lamps (often near 254 nm) damage the DNA and RNA of bacteria and viruses on surfaces and in biosafety cabinets, which is why those lamps are never switched on while people are in the room.

Valley Connection: Seeing Orchards in Infrared

Healthy leaves absorb most red light for photosynthesis but strongly reflect near-infrared light, wavelengths just a little longer than red that our eyes cannot see. Stressed or thirsty leaves reflect less near-infrared. Satellites, airplanes, and drones over the San Joaquin Valley measure both, and a simple index called the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) compares them:

\( \text{NDVI} = \dfrac{\text{NIR} - \text{Red}}{\text{NIR} + \text{Red}} \)

Values near 1 mean dense, healthy canopy; values near 0 mean bare ground. University of California researchers have used Landsat satellite images of Central Valley almond and walnut orchards to map canopy vigor and help predict yield, and UC Davis's digital agriculture work uses near-infrared images to spot weak trees and uneven irrigation. For an irrigation technician like Mateo, a map built from two wavelengths of reflected light can show which blocks of trees are thirsty before the leaves look wilted, much as his pulse oximeter uses two wavelengths to show what his eyes cannot.

Sources: University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources, Advanced Sensing and Management Technology for Specialty Crops, "Update 2012: Predicting yield with Landsat" (ucanr.edu); UC Davis Digital Agriculture Laboratory, Virtual Orchard project.

Common Mistakes

  • Leaving the wavelength in nanometers. Convert to meters before using \( c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1} \).
  • Thinking longer wavelength means more energy. It is the reverse: long wavelength, low frequency, low energy.
  • Multiplying when you should divide. \( \nu = c/\lambda \) and \( \lambda = c/\nu \). A visible-light frequency should come out near 1014 Hz; if you get 102 or 10−15, recheck.
  • Mixing up "brighter" and "more energetic." Brightness is amplitude (intensity): a brighter light has taller waves and more photons, but each photon's energy depends only on its wavelength.

Summary

  • Every wave has a wavelength (distance between crests), a frequency (crests per second), and an amplitude (height; for light, brightness).
  • Electromagnetic radiation travels at \( c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1} \); \( c = \lambda\nu \), so wavelength and frequency are inversely related.
  • Each photon has energy \( E = h\nu = hc/\lambda \), with \( h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s} \).
  • From radio waves to gamma rays, wavelength decreases and frequency and energy increase. Visible light runs from red (about 700 nm) to violet (about 400 nm).

Exercises

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What is the frequency of green light with a wavelength of 532 nm?

Hint

Change nm to meters first (1 nm = 10−9 m), then solve c = λν for frequency: ν = c/λ.

Answer

Convert 532 nm to 5.32 × 10−7 m, then divide:

\( \nu = \dfrac{3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}}{5.32 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 5.64 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz} \)

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

A germicidal lamp gives off UV-C light at 254 nm. What is the energy of one photon?

Hint

Convert the wavelength to meters, then use E = hc/λ with h = 6.626 × 10−34 J s and c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1.

Answer

\( E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1})}{2.54 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}} = 7.83 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \)

That is more than twice the energy of a 660 nm red photon.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Put these in order of increasing photon energy: X-rays, infrared, radio waves, ultraviolet, visible light.

Hint

Shorter wavelength means higher photon energy. Order the regions by wavelength across the electromagnetic spectrum, then reverse the order.

Answer

Radio waves < infrared < visible light < ultraviolet < X-rays (the same order as decreasing wavelength).

Exercise \(\PageIndex{4}\)

Two red laser pointers have the same wavelength, 650 nm, but one is much brighter. Which wave property is different, and do their photons have different energies?

Hint

Which wave property shows up as brightness? Photon energy depends only on wavelength, so compare the two wavelengths.

Answer

The amplitude (intensity) is different: the brighter pointer has taller waves and sends out more photons each second. The wavelength is the same, so each photon has the same energy, and the color is the same red.
