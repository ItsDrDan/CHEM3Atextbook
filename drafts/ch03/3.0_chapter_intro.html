Patient Case: Mateo and the Clip on His Finger

When Mateo Herrera (Chapter 2) reaches the emergency department during the smoke week, a nurse clips a small device onto his fingertip before anyone listens to his lungs. A number appears: SpO2 91%. SpO2 is the oxygen saturation measured by a pulse oximeter, an estimate of how much of the hemoglobin in his arterial blood is carrying oxygen. A healthy adult usually reads about 95 to 100%, so the team pays attention.

The clip has no needle. It shines two colors of light through his finger, one red and one infrared that his eyes cannot see, and measures how much of each gets through. Hemoglobin carrying oxygen and hemoglobin without it absorb those two wavelengths differently, and the device turns that difference into a percentage. To understand how the clip works, you need to understand light, and to understand why atoms and molecules absorb and give off light, you need to know where their electrons are. That is this chapter.

Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Relate the wavelength, frequency, and energy of light, and use \( c = \lambda\nu \) and \( E = h\nu \) (Section 3.1).
  • Explain emission spectra with the Bohr model of the atom (Section 3.2).
  • Describe shells, subshells, and orbitals, and draw orbital diagrams using the Aufbau principle, the Pauli exclusion principle, and Hund's rule (Section 3.3).
  • Write full and noble-gas electron configurations for main-group atoms and ions (Section 3.4).
  • Count valence electrons and use the octet rule to explain the charges of main-group ions (Section 3.5).
  • Predict trends in atomic radius and ionization energy across the periodic table (Section 3.6).

Why This Chapter Matters

Pulse oximeters, X-ray images, the blue curing light in a dental office, ultraviolet lamps that disinfect hospital rooms, and the satellite images that tell a farmer which orchard rows need water all depend on how light and electrons interact. The arrangement of electrons also explains the periodic table you used in Chapter 2: why sodium always forms Na+, why chlorine forms Cl−, and why the elements in a column behave alike. Chapter 4 uses these ideas to build compounds, and Chapter 6 uses valence electrons to draw molecules.
